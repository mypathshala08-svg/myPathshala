GROUP BY, HAVING, ROLLUP and GROUPING SETS

2 Definition

GROUP BY, aggregates and grouping sets

Formal definition (write this in exams)

GROUP BY partitions the rows that pass WHERE into groups with equal values of the grouping expressions and returns one row per group; the select list may contain only grouping expressions and aggregate functions (COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX, collect_set, percentile_approx and others) computed over each group. HAVING filters groups after aggregation. GROUPING SETS, ROLLUP and CUBE compute several groupings in one query, with NULL in the columns that are aggregated away and the GROUPING__ID virtual column or grouping() function identifying each grouping.

In simple words

GROUP BY makes one result row per group and answers “how many, how much” for each. HAVING keeps only the groups you want. ROLLUP and CUBE add subtotal and total rows in the same query.

Grouping at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Sorting coins into piles

  • GROUP BY city puts all orders of the same city into one pile.
  • For each pile you can ask summary questions: how many (COUNT), total (SUM), average (AVG), smallest and largest (MIN, MAX).
  • HAVING throws away whole piles after counting (“only cities with more than 6,000 orders”). WHERE throws away single orders before piling.
  • ROLLUP adds a pile total and a grand total, like the last lines of a bill.

Intermediate The rules

  • Every SELECT column must be in GROUP BY or inside an aggregate (error 10025 otherwise).
  • COUNT(*) counts rows; COUNT(col) skips NULLs; COUNT(DISTINCT col) counts unique values; Hive also allows COUNT(DISTINCT a, b).
  • Hive extras: collect_set (unique values as an array), collect_list (all values), percentile_approx, variance, stddev_pop, corr.
  • GROUPING SETS ((a,b),(a),()) = several GROUP BYs in one query; WITH ROLLUP = hierarchy (a,b) → (a) → (); WITH CUBE = every combination.
  • In subtotal rows the rolled-up column is NULL; grouping(col) = 1 tells you that NULL means “all”.

Advanced What happens on the cluster

  • Map-side aggregation (hive.map.aggr=true, default): each map task keeps a hash table of partial results and sends one row per key. Our lab: 8 intermediate rows instead of 50,000.
  • The reducer merges partial results (mode: mergepartial in EXPLAIN). Averages travel as (sum, count) pairs, never as averages.
  • COUNT(DISTINCT) needs every distinct value to reach a reducer; on huge data rewrite it as a GROUP BY in a subquery, or use an approximate function.
  • Skew: one giant key (a city with 80% of orders) overloads one reducer; hive.groupby.skewindata=true adds an extra stage that spreads it first.
  • GROUP BY 1 (by position) works only with hive.groupby.position.alias=true; it is off by default (error 10025 in our lab).

Exam tip: “Difference between WHERE and HAVING.” WHERE filters individual rows before grouping and cannot use aggregates; HAVING filters groups after aggregation and can, e.g. HAVING COUNT(*) > 6000. Add an example of each in one query.

4 Step by step

Build the business review table

All outputs are real, from the Hive 4.2.1 lab on the curated orders table.

  1. Step 1 · Aggregates per city

    SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS orders, SUM(amount) AS revenue,
       ROUND(AVG(amount),2) AS avg_order, MIN(amount) AS min_amt, MAX(amount) AS max_amt
FROM orders WHERE status = 'DELIVERED'
GROUP BY city ORDER BY revenue DESC;
    +--------------+---------+-------------+------------+----------+----------+ | city | orders | revenue | avg_order | min_amt | max_amt | +--------------+---------+-------------+------------+----------+----------+ | Mumbai | 9249 | 2712610.00 | 293.29 | 40.00 | 880.00 | | Bengaluru | 8535 | 2499060.00 | 292.80 | 40.00 | 840.00 | | Pune | 7268 | 2148270.00 | 295.58 | 40.00 | 880.00 | | Hyderabad | 5493 | 1627080.00 | 296.21 | 40.00 | 840.00 | | Delhi | 5078 | 1501340.00 | 295.66 | 40.00 | 840.00 | | Thane | 4422 | 1293290.00 | 292.47 | 40.00 | 880.00 | | Navi Mumbai | 3655 | 1080250.00 | 295.55 | 40.00 | 840.00 | | Chennai | 2318 | 693590.00 | 299.22 | 40.00 | 840.00 | +--------------+---------+-------------+------------+----------+----------+
    WHERE removed cancelled and refunded orders first; then each city became one row. Mumbai leads with Rs 27.1 lakh from 9,249 delivered orders.

  2. Step 2 · Keep only big groups with HAVING

    SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS orders FROM orders
GROUP BY city HAVING COUNT(*) > 6000 ORDER BY orders DESC;
    +------------+---------+ | city | orders | +------------+---------+ | Mumbai | 10014 | | Bengaluru | 9298 | | Pune | 7930 | +------------+---------+
    HAVING runs after grouping and can use COUNT(*). Writing WHERE COUNT(*) > 6000 would fail: WHERE sees single rows, not groups.

  3. Step 3 · The classic error

    SELECT city, payment, COUNT(*) FROM orders GROUP BY city;
    Error: Error while compiling statement: FAILED: SemanticException [Error 10025]: Line 1:13 Expression not in GROUP BY key 'payment'
    payment is neither grouped nor aggregated, so Hive cannot know which value to print. Add it to GROUP BY, aggregate it (collect_set(payment)) or remove it.

  4. Step 4 · Counting in different ways

    SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_, COUNT(payment) AS non_null_payment,
       COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS customers,
       COUNT(DISTINCT city, payment) AS city_pay_pairs FROM orders;
SELECT city, collect_set(payment) AS pay_modes, size(collect_list(order_id)) AS n
FROM orders WHERE city IN ('Chennai','Thane') GROUP BY city;
    +--------+-------------------+------------+-----------------+ | rows_ | non_null_payment | customers | city_pay_pairs | +--------+-------------------+------------+-----------------+ | 50000 | 50000 | 2000 | 32 | +--------+-------------------+------------+-----------------+ +----------+--------------------------------+-------+ | city | pay_modes | n | +----------+--------------------------------+-------+ | Chennai | ["COD","UPI","Card","Wallet"] | 2523 | | Thane | ["Wallet","Card","UPI","COD"] | 4820 | +----------+--------------------------------+-------+
    All 2,000 customers ordered at least once; 8 cities × 4 modes = 32 pairs all occur. collect_set returns an array of unique values, in no guaranteed order (compare the two rows).

  5. Step 5 · Medians and spread

    SELECT city, percentile_approx(CAST(amount AS DOUBLE), 0.5) AS median_amt,
       percentile_approx(CAST(amount AS DOUBLE), 0.9) AS p90,
       ROUND(stddev_pop(amount),2) AS sd
FROM orders GROUP BY city ORDER BY city LIMIT 3;
    +------------+---------------------+---------------------+---------+ | city | median_amt | p90 | sd | +------------+---------------------+---------------------+---------+ | Bengaluru | 271.04046242774564 | 508.96470588235303 | 158.46 | | Chennai | 274.6875 | 521.6818181818184 | 163.13 | | Delhi | 275.30973451327435 | 517.7647058823529 | 161.41 | +------------+---------------------+---------------------+---------+
    Amounts are multiples of 10, yet the median is 271.04: percentile_approx builds a histogram and interpolates. It is fast and memory-light; round it for reports, or use exact percentile() on integer columns.

  6. Step 6 · Subtotals and a grand total with ROLLUP

    SELECT city, payment, COUNT(*) AS n, GROUPING__ID AS gid,
       grouping(city) AS g_city, grouping(payment) AS g_pay
FROM orders WHERE city IN ('Pune','Thane')
GROUP BY city, payment WITH ROLLUP
ORDER BY gid, city, payment;
    +--------+----------+--------+------+---------+--------+ | city | payment | n | gid | g_city | g_pay | +--------+----------+--------+------+---------+--------+ | Pune | COD | 783 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Pune | Card | 1567 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Pune | UPI | 4746 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Pune | Wallet | 834 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Thane | COD | 428 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Thane | Card | 972 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Thane | UPI | 2914 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Thane | Wallet | 506 | 0 | 0 | 0 | | Pune | NULL | 7930 | 1 | 0 | 1 | | Thane | NULL | 4820 | 1 | 0 | 1 | | NULL | NULL | 12750 | 3 | 1 | 1 | +--------+----------+--------+------+---------+--------+
    One query, three levels: 8 detail rows (gid 0), 2 city subtotals (gid 1, payment rolled up) and 1 grand total (gid 3). 7,930 + 4,820 = 12,750. grouping() marks which NULLs mean “all”.

  7. Step 7 · Every combination with CUBE

    SELECT payment, status, COUNT(*) AS n FROM orders WHERE city = 'Chennai'
GROUP BY payment, status WITH CUBE ORDER BY payment, status;
    +----------+------------+-------+ | payment | status | n | +----------+------------+-------+ | COD | CANCELLED | 15 | | COD | DELIVERED | 244 | | COD | REFUNDED | 3 | | COD | NULL | 262 | … (Card, UPI and Wallet rows) | Wallet | NULL | 267 | | NULL | CANCELLED | 161 | | NULL | DELIVERED | 2318 | | NULL | REFUNDED | 44 | | NULL | NULL | 2523 | +----------+------------+-------+
    CUBE adds subtotals in both directions: per payment (status NULL) and per status (payment NULL), plus the total 2,523. 20 rows from one scan, ready for a pivot table.
5 See it happen

From 50,000 rows to 8, and then to subtotals

Left: real Beeline output and Tez counters. Right: what the map tasks and the reducer do.

How 50,000 rows become 8 5 steps
What you see
  1. Kabir counts orders per city. On a real cluster several map tasks share the 50,000 rows.
  2. With map-side aggregation on (the default), only 8 intermediate rows leave the map stage.
  3. With it switched off, all 50,000 rows go to the shuffle.
  4. Either way the answer is the same: 8 cities.
  5. ROLLUP adds two city subtotals and a grand total of 12,750 to the 8 detail rows.
Behind the screen
  1. Each map task reads part of the table (here the file is small, so one task does it all). Per-task numbers below are illustrative.
  2. Each task keeps a hash table and emits one partial count per city, so very few rows are shuffled.
  3. Without partial aggregation every row is sent to the reducers.
  4. The reducer adds the partial counts for each city and produces one row per city.
  5. Hive computes the three groupings from one scan and marks rolled-up columns with grouping() and GROUPING__ID.

Real-time scenario 1

Replacing UNION ALL reports with GROUPING SETS

Many old reports stack several GROUP BY queries. One query does the same work in one scan.

Grouping sets 5 steps
Animated example
  1. An old report computes detail, subtotals and the total with three queries joined by UNION ALL.
  2. GROUPING SETS lists the three groupings in one query.
  3. The table is read once instead of three times.
  4. grouping() lets you show “ALL” instead of NULL in total rows.

Real-time scenario 2

One huge group slows the whole job

Skew is the most common GROUP BY performance problem. See when map-side aggregation saves you and when it does not.

Skewed groups 5 steps
Animated example
  1. One key holds a large share of rows, so the reducer that receives it runs much longer.
  2. For COUNT and SUM, map-side aggregation shrinks that key to a few partial rows.
  3. COUNT(DISTINCT) cannot be pre-summed, so the hot key still lands on one reducer.
  4. Use the skew setting, a two-level GROUP BY, approximate counts or special handling of the hot key.
  5. The two-level rewrite gives the same answer as COUNT(DISTINCT) with better parallelism.
Real-time scenario 3 · advanced

Approximate medians: why 271.04?

Percentiles on big data are usually approximate. Our lab result shows why the answer can be a value that never occurs.

Percentiles 5 steps
Animated example
  1. percentile_approx returned 271.04 for Bengaluru although every amount is a multiple of 10.
  2. It builds a compact histogram and interpolates between bins.
  3. Exact percentile() works on integer columns but holds all values of a group in memory.
  4. A larger accuracy argument gives more bins and a closer answer.
  5. Round approximate results and label them in reports.

7 Run the code

Run the code: grouping, partial aggregation and rollups

These programs do what Hive does for GROUP BY: partial counts per map task, a merge in the reducer, HAVING on groups, averages from (sum, count) pairs, and ROLLUP subtotals. Press Run, then try the challenges.

# GROUP BY city with map-side (partial) aggregation, in Python
splits = [["Pune", "Thane", "Pune", "Mumbai"], ["Pune", "Mumbai", "Mumbai"], ["Thane", "Pune"]]

partials = []
for split in splits:                    # each map task
    counts = {}
    for city in split:
        counts[city] = counts.get(city, 0) + 1
    partials.append(counts)
print("sent to shuffle:", partials)

final = {}
for p in partials:                      # the reducer merges
    for city, n in p.items():
        final[city] = final.get(city, 0) + n
print("result:", final)
sent to shuffle: [{'Pune': 2, 'Thane': 1, 'Mumbai': 1}, {'Pune': 1, 'Mumbai': 2}, {'Thane': 1, 'Pune': 1}] result: {'Pune': 4, 'Thane': 2, 'Mumbai': 3}
  • for split in splits: # each map taskEach map task builds its own small hash table: mode: hash in EXPLAIN.
  • for p in partials: # the reducer mergesThe reducer adds the partial counts: mode: mergepartial.

Interview and viva questions

What is the difference between GROUP BY and DISTINCT?

SELECT DISTINCT a, b returns the unique combinations of a and b; GROUP BY a, b returns the same rows but also lets you compute aggregates per group. Hive plans both as a shuffle by the keys. Use DISTINCT for unique lists, GROUP BY when you need counts or sums.

Why are there NULLs in my ROLLUP or CUBE output?

In subtotal and total rows the columns that were aggregated away are shown as NULL. If the data itself can contain NULLs in those columns, use grouping(col) (1 means rolled up) or GROUPING__ID to tell them apart, and replace them with labels like “ALL” in reports.

Is percentile_approx accurate enough?

For medians and percentiles of large numeric columns it is usually close, and it uses bounded memory. You can increase accuracy with a third argument (more histogram bins). For small groups or integer data where you need the exact value, percentile() is exact. Always label approximate numbers in reports.

Does GROUP BY sort the result?

No guarantee. Results may look sorted because of how reducers emit rows, but only ORDER BY guarantees order. Add ORDER BY when the order matters.

How do I speed up COUNT(DISTINCT) on huge tables?

Hive needs all distinct values to meet, which can bottleneck one reducer. Rewrite as SELECT COUNT(*) FROM (SELECT DISTINCT col FROM t) x, or a two-level GROUP BY when counting distinct per group; consider approximate distinct counting where small errors are acceptable; and keep statistics fresh so the optimiser picks good parallelism.

(7210, 25), (2300, 8)
Expected output
288.18
Show solution and explanation 
parts = [(7210, 25), (2300, 8)]
print(round(sum(s for s, _ in parts) / sum(c for _, c in parts), 2))
288.18

How it works: 9,510 ÷ 33 = 288.18. This is exactly how Hive merges partial AVG results.

P6Medium

Collect set per city

Build the set of payment modes per city (collect_set).

Input
(Pune, UPI), (Pune, COD), (Thane, Card), (Pune, UPI)
Expected output
{'Pune': ['COD', 'UPI'], 'Thane': ['Card']}
Show solution and explanation 
rows = [("Pune", "UPI"), ("Pune", "COD"), ("Thane", "Card"), ("Pune", "UPI")]
sets = {}
for city, pay in rows:
    sets.setdefault(city, set()).add(pay)
print({c: sorted(s) for c, s in sets.items()})
{'Pune': ['COD', 'UPI'], 'Thane': ['Card']}

How it works: Pune → [COD, UPI], Thane → [Card]. In Hive the array order is not guaranteed; sort_array() makes it stable.

P7Business problem

Refund abuse rule

Flag customers with 3 or more refunds in a month (GROUP BY customer HAVING COUNT(*) >= 3).

Input
refunds by customer id: 17, 42, 17, 99, 17, 42, 42, 8
Expected output
flag: [17, 42]
Show solution and explanation 
refunds = [17, 42, 17, 99, 17, 42, 42, 8]
counts = {}
for c in refunds:
    counts[c] = counts.get(c, 0) + 1
print("flag:", sorted(c for c, n in counts.items() if n >= 3))
flag: [17, 42]

How it works: Customers 17 and 42. Hive: SELECT customer_id FROM orders WHERE status = 'REFUNDED' GROUP BY customer_id HAVING COUNT(*) >= 3.

P8Business problem

Cost of three scans vs one

A report reads a 1.5 TB table three times with UNION ALL at Rs 0.4 per GB scanned. What does switching to GROUPING SETS save per run and per year (daily)?

Input
1.5 TB × 3 scans vs 1 scan
Expected output
per run: Rs 1,229 | per year: Rs 448,512
Show solution and explanation 
gb, rate = 1.5 * 1024, 0.4
saved = (3 - 1) * gb * rate
print(f"per run: Rs {saved:,.0f} | per year: Rs {saved * 365:,.0f}")
per run: Rs 1,229 | per year: Rs 448,512

How it works: About Rs 1,229 per run and Rs 4.5 lakh a year, for rewriting one query.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Aggregation questions appear in every data interview. Expect HAVING vs WHERE, ROLLUP and a performance follow-up.

Any company · SQLFresher

“WHERE or HAVING?”

The interviewer asks for cities with more than 6,000 orders, counting only delivered orders.

The interviewer is checking: do you place each filter correctly?

Show a strong answer
SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS orders
FROM orders
WHERE status = 'DELIVERED'          -- row filter, before grouping
GROUP BY city
HAVING COUNT(*) > 6000;             -- group filter, after grouping

Avoid: putting COUNT(*) in WHERE.

Retail · reportingMid-level

Subtotals without UNION ALL

A manager wants state, city and grand totals in one result set.

The interviewer is checking: do you know ROLLUP/GROUPING SETS?

Show a strong answer
  • GROUP BY state, city WITH ROLLUP gives (state, city), (state) and () in one scan.
  • Use grouping(city) to label subtotal rows and avoid confusing NULLs.
  • For custom combinations use GROUPING SETS; for every combination CUBE.

Avoid: three separate queries stitched together.

Data engineering · performanceMid-level

GROUP BY is slow on one reducer

A daily job groups 3 billion events by app_id; one reducer runs for an hour while others finish in minutes.

The interviewer is checking: can you diagnose skew and use Hive’s tools?

Show a strong answer
  1. Find the hot key: SELECT app_id, COUNT(*) … ORDER BY 2 DESC LIMIT 10.
  2. Make sure hive.map.aggr is on (partial aggregation absorbs most skew for COUNT/SUM).
  3. For DISTINCT-style aggregates, enable hive.groupby.skewindata or use a two-level GROUP BY; process the hot key separately if needed.

Avoid: “increase the number of reducers”: the hot key still lands on one.

Analytics · statisticsFresher

Median vs average

Product asks for the “typical” order value per city. Averages look inflated by a few large orders.

The interviewer is checking: do you know robust statistics in Hive?

Show a strong answer
  • Report the median with percentile_approx(amount, 0.5) alongside the average.
  • Explain that the approximation interpolates (our lab gave 271.04) and round for presentation.
  • Add p90 to show the tail.

Avoid: reporting only AVG for skewed money data.
