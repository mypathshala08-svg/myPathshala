The life of a YARN application

Every job on a Hadoop cluster, MapReduce or Spark, goes through the same lifecycle in YARN: it is submitted, gets an ApplicationMaster, asks for containers, runs and reports back. This lesson follows one real application through every state the ResourceManager and NodeManager log, then kills one and breaks one, so you can read any stuck or failed job.

2 Definition

The YARN application lifecycle

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A YARN application is a unit of work submitted to the ResourceManager, such as a MapReduce job or a Spark program. Its lifecycle begins when a client obtains a new application ID and submits an ApplicationSubmissionContext containing the queue, the resources of the ApplicationMaster and a ContainerLaunchContext (command, environment and local resources such as the job jar staged in HDFS). The ResourceManager saves the application to its state store (NEW → NEW_SAVING → SUBMITTED), the scheduler admits it to a queue (ACCEPTED) and allocates a container for the ApplicationMaster; a NodeManager localizes the resources and launches it. After the ApplicationMaster registers, the application is RUNNING: the ApplicationMaster requests further containers through periodic allocate heartbeats, launches its tasks on NodeManagers, monitors them, and finally unregisters with a final status. Each run of an ApplicationMaster is an application attempt; if one fails, the ResourceManager can start a new attempt, up to yarn.resourcemanager.am.max-attempts (2 by default). The application ends in FINISHED, FAILED or KILLED, and the ResourceManager records its aggregate resource usage in MB-seconds and vcore-seconds.

In simple words

Your job first gets a manager (the ApplicationMaster) in one container. The manager introduces itself to YARN, asks for workers (more containers), runs the work, reports the result and leaves. YARN writes down every step and how much memory was used for how long.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

ApplicationOne submission to YARN, e.g. application_1792643020992_0136.
Application attemptOne run of its ApplicationMaster: appattempt_…_0136_000001.
ApplicationMaster (AM)The per-application manager, running in container …_01_000001.

Allocate heartbeatThe AM's regular call to the RM: “here are my requests, what did I get?”

The lifecycle at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner A wedding caterer

  • You (the client) call the catering company's office (the ResourceManager) and book an event. The office writes it in the register first, so it isn't lost if the phone line drops.
  • The office sends one event manager (the ApplicationMaster) to the venue. Nothing is cooked until the manager arrives and reports in.
  • The manager asks the office for cooks and waiters (containers) as needed, tells them what to do, and lets them go when their part is done.
  • At the end the manager reports “done” and the office sends you the bill: people × hours, just like MB-seconds.

Intermediate Who talks to whom

  • Client → RM (ApplicationClientProtocol): getNewApplication, then submitApplication. Before submitting, the MapReduce client uploads the jar, job.xml and split list to .staging in HDFS (Lesson 8.6).
  • RM → NM: launches the AM container. The NM localizes the staged files (LOCALIZING), then starts the JVM (RUNNING).
  • AM → RM (ApplicationMasterProtocol): registerApplicationMaster, then allocate about every second with container requests that name preferred hosts and racks (data locality), and finally finishApplicationMaster.
  • AM → NM (ContainerManagementProtocol): startContainers for each task. Map and reduce tasks then report progress to the AM over the “umbilical” protocol, not to the RM.

Advanced Attempts, recovery and the state store

  • An application can have several attempts. If the AM container dies, the RM starts attempt _000002 elsewhere, up to yarn.resourcemanager.am.max-attempts (default 2; MapReduce also reads mapreduce.am.max-attempts). The MapReduce AM can recover finished tasks from the job history of the previous attempt.
  • The _SAVING states (NEW_SAVING, ALLOCATED_SAVING, FINAL_SAVING) are writes to the RM state store, so a restarted or failed-over RM (RM HA with ZooKeeper) still knows every application.
  • ACCEPTED can last a long time on a busy cluster: the queue or its AM limit (maximum-am-resource-percent) may not allow another ApplicationMaster yet (Lesson 11.3).
  • The RM's state (FINISHED) and the app's final status (SUCCEEDED or FAILED) are different things: a MapReduce job whose tasks failed still ends as FINISHED with final status FAILED.

Exam tip: For “Explain the lifecycle of a YARN application”, draw client, RM, NM with the AM and NMs with containers, and number the steps: new ID, submit, AM container allocated, AM launched, AM registers, allocate requests, containers launched, AM unregisters. Name the states NEW, SUBMITTED, ACCEPTED, RUNNING, FINISHED/FAILED/KILLED.

3 Types and ways to use it

Animated example: results day at a university board

The same states, on a different job: a board computes 12 lakh students' results overnight. Times are from our lab run (06:25:43 → 06:26:25); only the story around them is invented.

Application and attempt states 6 steps
Animated example
  1. Submit: the application goes NEW → NEW_SAVING → SUBMITTED → ACCEPTED in the same second; the state store write comes first.
  2. AM container: the first attempt is scheduled and a container is allocated and launched on a NodeManager.
  3. Register: five seconds later the ApplicationMaster JVM is up and registers; attempt and application both become RUNNING.
  4. Work: for 30 seconds the RM log is quiet about this app. The AM asks for containers and watches its tasks itself.
  5. Unregister: the AM reports success; the RM saves the final status (FINAL_SAVING) and waits for the AM to exit (FINISHING).
  6. Done: the AM container exits with success and the application is FINISHED. Board results are ready long before morning.

yarn application -list -appStates ALL shows every application the RM still remembers, with its state and final status. yarn applicationattempt -list <appId> and yarn container -list <attemptId> zoom in one level at a time.

4 Step by step

Following one application on the lab cluster

We use the Module 8 lab (HDFS + YARN, three workers) and the 140 MB orders_x30.csv. app_states.py polls the RM REST API every 0.2 s; docker logs shows the RM and NM logs. Both are in “Run it on your PC”.

  1. Steps 1–3: the client asks for an ID, stages files, submits

    hadoop jar mr.jar ItemRevenue /user/hadoop/data/orders_x30.csv /out/lc1 &
python3 app_states.py application_1792643020992_0136
    submitted application_1792643020992_0136 + 0.1 s state=ACCEPTED final=UNDEFINED containers=1 allocatedMB=512
    By the time our first poll arrived, the application had already passed NEW, NEW_SAVING and SUBMITTED. ACCEPTED with 1 container and 512 MB means the scheduler has already reserved the ApplicationMaster's container.

  2. Step 4: the ResourceManager records and schedules

    docker logs lab8-resourcemanager-1 | grep application_1792643020992_0136
    06:25:43 RMAppImpl - application_…_0136 State change from NEW to NEW_SAVING on event = START 06:25:43 RMAppImpl - application_…_0136 State change from NEW_SAVING to SUBMITTED on event = APP_NEW_SAVED 06:25:43 RMAppImpl - application_…_0136 State change from SUBMITTED to ACCEPTED on event = APP_ACCEPTED 06:25:43 RMAppAttemptImpl - appattempt_…_0136_000001 State change from SCHEDULED to ALLOCATED_SAVING on event = CONTAINER_ALLOCATED 06:25:43 RMAppAttemptImpl - appattempt_…_0136_000001 State change from ALLOCATED to LAUNCHED on event = LAUNCHED
    Every “_SAVING” state is a write to the RM's state store, so a restarted RM can recover. The scheduler found room for the AM in the same second.

  3. Step 5: the NodeManager localizes and launches the AM

    docker logs lab8-worker2-1 | grep container_1792643020992_0136_01_000001
    06:25:43 ContainerImpl - Container container_…_0136_01_000001 transitioned from NEW to LOCALIZING 06:25:43 ContainerImpl - Container container_…_0136_01_000001 transitioned from LOCALIZING to SCHEDULED 06:25:43 ContainerImpl - Container container_…_0136_01_000001 transitioned from SCHEDULED to RUNNING
    LOCALIZING copies the job jar and job.xml from the HDFS staging folder to the worker's disk. Container _000001 of attempt _01 is always the ApplicationMaster.

  4. Step 6: the ApplicationMaster registers

    06:25:48 appattempt_…_000001 State change from LAUNCHED to RUNNING on event = REGISTERED
06:25:48 application_…_0136 State change from ACCEPTED to RUNNING on event = ATTEMPT_REGISTERED
+  5.0 s  state=RUNNING    final=UNDEFINED containers=1  allocatedMB=512
    The five seconds between LAUNCHED and REGISTERED are the MRAppMaster JVM starting and reading the job configuration. Only a registered AM may send allocate requests.

  5. Steps 7–8: the AM gets containers and runs tasks

    +  6.0 s  state=RUNNING    final=UNDEFINED containers=2  allocatedMB=1024
+ 27.1 s  state=RUNNING    final=UNDEFINED containers=1  allocatedMB=512
+ 28.1 s  state=RUNNING    final=UNDEFINED containers=2  allocatedMB=1024
    One second after registering, the AM held a second container: the single map task (one 140 MB split, Lesson 8.2). When the map finished at +27 s its container was released, and a reduce container arrived at +28 s. The RM logged no application state changes in this phase: tasks are the AM's business.

  6. Step 9: unregister, clean up, send the bill

    yarn application -status application_1792643020992_0136
    + 35.6 s state=FINISHED final=SUCCEEDED containers=1 allocatedMB=512 Application-Type : MAPREDUCE Queue : root.default State : FINISHED Final-State : SUCCEEDED AM Host : worker2 Aggregate Resource Allocation : 33224 MB-seconds, 64 vcore-seconds
    The AM unregistered at 06:26:19 (FINAL_SAVING, FINISHING); its container exited at 06:26:25 (EXITED_WITH_SUCCESS, DONE) and the app became FINISHED. 33,224 MB-seconds over about 36 s means roughly 920 MB held on average: the AM all the time, plus one task container most of the time.

Two ways it goes differently

# kill the second run 15 s after submitting it
yarn application -kill application_1792643020992_0137
# ask for a 3 GB ApplicationMaster on 2 GB NodeManagers
hadoop jar mr.jar ItemRevenue -D yarn.app.mapreduce.am.resource.mb=3072 …
+ 16.9 s state=KILLED final=KILLED containers=0 allocatedMB=0 diagnostics: Application application_1792643020992_0137 was killed by user hadoop at 172.22.0.2 Job job_1792643020992_0137 failed with state KILLED due to: Application … was killed by user hadoop at 172.22.0.2 InvalidResourceRequestException: Invalid resource request! Cannot allocate containers as requested resource is greater than maximum allowed allocation.

A kill releases every container at once and records who killed it and from where. An impossible request is rejected at submission, so no application ever reaches ACCEPTED.

An application stuck in ACCEPTED is not “almost running”. It means no container has been found for its ApplicationMaster yet: the queue is full, the queue's AM share (maximum-am-resource-percent) is used up, or no node is big enough. Check the queue in the RM UI before resubmitting, or you will just add another waiting application.

5 See it happen

From Enter to FINISHED

Six steps of our 36-second run: what the polling script printed, and what the daemons were doing.

One YARN application 6 steps
What you see
  1. The client submits the job and prints the application ID.
  2. 0.1 s later the application is ACCEPTED and holds one 512 MB container.
  3. At 5 s it becomes RUNNING, still with only that one container.
  4. At 6 s it holds two containers: 1,024 MB.
  5. At 27 s one container is released, at 28 s a new one appears.
  6. At 35.6 s it is FINISHED and SUCCEEDED; YARN reports the usage.
Behind the screen
  1. The client gets an application ID, uploads the jar and configuration to HDFS and submits.
  2. The RM saves the application, accepts it into the queue and places the AM container on worker2, which starts localizing.
  3. The ApplicationMaster JVM starts and registers with the RM.
  4. On its next allocate heartbeat the AM receives a container on worker1 and starts the map task there.
  5. The map container is released; a reduce container is started on another worker.
  6. The AM unregisters with SUCCEEDED and exits; the NodeManagers aggregate the logs into HDFS.
6 Real-world use

The lifecycle at work

1. Chargeback in a shared bank cluster

A bank's platform team reads every finished application's MB-seconds and vcore-seconds from the RM and bills each department monthly for its share of the cluster.

RM apps API→MB-s per queue→Monthly bill

2. Alerting on stuck jobs at a telecom operator

The nightly billing pipeline alerts if any application stays ACCEPTED for more than 10 minutes, a sign that its queue is full or an AM can't be placed.

Poll app states→ACCEPTED too long→Page on-call

3. Surviving a lost node during a long ETL

An e-commerce company's 3-hour ETL lost the node running its ApplicationMaster. YARN started attempt 2, and the MapReduce AM recovered the completed tasks instead of starting from zero.

AM node dies→attempt 2 + recovery→Job continues

4. Spark and other engines on the same YARN

A Spark job in cluster mode follows the same lifecycle: its driver runs inside the ApplicationMaster container and its executors are ordinary YARN containers. Hive on Tez and Flink do the same.

spark-submit→AM + executors→Same states

5. Queue-time dashboards for a research cluster

A university HPC/Hadoop team plots “time from submit to RUNNING” per queue each day. Rising queue time tells them when students' assignment deadlines need a bigger share.

startedTime, launchTime→wait per queue→Capacity decisions

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.

Poll the ResourceManager

# app_states.py: submit-and-watch. Polls the ResourceManager REST API every 0.2 s and prints every
# change of application state, plus how many containers the app holds. Usage: python3 app_states.py <app_id>
import json, sys, time, urllib.request
RM = "http://resourcemanager:8088/ws/v1/cluster/apps/"
app, t0, last = sys.argv[1], time.time(), None
while True:
    try:
        a = json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(RM + app))["app"]
    except Exception:
        time.sleep(0.2); continue
    now = (a["state"], a.get("finalStatus"), a.get("runningContainers", 0), a.get("allocatedMB", 0))
    if now != last:
        print(f"+{time.time() - t0:5.1f} s  state={now[0]:10} final={now[1]:9} containers={max(now[2], 0)}  allocatedMB={max(now[3], 0)}")
        last = now
    if a["state"] in ("FINISHED", "FAILED", "KILLED"):
        print("diagnostics:", (a.get("diagnostics") or "-")[:150])
        break
    time.sleep(0.2)

9 Quiz and revision

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 11.2 in one screen

  • Client: getNewApplication → stage jar/conf in HDFS → submitApplication (with the AM's ContainerLaunchContext).
  • App states: NEW → NEW_SAVING → SUBMITTED → ACCEPTED → RUNNING → FINAL_SAVING → FINISHING → FINISHED (or FAILED, KILLED).
  • Attempt states: … SCHEDULED → ALLOCATED → LAUNCHED → RUNNING (on REGISTERED) …; container: NEW → LOCALIZING → SCHEDULED → RUNNING → EXITED_WITH_SUCCESS → DONE.
  • Lab: ACCEPTED +0.1 s, RUNNING +5.0 s (AM JVM start), FINISHED +35.6 s; 33,224 MB-seconds, 64 vcore-seconds.
  • The AM registers, sends allocate heartbeats with locality preferences, and launches tasks with startContainers; tasks report to the AM.
  • An application can have several attempts (yarn.resourcemanager.am.max-attempts, default 2).
  • Kill: yarn application -kill frees all containers at once; too-big requests fail with InvalidResourceRequestException.

Interview and viva questions

What are the steps when a YARN application is submitted?

The client asks the ResourceManager for a new application ID, uploads the application's files (for MapReduce the jar, job.xml and splits) to a staging directory in HDFS, and submits an ApplicationSubmissionContext. The ResourceManager saves it, the scheduler admits it to a queue and allocates a container for the ApplicationMaster, and a NodeManager localizes the files and launches it. The ApplicationMaster registers, requests further containers through allocate calls, launches its tasks on NodeManagers, and unregisters with a final status when the work is done.

What is the difference between an application and an application attempt in YARN?

An application is the whole submission. An attempt is one run of its ApplicationMaster. If the ApplicationMaster fails, the ResourceManager can start a new attempt with a new AM container, up to yarn.resourcemanager.am.max-attempts (2 by default). Attempt IDs look like appattempt_<cluster timestamp>_<app number>_000001.

Why is my YARN application stuck in ACCEPTED?

ACCEPTED means the application is queued but its ApplicationMaster container has not been started. Common causes: the queue is at capacity, the queue's limit for ApplicationMasters (maximum-am-resource-percent) is reached, no NodeManager has enough free memory, or NodeManagers are unhealthy or lost. Check the queue and node pages in the ResourceManager UI and the application's diagnostics.

What is the difference between state and final status of a YARN application?

State is the ResourceManager's lifecycle state (for example RUNNING or FINISHED). Final status is the outcome reported by the application: SUCCEEDED, FAILED, KILLED or UNDEFINED while running. An application can be in state FINISHED with final status FAILED, for example a MapReduce job whose tasks failed.

What are MB-seconds in YARN?

The aggregate resource allocation of an application: for every container, its memory in MB multiplied by the seconds it was allocated, summed. vcore-seconds are the same for virtual cores. They appear in yarn application -status and the REST API and are used for chargeback and capacity planning.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

The YARN application lifecycle is asked in Hadoop, Spark and platform-engineering interviews.

YARN · basicsFresher

“What happens when you run hadoop jar?”

The classic “walk me through it” question.

The interviewer is checking: the roles of client, RM, AM and NM.

Show a strong answer
  • The client gets an application ID, uploads the jar, configuration and splits to HDFS staging, and submits to the ResourceManager.
  • The RM allocates a container for the MRAppMaster; a NodeManager localizes the files and launches it; the AM registers.
  • The AM requests containers (preferring nodes with the data), launches map and reduce tasks via NodeManagers, tracks them, then unregisters; logs are aggregated.
Troubleshooting · queuesExperienced

Jobs sit in ACCEPTED for 30 minutes every morning

The cluster dashboard shows 40% free memory at the same time.

The interviewer is checking: AM limits and queue limits, not just total capacity.

Show a strong answer
  • Free memory may belong to other queues; the submitting queue can be at its capacity or user limit.
  • The queue's maximum-am-resource-percent may be reached by many small jobs, so no new AM can start.
  • Check queue usage in the RM scheduler page, raise the AM share or queue capacity, or batch small jobs.
Reliability · attemptsExperienced

“The node running the ApplicationMaster died. Is the job lost?”

A follow-up on fault tolerance.

The interviewer is checking: application attempts and recovery.

Show a strong answer
  • The RM notices the AM container is gone and starts a new attempt on another node, up to yarn.resourcemanager.am.max-attempts (default 2).
  • The MapReduce AM can recover completed tasks from the previous attempt's history; other frameworks may restart the work.
  • With RM HA and the state store, even an RM failover keeps the application.
Platform · costExperienced

Design chargeback for a shared Hadoop cluster

Five departments share one cluster and finance wants a monthly split.

The interviewer is checking: knowing where YARN usage data comes from.

Show a strong answer
  • Each finished application has memorySeconds and vcoreSeconds in the RM REST API (and the timeline/history servers).
  • Collect them per queue or user daily, convert to GB-hours and vcore-hours, and price both.
  • Reserve capacity costs separately; idle reserved capacity is a policy choice, not just usage.
