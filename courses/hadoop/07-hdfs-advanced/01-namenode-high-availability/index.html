NameNode high availability (QJM and ZooKeeper)

One NameNode means one machine whose failure stops the whole file system. HA runs a standby NameNode that follows every change through a quorum of JournalNodes, and ZooKeeper promotes it automatically. This lesson builds a real HA cluster in Docker, breaks it three different ways, and shows from the logs how Hadoop prevents two NameNodes from ever being active at once.

1 The story

The day the NameNode went down

Exam-result week at the university. The results portal reads marks files from HDFS. At 10:40 the NameNode server's motherboard dies. Every DataNode is healthy, every block is safe on disk, and yet nobody can open a single file: the only machine that knows which blocks make which file is gone. Restarting on new hardware from the last checkpoint takes the admin team almost an hour. Riya, watching the angry messages, asks the obvious question: why was there only one?

Arjun Sir

In Hadoop 1 the NameNode was a single point of failure. Since Hadoop 2 you can run two (or more) NameNodes: one active, the others standby. The standby replays every change from a shared edit log kept on JournalNodes, and receives block reports from all DataNodes, so it is ready to take over in seconds.

Riya

Who decides when to switch?

Arjun Sir

ZooKeeper and a small process next to each NameNode, the ZKFailoverController. And there's a harder question: what if the old active isn't dead, just frozen, and wakes up later? Two active NameNodes writing different histories would corrupt the file system. Today we build a real HA cluster with 2 NameNodes, 3 JournalNodes and ZooKeeper, crash it, freeze it, and starve it, and watch exactly what Hadoop does.

2 Definition

NameNode high availability

Formal definition (write this in exams)

HDFS high availability (HA) runs two or more NameNodes for one namespace (a nameservice): exactly one is active and serves clients; the others are standby. With the Quorum Journal Manager (QJM), the active NameNode writes every namespace edit to a group of JournalNodes (typically 3 or 5) and an edit succeeds only when a majority acknowledges it; standbys tail these edits to keep their namespace current, while DataNodes send heartbeats and block reports to all NameNodes. Automatic failover uses a ZooKeeper ensemble and a ZKFailoverController (ZKFC) beside each NameNode: the ZKFC monitors its NameNode's health and holds an ephemeral lock znode while it is active; when the active's ZooKeeper session expires, another ZKFC takes the lock, fences the old active, and transitions its NameNode to active. JournalNodes additionally enforce a monotonically increasing epoch, rejecting writes from any NameNode with an older epoch, which prevents split-brain.

In simple words

Two NameNodes, one in charge. The one in charge writes every change to three JournalNodes; the spare reads those changes and stays up to date. ZooKeeper notices when the one in charge disappears and promotes the spare in about ten seconds. A NameNode that was replaced and comes back is refused by the JournalNodes, so there is never more than one boss.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

NameserviceA logical name for the HA pair, e.g. hdfs://canteen; clients never name a single NameNode.
Active / standbyThe NameNode serving clients / the warm spare replaying edits.
JournalNode (JN)A small daemon storing the shared edit log; run 3 or 5.
QJMQuorum Journal Manager: writes succeed when a majority of JNs accept.
ZKFCZKFailoverController: health-checks its NameNode and runs the election in ZooKeeper.
FencingMaking sure the old active can no longer act (e.g. ssh and kill it).
EpochA number that grows with each new active writer; JNs reject older epochs.
Split-brainTwo nodes both believing they are active: the disaster HA must prevent.

HA at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner A cricket team captain

  • The captain (active NameNode) makes every decision; the vice-captain (standby) watches every ball and knows the match situation.
  • The scorebook is kept by three scorers (JournalNodes). A decision counts only if at least two of them write it down.
  • If the captain is injured, the umpire (ZooKeeper) hands the armband to the vice-captain. If the old captain walks back on, the scorers refuse to record his decisions: the new captain has a newer armband number.

Intermediate How a failover happens

  • The active's ZKFC holds an ephemeral znode (ActiveStandbyElectorLock) in ZooKeeper. It disappears when its session expires (10 s without contact).
  • The standby's ZKFC grabs the lock, fences the old active, and tells its NameNode to become active. Our log: “Checking for any old active” → “Fencing successful” → “Successfully transitioned … to active state” within one second.
  • Clients use a failover proxy provider: they know both addresses and retry the other on errors such as StandbyException.
  • Manual, graceful switch: hdfs haadmin -failover nn2 nn1 (2.2 s in our lab).

Advanced Why split-brain can't happen with QJM

  • Each new active asks the JournalNodes for a new epoch greater than any they have seen; a majority must promise it. Our epochs went 2 → 3 → 4 → … with every switch.
  • A frozen old active wakes up with epoch 7 and tries to write: every JN answers “IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8”, and the NameNode exits. Even with a fake fencer (/bin/true), the shared log cannot be corrupted.
  • Fencing is still needed in production (e.g. sshfence): an old active could otherwise answer stale reads to clients for a while.
  • With fewer than a majority of JNs, no NameNode can write, so both shut down: HDFS chooses consistency over availability (the CP side of the CAP theorem).
  • Since Hadoop 3, more than two NameNodes are supported (one active, several standbys), and Observer NameNodes can serve reads.

Exam tip: For “Explain NameNode HA with QJM”: draw active + standby NameNodes, 3 JournalNodes, ZooKeeper with two ZKFCs, and DataNodes reporting to both. Then list: shared edits via quorum (majority), standby tails edits, automatic failover via ZooKeeper session + ZKFC, fencing and epochs to prevent split-brain. Mention the Secondary NameNode is not used in HA; the standby does checkpointing.

Building and breaking a real HA cluster

SHUTDOWN_MSG: Shutting down NameNode at nn1/172.20.0.6 15:41:39 after : nn1:8020=Failed nn2:8020=active
    A frozen process (like a long garbage-collection pause) is the dangerous case: it doesn't know it was replaced. When nn1 woke and tried to write with epoch 7, all three JournalNodes refused, and nn1 shut itself down. Split-brain prevented, even with a fencer that does nothing.

  6. Lose the JournalNode majority

    docker stop lab7-jn3-1 ; … ; docker stop lab7-jn2-1
    15:45:56 nn1:8020=standby nn2:8020=active 15:46:00 write 1: ok 15:46:00 >>> stop jn3 (2 of 3 JournalNodes left) 15:46:02 write 2: ok 15:46:02 >>> stop jn2 (1 of 3 left: no majority) 15:47:32 write 3: FAILED (no answer) 15:47:42 nn1:8020=Failed nn2:8020=Failed nn1: java.io.IOException: Timed out waiting 20000ms for a quorum of nodes to respond. nn2: java.io.IOException: Timed out waiting 20000ms for a quorum of nodes to respond.
    With 2 of 3 JournalNodes, everything works. With 1 of 3, the active can't get a majority for 20 s and stops; the standby can't become active either. HDFS stops rather than risk losing edits. Starting the JournalNodes and NameNodes again (restore.sh) brought the cluster back with all files.

Put the three JournalNodes on three different machines (ideally different racks), never all on the NameNode hosts. Losing two JournalNodes stops the whole file system, as step 6 showed.

5 See it happen

Crash, takeover, and the zombie that wakes up

Six steps from our lab. Left: what admins and clients see. Right: ZooKeeper, the ZKFCs and the JournalNodes.

Automatic failover with QJM 6 steps
What you see
  1. nn1 is active, nn2 standby. A client writes a file every 2 seconds.
  2. nn1 crashes. For a few seconds nn2 is still standby; one client write is waiting.
  3. About 10 s later nn2 is active. The waiting write succeeds (22 s), and all 12 writes complete.
  4. A different test: nn1 is frozen, not dead. nn2 takes over and the epoch rises from 7 to 8.
  5. nn1 wakes up and tries to write edits with epoch 7. All three JournalNodes refuse.
  6. nn1 shuts itself down. There is only ever one active writer.
Behind the screen
  1. nn1's ZKFC holds the lock znode; nn1 writes each edit to the JournalNodes; nn2 tails them.
  2. nn1 crashes. Its ZooKeeper session stops being renewed.
  3. After the 10 s session timeout the lock disappears; nn2's ZKFC takes it, fences nn1 and makes nn2 active with a higher epoch.
  4. In the zombie test, nn1 is only frozen. nn2 becomes active with epoch 8, and the JNs promise to ignore anything older.
  5. nn1 wakes up and sends edits with epoch 7. The JNs reject them.
  6. nn1 exits. The epoch rule guarantees a single writer even if fencing fails.

6 Real-world use

HA at work

1. Every production cluster

Enterprise distributions and admin guides treat NameNode HA with QJM as the standard for production clusters; a single NameNode is accepted only for dev and test.

2 NameNodes→3 or 5 JournalNodes→ZooKeeper

2. Rolling upgrades and patching

Admins patch the standby, fail over to it with haadmin -failover, then patch the old active. The file system stays online during OS and Hadoop upgrades.

Patch standby→Graceful failover→Patch the other

3. Long garbage-collection pauses

A NameNode with a very large heap can pause for many seconds. If that exceeds the ZooKeeper timeout, a failover happens and the paused NameNode exits on waking, exactly our zombie test. Admins tune heap and timeouts to avoid needless failovers.

Pause > 10 s→Failover→Old NN exits

4. Monitoring failovers

Monitoring reads tag.HAState from each NameNode's JMX and alerts on changes. A failover is not an outage, but it means something failed and should be investigated.

/jmx→HAState watch→On-call alert

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.

7 Run the code

A quorum journal with epochs, in Python

Three JournalNodes each remember the highest epoch they have promised. A NameNode becomes the writer by getting a majority to promise its new epoch; an edit is accepted only by JNs that have not promised a higher one. Run it to replay our zombie test.

jns = {"jn1": 7, "jn2": 7, "jn3": 7}          # last promised epoch on each JournalNode
log = []

def become_active(name):
    epoch = max(jns.values()) + 1
    ok = [j for j in jns if jns[j] < epoch]
    if len(ok) >= 2:                          # majority of 3
        for j in ok:
            jns[j] = epoch
        print(f"{name} is ACTIVE with epoch {epoch}")
        return epoch
    print(f"{name} could not get a majority")

def write(name, epoch, edit):
    accepted = [j for j in jns if epoch >= jns[j]]
    if len(accepted) >= 2:
        log.append(edit)
        print(f"{name} wrote {edit!r} (accepted by {len(accepted)} JNs)")
    else:
        print(f"{name} REJECTED: epoch {epoch} is less than promised {max(jns.values())}")

e1 = 7                                        # nn1 was active with epoch 7, then froze
e2 = become_active("nn2")                     # nn2 takes over
write("nn2", e2, "mkdir /results")
write("nn1", e1, "delete /results")           # the zombie wakes up
print("edit log:", log)
nn2 is ACTIVE with epoch 8 nn2 wrote 'mkdir /results' (accepted by 3 JNs) nn1 REJECTED: epoch 7 is less than promised 8 edit log: ['mkdir /results']
  • epoch = max(jns.values()) + 1A new writer always asks for a higher epoch than any JournalNode has seen. Our lab epochs went 7 → 8.
  • accepted = [j for j in jns if epoch >= jns[j]]A JournalNode refuses any write whose epoch is lower than what it promised: “IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8”.
  • if len(accepted) >= 2:A write needs a majority (2 of 3). That is why losing two JournalNodes stopped the whole cluster.

9 Quiz and revision

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 7.1 in one screen

  • HA = one active + standby NameNode(s) per nameservice; no Secondary NameNode.
  • QJM: edits to 3 or 5 JournalNodes; a majority must accept. Lose the majority → NameNodes stop (lab: “Timed out waiting 20000ms for a quorum”).
  • ZKFC + ZooKeeper: ephemeral lock znode; session timeout 10 s → automatic failover (lab: ~10 s).
  • Failover steps: lock → fence old active → transition standby to active.
  • Epochs stop a zombie: “IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8” → old active exits.
  • Clients use the nameservice and a failover proxy provider; a dead address can cost a 20 s connect timeout.
  • Commands: hdfs haadmin -getAllServiceState, -failover nn2 nn1, hdfs zkfc -formatZK, namenode -bootstrapStandby.
  • Lab: 12/12 writes survived a crash; graceful failover 2.2 s.

Interview and viva questions

How does NameNode high availability work in Hadoop?

Two or more NameNodes serve one namespace: one active, the rest standby. The active writes every edit to a quorum of JournalNodes; standbys read those edits and receive block reports from all DataNodes, so they hold an up-to-date namespace and block map. ZooKeeper and a ZKFailoverController next to each NameNode run an election; when the active fails, a standby is fenced in and promoted automatically.

What is the role of ZooKeeper in HDFS HA?

It provides the lock used for leader election. The active NameNode's ZKFC holds an ephemeral znode while its session is alive. If the NameNode or ZKFC dies or is partitioned, the session expires, the znode disappears, and another ZKFC acquires it and triggers failover. ZooKeeper does not store HDFS metadata.

What is fencing and why is it needed?

Fencing ensures that a previously active NameNode can no longer act as active, for example by killing it over SSH. With QJM, epochs already prevent the old active from writing edits, but fencing also prevents it from serving stale reads to clients that still contact it.

What is the difference between the Secondary NameNode and the Standby NameNode?

The Secondary NameNode only merges the edit log into a new fsimage (checkpointing) and cannot replace the NameNode. The Standby NameNode keeps a live, up-to-date copy of the namespace, performs checkpointing, and can become active within seconds.

Why use an odd number of JournalNodes?

Writes need a majority. With 3 JNs one may fail; with 4 still only one may fail (majority 3), so the fourth adds cost without tolerance. 5 JNs tolerate two failures.

What happens to clients during a failover?

Clients using the nameservice retry on the other NameNode. In-flight calls may be delayed by timeouts (in our lab one write took 22 s), but they complete once the new active is up; applications normally see only a pause.

P8Advanced

Majority from votes

Simulate the epoch protocol: JNs promise a new epoch only if it is higher than their current one. Run two competing candidates with epochs 5 and 5 and show only one can win.

Input
3 JNs at epoch 4
Expected output
nn1: 3 promises -> ACTIVE
nn2: 0 promises -> refused
Show solution and explanation 
jns = [4, 4, 4]
def propose(name, epoch):
    yes = 0
    for i, e in enumerate(jns):
        if epoch > e:
            jns[i] = epoch; yes += 1
    return f"{name}: {yes} promises -> {'ACTIVE' if yes >= 2 else 'refused'}"
print(propose("nn1", 5))
print(propose("nn2", 5))
nn1: 3 promises -> ACTIVE nn2: 0 promises -> refused

How it works: nn1 gets 3 promises; nn2 with the same epoch gets 0 and is refused. Real QJM uses the same rule: a strictly higher epoch from a majority.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

HA is a favourite topic for Hadoop administrator and senior data engineer interviews.

HDFS · architectureFresher

“Is the NameNode a single point of failure?”

The classic question.

The interviewer is checking: Hadoop 1 vs Hadoop 2+ HA.

Show a strong answer
  • In Hadoop 1, yes. Since Hadoop 2, HA runs an active and standby NameNode sharing edits through JournalNodes (QJM).
  • ZooKeeper + ZKFC fail over automatically in seconds; epochs and fencing prevent split-brain.
  • The Secondary NameNode was never a backup; it only checkpoints.
Operations · incidentExperienced

Failovers happen every few days at peak hours

No hardware is failing, but the active NameNode keeps losing its role around 2 pm.

The interviewer is checking: GC pauses vs ZooKeeper timeouts.

Show a strong answer
  • Check NameNode GC logs: a pause longer than the ZooKeeper session timeout (10 s default) makes ZKFC lose the lock, and the woken NameNode exits.
  • Fix the cause: heap sizing, GC tuning, fewer objects (small files), RPC load from a misbehaving job.
  • Only then consider a slightly longer session timeout; it also slows real failovers.
Design · placementExperienced

Where do you put JournalNodes and ZooKeeper?

Designing a new 60-node cluster with 2 racks of masters.

The interviewer is checking: failure domains.

Show a strong answer
  • 3 JNs and 3 ZooKeeper servers on separate machines across racks, so one rack failure leaves a majority (ideally 3 racks for masters).
  • The two NameNodes on different racks and power feeds; JN disks dedicated and fast (edits are synchronous).
  • Use real fencing (sshfence or a power fencer) and monitor HAState.
Concepts · split-brainExperienced

“What if fencing fails? Can two NameNodes both be active?”

A follow-up to test depth.

The interviewer is checking: QJM epochs.

Show a strong answer
  • With QJM, a new active gets a higher epoch from a majority of JournalNodes; they reject writes from older epochs, so the old active cannot change the namespace and exits on its first write.
  • It could briefly serve stale reads, which is why fencing is still configured.
  • Real lab log: “IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8”, then “Shutting down NameNode”.
