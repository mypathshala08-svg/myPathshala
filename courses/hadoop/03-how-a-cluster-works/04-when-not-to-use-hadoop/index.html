When not to use Hadoop

Hadoop is brilliant for very large, batch, on-premises data with a team to run it, and a poor choice almost everywhere else. This lesson gives you a five-question checklist (size, latency, updates, team, location), the better tool for each case Hadoop handles badly, the situations where Hadoop is still the right answer, and the cost arithmetic behind the decision. Interviewers value this judgement as much as Hadoop knowledge itself.

Hadoop is brilliant for the problem it was built for: very large, batch, on-premises data with a team to run it. Using it for small or real-time problems wastes money and makes things slower. Today you'll learn to say “no, use this instead” with clear reasons, which interviewers value as much as knowing Hadoop itself.

2 Definition

When Hadoop is the wrong tool

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Hadoop is designed for high-throughput batch processing of very large, mostly append-only data sets on clusters operated by a skilled team. It is a poor fit when: the data comfortably fits on one machine; the workload needs low-latency reads or writes of individual records (OLTP); results must be produced in real time from streaming events; data must be updated or deleted frequently at record level; the data consists of very many small files; work is iterative or interactive and MapReduce is the engine; or the organisation lacks the operational capacity to run a distributed system, in which case managed cloud services or simpler tools have a lower total cost of ownership.

In simple words

Use Hadoop for huge data, processed in big batches, by a team that can run a cluster. Don't use it for small data, instant answers, live streams, constantly changing records, millions of tiny files, or when nobody can look after the cluster. Something simpler or more specialised will be faster and cheaper.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

OLTPOnline transaction processing: many small, fast reads and writes (orders, payments). Databases like PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle.
OLAPOnline analytical processing: big scans and aggregations for reports. Warehouses, Hive, Spark SQL.
TCOTotal cost of ownership: hardware + software + people + power + downtime over several years.
Cloud data warehouseManaged analytical database billed by storage and usage: BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, Synapse.
Embedded analytics engineA fast analytical library on one machine, such as DuckDB or Polars. Handles tens to hundreds of GB on a good laptop or server.
Stream processingProcessing events continuously as they arrive (Kafka, Flink, Spark Structured Streaming).
LakehouseFiles in cheap object storage plus table formats (Iceberg, Delta, Hudi) that add SQL tables, updates and transactions.
Over-engineeringBuilding a more complex system than the problem needs.

Rough thresholds (2026, approximate)

Up to tens of GBA laptop with pandas/Polars/DuckDB, or a single database server
Hundreds of GB to a few TBOne large server (hundreds of GB RAM, NVMe) with DuckDB/PostgreSQL, or a cloud warehouse
Tens of TB to petabytes, batchDistributed: Spark on Hadoop/YARN or Kubernetes, a lakehouse, or a cloud warehouse
Response in millisecondsOLTP database or key–value store (or HBase on HDFS), not MapReduce/Hive
Results within seconds of an eventKafka + Flink or Spark Structured Streaming

Thresholds move every year as single machines get faster. Measure your own workload before deciding.

The decision at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner You don't hire a truck to carry a bag

  • A truck (Hadoop) is perfect for moving a whole house. For one bag of vegetables, a scooter (a laptop or a database) is faster, cheaper and needs no special driver.
  • A truck is also wrong for delivering a pizza in 10 minutes (real-time) or for changing one item in a parcel again and again (updates).
  • And a truck needs a licensed driver and maintenance (an operations team). If you don't have one, rent a delivery service (the cloud).

Intermediate Six warning signs

  • Small data: under ~1 TB and not growing fast. One machine is simpler and often faster.
  • Point lookups: “show this customer's balance in 50 ms”. Use a database or HBase.
  • Real time: “alert within 2 seconds of a suspicious payment”. Use streaming.
  • Frequent updates: “change order status 10 times a day”. Use a database, or a lakehouse table format.
  • Millions of small files: each costs NameNode memory and a task. Combine them or use an object store.
  • No one to run it: a cluster needs patching, monitoring, security, capacity planning. Use managed services.

Advanced The modern landscape: what replaced Hadoop where

  • Engine: Spark (and Flink, Trino) replaced MapReduce for most processing, often still on YARN or now on Kubernetes.
  • Storage: cloud object storage (S3, GCS, ADLS) replaced HDFS for many new cloud platforms; separate compute and storage scale independently.
  • Tables: Iceberg, Delta Lake and Hudi brought updates, time travel and schema evolution to files, which Hive tables on HDFS lacked.
  • Warehouses: serverless warehouses let small teams query terabytes with SQL and no cluster at all.
  • Where Hadoop still wins: large on-premises estates with existing investment, strict data-residency or air-gapped environments, steady heavy batch load where owned hardware is cheaper than cloud, and teams already skilled in it.

Exam tip: For “Discuss the limitations of Hadoop” or “When should Hadoop not be used?”, list: small data, low-latency/OLTP, real-time streaming, frequent updates, small files, iterative/interactive workloads with MapReduce, operational complexity/security setup, and name a better alternative for each.

A good answer to “Should we use Hadoop?” starts with questions, not tools: How much data, and how fast is it growing? How quickly do you need answers? Do records change? Who will run it? Where must the data live? The answers usually make the choice obvious.

4 Step by step

A five-question checklist, applied to the pickle startup

  1. How big is the data, and how fast is it growing?

    gb, growth, years = 40, 0.5, 3
print(round(gb * (1 + growth) ** years), "GB in 3 years")
    135 GB in 3 years
    Even growing 50% a year, it stays small enough for one server. Distributed storage adds nothing.

  2. How fast must answers come?

    The website needs order status in milliseconds; the founders want a sales dashboard updated daily.

    The website needs an OLTP database (they already have one). The daily dashboard is light analytics, not a batch job over terabytes.

  3. Do records change?

    Orders change status (placed → packed → shipped → delivered).

    Frequent updates fit a database, not write-once files.

  4. Who will run it?

    Three people, none of them infrastructure engineers.

    A self-managed cluster would need monitoring, upgrades, security and disk swaps. That is a job they cannot staff.

  5. Decision

    Keep PostgreSQL for the app; copy data nightly into DuckDB or a small cloud warehouse for dashboards. Revisit if data reaches many TB.

    Cost: close to zero extra. Time to first dashboard: days, not months. Kabir has just given his first expert “no”.

6 Real-world use

Real decisions about Hadoop

1. Analysts moving back to one machine

Many analytics teams found that most of their queries touch tens of GB, not TB. Fast single-machine engines answer them in seconds on a laptop or one server, without a cluster queue.

Medium data→Single-node engine→Answers in seconds

2. Migrations from Hadoop to the cloud

A common project today: move Hive tables and Spark jobs from an ageing on-prem cluster to cloud object storage and a lakehouse or warehouse. Engineers who understand Hadoop lead these migrations.

HDFS + Hive→Copy + rewrite jobs→Cloud lakehouse

3. Real-time fraud with a batch partner

Payment companies use streaming systems to score each transaction in milliseconds, while large batch jobs (on Hadoop or a lakehouse) train the models on years of history every night. Neither replaces the other.

Live events→Streaming score→Model from nightly batch

4. Government and regulated data

Organisations bound by strict data-residency rules or air-gapped networks keep large on-premises clusters, where Hadoop (with Spark on YARN) remains a practical platform.

Sensitive data→Own data centre→Hadoop + Spark

5. IoT data that needed a different shape

Teams that wrote one tiny file per sensor reading into HDFS hit NameNode memory limits. The fix was to stream readings through Kafka and write hourly Parquet files, or to use object storage.

Tiny readings→Batch into big files→Healthy storage

Examples describe common patterns, not any single company. Thresholds are approximate and change as hardware improves.

7 Run the code

A tool-recommendation function

This function applies the five-question checklist from Part 4 to a workload and recommends a tool. Run it on the three projects from the animation, then try the challenges.

def recommend(size_tb, latency_s, updates, ops_team, must_be_onprem):
    if latency_s < 1:
        return "OLTP database or key-value store"
    if latency_s < 60:
        return "Stream processing (Kafka + Flink / Spark Streaming)"
    if size_tb < 1:
        return "One machine: PostgreSQL / DuckDB / pandas"
    if not ops_team and not must_be_onprem:
        return "Managed cloud warehouse or lakehouse"
    if updates:
        return "Lakehouse tables (Iceberg/Delta/Hudi) on Spark"
    return "Hadoop + Spark (batch, distributed)"

projects = {
    "pickle startup": (0.04, 86400, True, False, False),
    "fraud alerts":   (50, 2, False, True, False),
    "telecom CDRs":   (4000, 86400, False, True, True),
}
for name, p in projects.items():
    print(f"{name:15} -> {recommend(*p)}")
pickle startup -> One machine: PostgreSQL / DuckDB / pandas fraud alerts -> Stream processing (Kafka + Flink / Spark Streaming) telecom CDRs -> Hadoop + Spark (batch, distributed)
  • def recommend(size_tb, latency_s, updates, ops_team, must_be_onprem):The five questions as parameters: size, how fast answers are needed, whether records change, whether a team can run a cluster, and where the data must live.
  • if latency_s < 1:The order of the checks matters: latency rules out batch tools first, whatever the data size.
  • print(f"{name:15} -> {recommend(*p)}")*p unpacks the tuple into the five arguments.

Run it on your PC: how much can one computer do?

Before reaching for a cluster, measure a single machine. This program writes 20 lakh fake orders to a CSV file and totals them per item with plain Python on one core. Save it as one_machine.py and run it.

Millions of rows per second, no cluster

# one_machine.py: how much data can ONE ordinary computer aggregate quickly?
import random, time, csv, os, tempfile

random.seed(1)
items = ["chai", "vada pav", "samosa", "poha", "idli", "dosa", "thali", "lassi"]
rows = 2_000_000
path = os.path.join(tempfile.gettempdir(), "orders_2m.csv")

t0 = time.time()
with open(path, "w", newline="") as f:
    w = csv.writer(f)
    for i in range(rows):
        w.writerow([i, random.choice(items), random.randint(10, 300)])
t1 = time.time()

totals = {}
with open(path, newline="") as f:
    for _, item, amount in csv.reader(f):
        totals[item] = totals.get(item, 0) + int(amount)
t2 = time.time()

size_mb = os.path.getsize(path) / 1024 / 1024
print(f"Rows: {rows:,}  File: {size_mb:.0f} MB")
print(f"Write: {t1 - t0:.1f} s   Group-by in plain Python: {t2 - t1:.1f} s")
print("Top item:", max(totals, key=totals.get))
print(f"Rough speed: {rows / (t2 - t1) / 1e6:.1f} million rows per second on one core")
os.remove(path)

# In a terminal:  python one_machine.py
Rows: 2,000,000 File: 35 MB Write: 2.3 s Group-by in plain Python: 1.1 s Top item: vada pav Rough speed: 1.8 million rows per second on one core

Recorded on a small cloud machine with Python 3. Your numbers will differ. Plain Python is the slow option: engines like DuckDB or Polars are typically many times faster and use all cores. At these speeds, a few hundred million rows are a single-machine job.

9 Quiz and revision

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 3.4 in one screen

  • Hadoop fits: huge, batch, mostly append-only data, run by a skilled team, often on-premises.
  • Not Hadoop: small data (one machine), ms lookups (database), real time (streaming), frequent updates (DB / table formats).
  • Also bad: millions of small files, iterative/interactive work on MapReduce, no ops team.
  • Checklist: size + growth · latency · updates · team · location.
  • Rough sizes: ≤ tens of GB laptop · ≤ few TB one big server / warehouse · tens of TB+ distributed.
  • Still wins: petabytes on-prem, data residency, existing investment, shared multi-engine platform.
  • Modern replacements: Spark (engine), object storage (HDFS), Iceberg/Delta/Hudi (tables), cloud warehouses.
  • Decide with numbers: TCO over 3 years and a weighted decision matrix.

Interview and viva questions

What are the limitations of Hadoop?

High latency (batch-oriented), unsuitable for real-time processing and point lookups, inefficient with many small files because of NameNode memory and task overhead, write-once files that make updates difficult, slow iterative processing with MapReduce, and considerable operational and security complexity. For small data it is slower and costlier than single-machine tools.

When would you not recommend Hadoop?

When the data fits comfortably on one machine; when the workload needs millisecond responses or frequent record updates; when results are needed in real time; when data arrives as huge numbers of tiny files; or when the organisation has no team to operate a cluster and can use managed cloud services instead.

What are alternatives to Hadoop?

For processing: Spark, Flink, Trino. For storage: cloud object storage with table formats (Iceberg, Delta Lake, Hudi). For analytics: cloud data warehouses (BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, Synapse) and OLAP engines (ClickHouse, Druid). For small data: PostgreSQL, DuckDB, Polars, pandas. For streams: Kafka with Flink or Spark Structured Streaming.

Is Hadoop still worth learning?

Yes, as a foundation. Many enterprises still run it, migrations from it are common, and its concepts (distributed storage, replication, data locality, MapReduce, resource management) underlie modern platforms. Knowing its limits is part of knowing it well.

How would you decide between Hadoop and a cloud data warehouse?

Compare requirements and total cost: data volume and growth, query patterns, latency, data-residency rules, existing skills and investments, and three-year TCO including staff. Steady, very large on-premises workloads with residency rules may favour Hadoop/Spark; smaller teams with variable, SQL-centric workloads often favour a managed warehouse.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Interviewers often test judgement with a “should we use Hadoop?” question. Practise answering with questions, numbers and alternatives.

Startup · architectureFresher

“Our 50 GB database is slow, so let's move to Hadoop”

A startup's PostgreSQL reports take minutes. Someone proposes moving everything to Hadoop “because it's built for big data”.

The interviewer is checking: do you diagnose before prescribing, and do you know that 50 GB is not big data?

Show a strong answer
  • 50 GB is small. A cluster would add overhead and operations work, and probably be slower for these queries.
  • First diagnose: missing indexes, reports running on the live transactional database, poor query design.
  • Options: add indexes and summary tables; run reports on a read replica; copy data nightly to DuckDB or a small cloud warehouse for analytics.
  • Revisit distributed tools if the data grows to many TB.

Avoid: recommending Hadoop because it is “the big data tool”.

Payments · real time1–3 years

Use Hive for live fraud alerts?

A payments team stores all transactions in Hive and runs a fraud query every 15 minutes. Fraudsters complete their attacks in under 5 minutes.

The interviewer is checking: can you separate real-time and batch needs?

Show a strong answer
  • A 15-minute batch loop can never stop a 5-minute attack. Scoring must happen on each transaction as it arrives.
  • Stream transactions through Kafka; score them with Flink or Spark Structured Streaming using features kept in a fast store; block or flag in milliseconds to seconds.
  • Keep Hive/Spark on the history to train and evaluate the models nightly, and for investigations.

Avoid: “Run the Hive query every minute.” Batch start-up and scan costs make that impractical and still too slow.

IoT · storage1–3 years

The NameNode keeps running out of memory

A smart-meter project writes every reading (a few KB) as its own file into HDFS. After six months the NameNode heap is full and restarts take hours.

The interviewer is checking: do you recognise the small-files problem and its fixes?

Show a strong answer
  • Hundreds of millions of tiny files mean hundreds of millions of file and block objects in NameNode memory.
  • Short term: compact existing files into large Parquet or SequenceFiles per hour/day, and archive old ones (HAR).
  • Long term: send readings to Kafka and write large files in batches; or store raw readings in an object store or a time-series database designed for many small writes.

Avoid: only increasing the NameNode heap; it delays the problem and makes pauses worse.

Enterprise · strategy3+ years

“Should we shut down our Hadoop cluster and move to the cloud?”

A bank runs a 150-node Hadoop cluster with 3 PB. The CIO hears “Hadoop is dead” and asks for a recommendation.

The interviewer is checking: can you give a balanced, requirement-driven recommendation?

Show a strong answer
  1. Constraints: can this data legally be in a public cloud region? Many banking rules require data to stay in India and under specific controls.
  2. Cost: compare 3–5 year TCO, including migration effort, cloud egress and staff, against hardware refresh costs.
  3. Workloads: which jobs are steady and heavy (cheaper on owned hardware) and which are spiky or new (better in the cloud)?
  4. Path: often a phased or hybrid approach: modernise on-prem (Spark, table formats), move suitable workloads first, keep the rest until hardware refresh.

Avoid: deciding on fashion (“Hadoop is dead”) or on fear of change.
