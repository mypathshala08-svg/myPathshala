Replication and rack awareness

Replication is what lets Hadoop run on ordinary servers that fail. This lesson explains the replication factor, the rack-aware placement rule that balances safety against network cost, and what the NameNode does when replicas go missing or reappear, with real timings from a two-rack cluster where we stopped DataNodes on purpose.

1 The story

Three copies, but not three copies anywhere

The lab cluster has two racks: datanode-1 and datanode-2 in /rack1, datanode-3 and datanode-4 in /rack2. Riya checks the five blocks of the canteen file. Every block has three copies, and every block has them in both racks: two in one rack, one in the other. Never all three together. Then Kabir pulls the plug on datanode-1. A minute later, the NameNode quietly makes new copies elsewhere. Nobody lost a single order.

Riya

Why not put the three copies on three different racks? Wouldn't that be safest?

Arjun Sir

Safest, but slowest. Data crossing between racks goes through the core switch, which is shared and limited. HDFS's default rule is a compromise: one copy on one rack, two copies on another rack. Lose a whole rack and you still have a copy, but a write crosses racks only once.

Kabir

And when I killed datanode-1?

Arjun Sir

The NameNode stopped hearing its heartbeats, waited, declared it dead, noticed some blocks had only two copies, and asked other DataNodes to copy them. When you switched datanode-1 back on, its old copies became extra, and the NameNode deleted them. All of it automatic. That's replication: the reason Hadoop could run on cheap machines that fail.

2 Definition

Replication and rack awareness

Formal definition (write this in exams)

HDFS stores each block as several replicas on different DataNodes; the replication factor (dfs.replication, default 3) is set per file and can be changed with hdfs dfs -setrep. Rack awareness means the NameNode knows each DataNode's rack, from a topology script or mapping (net.topology.script.file.name); without one, every node is in /default-rack. The default block placement policy (BlockPlacementPolicyDefault) puts the first replica on the writer's node if the writer is a DataNode (otherwise on a random node), the second on a node in a different rack, and the third on a different node in the same rack as the second; further replicas go to random nodes, with at most two replicas per rack where possible. The NameNode continuously compares live replicas with the target: under-replicated blocks are copied, over-replicated blocks have extra replicas deleted.

In simple words

Every block is kept three times on three machines, spread over two racks, so a broken disk, machine or even a whole rack doesn't lose data. If a copy disappears, HDFS makes a new one by itself.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

ReplicaOne stored copy of a block on one DataNode.
Replication factorTarget number of replicas for a file's blocks (the “3” in hdfs dfs -ls).
RackA group of nodes sharing a switch; a common failure and bandwidth boundary.

Over-replicatedMore replicas than the target, e.g. after a node returns. Extras are deleted.

Default factordfs.replication = 3; minimum for a write to succeed: dfs.namenode.replication.min = 1
Placement policyBlockPlacementPolicyDefault: writer's node, other rack, same rack as 2nd
Rack mappingnet.topology.script.file.name; none set → /default-rack for all
Dead node detectedAfter 2 × recheck-interval (5 min) + 10 × heartbeat (3 s) = 10 min 30 s by default
Copy speed limitsdfs.namenode.replication.max-streams = 2 copies per DataNode at a time (more for high-priority blocks)
Storage costReplication 3 = 3× raw disk (200% overhead); erasure coding is the alternative (Lesson 7.3)

Replication at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Important documents in three lockers

  • You keep three photocopies of your marksheet: one at home, two at your uncle's house in another city.
  • If your house floods (a rack fails), the uncle's copies survive. If one copy at the uncle's tears, you still have two.
  • Whenever a copy is lost, you make a new photocopy straight away. That's re-replication.

Intermediate Why this exact placement

  • Write cost: the pipeline crosses racks only once (between replica 1 and replica 2).
  • Rack failure: two racks hold copies, so losing one rack never loses a block.
  • Read speed: readers on two racks find a nearby copy; with locality, tasks run on a node holding a replica.
  • With no topology script, HDFS thinks everything is in one rack and spreads copies only across nodes.

Advanced What the NameNode does continuously

  • Keeps queues of low-redundancy blocks by priority: a block with 1 live replica left is repaired before one with 2.
  • Chooses a source DataNode and a target (following the rack rules) and sends the copy command in a heartbeat reply.
  • When a dead node returns, its replicas make blocks over-replicated; extras are deleted (“Pending deletion blocks”).
  • Decommissioning a node first copies all its blocks elsewhere, then lets it leave: the safe way to remove hardware.
  • Replication factor 1 is allowed (scratch data), but a single disk failure then loses blocks.

Exam tip: For “Explain rack awareness / replica placement”: draw two racks; replica 1 on the writer's node (or random), replica 2 on another rack, replica 3 on another node of replica 2's rack; state the benefits (rack-failure tolerance, one cross-rack transfer per write, read locality) and how racks are configured (net.topology.script.file.name).

5 See it happen

A node dies, the cluster heals

The real timeline from step 4, frame by frame.

Re-replication after datanode-3 stops 6 steps
What you see
  1. Each block has three live replicas, spread over both racks.
  2. datanode-3 is stopped. For a while the NameNode still lists 4 live DataNodes: a few missed heartbeats are tolerated.
  3. After the dead-node interval, the NameNode declares it dead (“lost heartbeat”).
  4. Blocks that had a replica there now have only two: 4 of 8 blocks are under-replicated.
  5. Seven seconds later all of them are back to three copies. Status: HEALTHY.
  6. When a stopped node returns with old replicas, the extras are deleted (7 pending deletions, then 0).
Behind the screen
  1. Block A has one replica in rack 1 and two in rack 2 (on dn-3 and dn-4).
  2. dn-3 stops. The NameNode notices missing heartbeats but doesn't act yet.
  3. At the dead-node limit, dn-3 is removed from the topology and its replicas from the block map.
  4. Block A now has 2 live replicas: under-replicated. The NameNode chooses an existing replica as source and a new node as target, following the rack rules.
  5. The copy happens directly between DataNodes. Block A is back to 3 replicas in two racks.
  6. If a dead node comes back with its old replicas, blocks are over-replicated and the NameNode deletes the extras.

6 Real-world use

Replication in real clusters

1. Surviving a rack outage

A top-of-rack switch or power strip failure takes 20–40 servers offline at once. With correct rack mapping, every block still has a copy in another rack, and jobs keep running while the rack is repaired.

Rack down→Copies in other racks→No data loss

2. Extra copies for hot files

Files read by every task (job jars, small lookup tables) get a higher replication factor so many nodes can read them locally. MapReduce does this automatically for job files (mapreduce.client.submit.file.replication, default 10).

Hot file→More replicas→Local reads everywhere

3. Retiring servers safely

Before old servers are removed, admins decommission them: HDFS copies every replica they hold to other nodes, and only then marks them safe to switch off.

Exclude node→Re-replicate→Remove hardware

4. Cheaper protection for cold data

Replication triples storage. Large platforms move rarely-read data to erasure coding, which protects it with about 1.5× storage instead of 3× (Lesson 7.3).

Hot: replication→Cold: erasure coding→Half the disk

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.

7 Run the code

Simulate the default placement policy

This program places three replicas of each block with HDFS's default rule on a cluster of two racks, then checks the result the way fsck would: at least two racks, at most two replicas per rack.

import random
random.seed(7)
racks = {"/rack1": ["dn1", "dn2"], "/rack2": ["dn3", "dn4"]}
rack_of = {dn: r for r, nodes in racks.items() for dn in nodes}

def place(writer=None):
    first = writer if writer in rack_of else random.choice(list(rack_of))
    other_racks = [r for r in racks if r != rack_of[first]]
    second = random.choice(racks[random.choice(other_racks)])
    third = random.choice([d for d in racks[rack_of[second]] if d != second])
    return [first, second, third]

for blk in ["blk_A", "blk_B", "blk_C"]:
    replicas = place()
    used = [rack_of[d] for d in replicas]
    ok = len(set(used)) >= 2 and max(used.count(r) for r in used) <= 2
    print(blk, replicas, used, "OK" if ok else "BAD")
blk_A ['dn3', 'dn2', 'dn1'] ['/rack2', '/rack1', '/rack1'] OK blk_B ['dn1', 'dn4', 'dn3'] ['/rack1', '/rack2', '/rack2'] OK blk_C ['dn2', 'dn3', 'dn4'] ['/rack1', '/rack2', '/rack2'] OK
  • first = writer if writer in rack_of else random.choice(list(rack_of))Replica 1: on the writer's own node if the writer is a DataNode (fast, local write); otherwise a random node.
  • second = random.choice(racks[random.choice(other_racks)])Replica 2: any node in a different rack, so a rack failure can't take everything.
  • third = random.choice([d for d in racks[rack_of[second]] if d != second])Replica 3: a different node in the same rack as replica 2, so the data crosses racks only once.

Rack spread of every block under /user/hadoop/data

# rack_check.py: does every block follow the rack-aware placement rules?
# Run in the hdfs-lab folder while the cluster is up.
import re
import subprocess
from collections import Counter

cmd = ["docker", "compose", "exec", "-T", "namenode",
       "hdfs", "fsck", "/user/hadoop/data", "-files", "-blocks", "-racks"]
out = subprocess.run(cmd, capture_output=True, text=True).stdout

for line in out.splitlines():
    m = re.search(r"(blk_\d+)_\d+ len=(\d+).*\[(.*)\]", line)
    if not m:
        continue
    blk, racks = m.group(1), re.findall(r"(/rack\d+)/", m.group(3))
    per_rack = Counter(racks)
    ok = len(per_rack) >= 2 and max(per_rack.values()) <= 2
    print(f"{blk}: {dict(per_rack)}  {'OK' if ok else 'CHECK'}")

Interview and viva questions

What is rack awareness in Hadoop?

The NameNode knows which rack each DataNode is in (from a topology script or mapping) and uses it to place replicas: by default one replica on one rack and two on another. This protects data against a whole rack failing while limiting cross-rack network traffic, and lets schedulers prefer rack-local data.

How does HDFS place the three replicas of a block?

The first replica goes on the writer's node if the writer is a DataNode, otherwise on a random node; the second on a node in a different rack; the third on a different node in the same rack as the second. More replicas go to random nodes, keeping at most two per rack where possible.

What happens when a DataNode fails?

The NameNode stops receiving its heartbeats. After the dead-node interval (10 min 30 s by default) it marks the node dead, removes its replicas from the block map, and schedules copies of the now under-replicated blocks from surviving replicas to other DataNodes.

How do I change the replication of a file?

Use hdfs dfs -setrep N path (add -w to wait until the change is complete; giving a directory applies it to every file below it). New files follow dfs.replication of the writing client.

What is the difference between under-replicated and missing blocks?

An under-replicated block still has at least one live replica, so it is readable and HDFS will copy it. A missing block has no live replica at all: its data cannot be read until a node holding it returns.

Is replication a backup?

No. Replication protects against hardware failure, but deletes and bad writes are applied to every replica. Backups, snapshots and DistCp copies to another cluster protect against those.

How is the rack of a DataNode configured?

Set net.topology.script.file.name in core-site.xml to a script that prints one rack path (e.g. /dc1/rack07) for each IP or host name it is given, or use TableMapping with a host-to-rack file.

    hops = sum(rack[a] != rack[b] for a, b in zip(pipeline, pipeline[1:]))
    print(f"replication {len(pipeline)}: {pipeline} -> {hops} GB crosses racks")
replication 3: ['dn1', 'dn3', 'dn4'] -> 1 GB crosses racks replication 2: ['dn1', 'dn3'] -> 1 GB crosses racks

How it works: The write pipeline dn1 → dn3 → dn4 has one cross-rack hop (dn1 → dn3), so 1 GB crosses racks with replication 3 or 2; the dn3 → dn4 hop stays inside rack r2.

P7Business problem

Cost of replication

Disk costs Rs 3,000 per TB. 500 TB of data: compare the disk cost of replication 3 with erasure coding at 1.5×.

Input
500 TB · Rs 3,000/TB · 3× vs 1.5×
Expected output
replication 3  : Rs 45.0 lakh
erasure coding : Rs 22.5 lakh
Show solution and explanation 
for name, f in [("replication 3", 3), ("erasure coding", 1.5)]:
    print(f"{name:15}: Rs {500 * f * 3000 / 1e5:.1f} lakh")
replication 3 : Rs 45.0 lakh erasure coding : Rs 22.5 lakh

How it works: Rs 45 lakh against Rs 22.5 lakh of raw disk: why cold data moves to erasure coding.

P8Advanced

Placement for a writer on a DataNode

Write a function placing 3 replicas for a writer on dn4 with racks r1=[dn1,dn2] and r2=[dn3,dn4], and check the rule.

Input
writer dn4
Expected output
['dn4', 'dn1', 'dn2'] ['r2', 'r1', 'r1']
Show solution and explanation 
racks = {"r1": ["dn1", "dn2"], "r2": ["dn3", "dn4"]}
rack_of = {d: r for r, ds in racks.items() for d in ds}
def place(writer):
    first = writer
    second = racks[[r for r in racks if r != rack_of[first]][0]][0]
    third = [d for d in racks[rack_of[second]] if d != second][0]
    return [first, second, third]
p = place("dn4")
print(p, [rack_of[d] for d in p])
['dn4', 'dn1', 'dn2'] ['r2', 'r1', 'r1']

How it works: [dn4, dn1, dn2] in racks [r2, r1, r1]: local first replica, then two in the other rack.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Replication questions test whether you can reason about failures and their cost.

HDFS · placementFresher

“Explain where the three replicas of a block go, and why”

Probably the most common HDFS interview question.

The interviewer is checking: the rule and its reasons.

Show a strong answer
  • Replica 1 on the writer's node (or a random node if the client is outside the cluster), replica 2 on another rack, replica 3 on a different node in replica 2's rack.
  • Reasons: survives a whole rack failure; the write crosses racks only once; reads can find a nearby copy.
  • Requires a correct topology script; otherwise everything is in /default-rack.
Operations · failureExperienced

A DataNode died 2 minutes ago. Why is nothing being re-replicated?

A junior engineer sees a DataNode process crash and expects immediate copying.

The interviewer is checking: the dead-node timeout and why it exists.

Show a strong answer
  • The NameNode waits 10 min 30 s by default (2 × recheck interval + 10 heartbeats) before declaring a node dead.
  • The delay avoids copying terabytes every time a node is briefly slow or restarting.
  • Meanwhile the node is marked stale after 30 s, and reads can avoid it if dfs.namenode.avoid.read.stale.datanode is enabled.
  • If the node won't return, re-replication starts automatically after the timeout.
Design · racksExperienced

After a rack power failure, some files were unreadable

The cluster has replication 3, yet a single rack outage made several files unavailable.

The interviewer is checking: that you suspect the rack mapping.

Show a strong answer
  • With correct rack awareness that can't happen, so check hdfs dfsadmin -printTopology: probably no topology script, or nodes mapped to the wrong racks.
  • Also check files with replication 1 or 2, and mis-replicated blocks in fsck.
  • Fix the mapping; then fsck shows mis-replicated blocks, which the NameNode corrects over time (or run the balancer).
Cost · storageFresher

“Can we save money with replication 2?”

A manager wants to reduce disk costs by a third.

The interviewer is checking: balanced judgement about risk.

Show a strong answer
  • Replication 2 survives only one failure; a second disk failure during re-replication loses blocks, and rack failure protection becomes weaker.
  • Better options: erasure coding for cold data (≈1.5×), deleting or archiving unused data, compressing with efficient formats.
  • Replication 2 can be acceptable for data that can be regenerated quickly (intermediate results).
