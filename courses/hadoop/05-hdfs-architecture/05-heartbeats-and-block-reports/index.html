Heartbeats and block reports

The NameNode never polls its DataNodes. It learns everything from two messages they send: a heartbeat every three seconds and block reports listing what they store. This lesson shows both on a real cluster: a DataNode frozen on purpose, the NameNode's view of it changing from live to stale to dead, and the re-registration and block report when it returns.

Kabir freezes datanode-4 with docker pause: the process is still there, but it can't do anything. He keeps asking the NameNode about it. “Live, last contact 10 seconds ago… 27 seconds… 43… 51…” Still live. Then at 57 seconds: dead. He unpauses it, and six seconds later the NameNode says “live, last contact 0 seconds”. Riya: “How does the NameNode know any of this? It never looks at the DataNodes.”

Arjun Sir

Like a class register. Every DataNode calls out “present” to the NameNode every 3 seconds: that's a heartbeat, with a few numbers about its disks and load. The NameNode just notes the time. If a DataNode goes quiet, the NameNode doesn't phone it; silence itself is the information.

Kabir

And how did it know what blocks datanode-4 has, after it came back?

Arjun Sir

That's the second message: the block report. A DataNode lists every block it holds when it registers, then again every 6 hours, plus small updates whenever it gets or deletes a block. Heartbeats say “I'm alive”; block reports say “here's what I have”. And the NameNode's replies to heartbeats are how it gives orders: “copy this block”, “delete that one”.

2 Definition

Heartbeats and block reports

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A heartbeat is a periodic RPC from each DataNode to the NameNode (every dfs.heartbeat.interval = 3 s) carrying the DataNode's capacity, used and remaining space, number of active transfers and other load figures. It tells the NameNode the DataNode is alive, and the NameNode's reply carries commands for the DataNode, such as replicating a block to another node, deleting (invalidating) blocks, or re-registering. A DataNode not heard from for dfs.namenode.stale.datanode.interval (30 s) is stale; after 2 × dfs.namenode.heartbeat.recheck-interval + 10 × heartbeat interval (10 min 30 s by default) it is declared dead and its replicas are re-replicated. A block report is a list of all block replicas a DataNode stores (ID, length, generation stamp) sent at registration and then every dfs.blockreport.intervalMsec (6 hours); between full reports, incremental block reports announce replicas received or deleted. The NameNode builds and corrects its block map from these reports.

In simple words

Heartbeat: “I'm alive, and here's how full I am” every 3 seconds. Block report: “Here is every block I hold”, when starting and every 6 hours, with small updates in between. The NameNode answers heartbeats with jobs to do.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

HeartbeatSmall RPC every 3 s: alive + capacity and load numbers.
Heartbeat replyThe NameNode's answer, carrying commands: replicate, invalidate (delete), etc.
Last contactSeconds since the last heartbeat, shown in dfsadmin -report and the UI.
Stale nodeNo heartbeat for 30 s. Still “live”, but can be avoided for reads/writes.
Dead nodeNo heartbeat for 10 min 30 s (default). Its replicas are re-created elsewhere.
Full block reportEvery replica on the DataNode. At start-up and every 6 hours.
Incremental block reportOnly changes: replicas received or deleted since the last report.

Heartbeats at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Attendance and inventory

  • Heartbeat = roll call: every 3 seconds each shelf-keeper says “present”, and how much space their shelf has left.
  • Block report = stock-taking: the shelf-keeper lists every book on the shelf. Done when they join, then every 6 hours.
  • The head librarian answers the roll call with tasks: “photocopy book 25 for shelf 3”, “throw away the extra copy of book 7”.

Intermediate What the NameNode does with them

  • Updates each DataNode's “last contact” and capacity, which dfsadmin -report shows.
  • Chooses targets for new blocks using reported free space and load.
  • Marks nodes stale, then dead, purely from missing heartbeats.
  • Rebuilds the block map after a restart from full block reports (Lesson 5.6).
  • Finds lost, extra or corrupt replicas by comparing reports with what it expects.

Advanced Large-cluster details

  • The NameNode never initiates calls to DataNodes; all commands travel in heartbeat replies. This keeps one NameNode able to manage thousands of nodes.
  • Full block reports from big DataNodes (millions of blocks) are expensive; they are spread out over time and can be split per disk.
  • A busy NameNode may process heartbeats late; the optional lifeline RPC (dfs.namenode.lifeline.rpc-address) keeps busy-but-healthy nodes from being declared dead.
  • hdfs dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport host:9867 forces a full report, useful after disk repairs.

Exam tip: “Explain heartbeat and block report”: heartbeat = DataNode → NameNode every 3 s, liveness + storage stats; reply carries commands; no heartbeat for 10 min 30 s → dead → re-replication. Block report = list of all blocks on a DataNode, at start-up and every 6 h (plus incremental reports); used to build the block map.

Don't lower heartbeat and dead-node intervals on real clusters just to “detect failures faster”. Short timers turn GC pauses and network blips into false deaths, which trigger mass re-replication and can overload the cluster. The lab uses short values only so you can watch.

5 See it happen

From live to dead and back

The real readings from step 3 and step 4, alongside what the NameNode is thinking.

datanode-4 goes silent 6 steps
What you see
  1. Normal state: datanode-4 checks in every 3 seconds and holds 8 blocks.
  2. The container is frozen. “Last contact” starts growing, but the node is still listed as live.
  3. After 30 s of silence it is counted as stale: still live, but suspicious.
  4. At about 60 s (lab settings) it is declared dead and drops out of the live count.
  5. Unfrozen, it heartbeats again, is told to re-register, and sends a full block report.
  6. Live again with all 8 blocks known. Block reports can also be triggered by hand.
Behind the screen
  1. While heartbeats arrive, the node is LIVE. Each heartbeat is also the NameNode's chance to send it work.
  2. The NameNode only measures time since the last heartbeat; it never pings the DataNode.
  3. After 30 s the node is STALE. Clients can be steered away from it if the avoid-stale options are enabled.
  4. After the dead interval it is DEAD: its replicas no longer count, and re-replication starts.
  5. When it speaks again, the NameNode asks it to register again, and its block report restores its replicas to the map.
  6. Back to LIVE. Block reports keep the map correct: full ones at start-up and every 6 hours, small incremental ones in between.

6 Real-world use

Heartbeats and reports at work

1. Alerts on dead and stale nodes

Monitoring reads NumDeadDataNodes and NumStaleDataNodes from the NameNode's /jmx. One dead node is routine; several at once usually means a rack or network problem worth waking someone up for.

/jmx→Dead/stale counts→Alert

2. Diagnosing flapping DataNodes

A DataNode that keeps going stale and coming back often has long Java garbage-collection pauses, a hung disk or network drops. Engineers compare its “last contact” history with GC and kernel logs.

Stale again and again→GC / disk / network→Fix the node

3. Block report storms

After a full cluster restart, thousands of DataNodes send full block reports at once. Big clusters tune report leases and splitting so the NameNode isn't overwhelmed, which shortens safe mode (Lesson 5.6).

Restart→Reports in waves→NameNode stays responsive

4. After disk repairs

When a failed disk is replaced or a data folder restored, admins run dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport so the NameNode learns the node's real contents now instead of in up to 6 hours.

Repair→Trigger report→Block map correct

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.

7 Run the code

The NameNode's view of a DataNode

This function classifies a DataNode from the seconds since its last heartbeat, using the real default thresholds. Run it for the readings from the lab, then try the challenges.

HEARTBEAT = 3            # dfs.heartbeat.interval (s)
STALE = 30               # dfs.namenode.stale.datanode.interval (s)
RECHECK = 300            # dfs.namenode.heartbeat.recheck-interval (s)
DEAD = 2 * RECHECK + 10 * HEARTBEAT

def state(last_contact):
    if last_contact >= DEAD:
        return "DEAD"
    if last_contact >= STALE:
        return "STALE"
    return "LIVE"

print("dead after", DEAD, "seconds")
for seconds in [2, 27, 43, 300, 629, 630, 900]:
    print(f"last contact {seconds:>3}s -> {state(seconds)}")
dead after 630 seconds last contact 2s -> LIVE last contact 27s -> LIVE last contact 43s -> STALE last contact 300s -> STALE last contact 629s -> STALE last contact 630s -> DEAD last contact 900s -> DEAD
  • DEAD = 2 * RECHECK + 10 * HEARTBEATThe famous 10 min 30 s: 2 × 300 s + 10 × 3 s = 630 s.
  • if last_contact >= STALE:Stale nodes are still live; they can be avoided for reads and writes if the avoid-stale options are on.

Run it on your PC: watch a DataNode die and come back

This script freezes datanode-4 with docker pause (no heartbeats, but the process stays), asks the NameNode about it every 8 seconds for 80 seconds, then unfreezes it. Save it as heartbeat_watch.py in the hdfs-lab folder and run it while the lab cluster is up.

Live → dead → live, from the NameNode's side

# heartbeat_watch.py: freeze one DataNode and watch the NameNode's view of it
# change from live to stale to dead. Run in the hdfs-lab folder.
import json
import subprocess
import time

DN = "lab5-datanode-4"
URL = "http://namenode:9870/jmx?qry=Hadoop:service=NameNode,name=NameNodeInfo"

def sh(*cmd):
    return subprocess.run(cmd, capture_output=True, text=True).stdout

def nn_view(host):
    info = json.loads(sh("docker", "compose", "exec", "-T", "namenode", "curl", "-s", URL))["beans"][0]
    for state in ("LiveNodes", "DeadNodes"):
        for name, node in json.loads(info[state]).items():
            if name.startswith(host):
                return state[:-5].lower(), node["lastContact"]
    return "unknown", None

host = sh("docker", "inspect", "-f", "{{.Config.Hostname}}", DN).strip()
sh("docker", "pause", DN)                      # the process freezes: no more heartbeats
start = time.time()
try:
    while time.time() - start < 80:
        time.sleep(8)
        state, last = nn_view(host)
        print(f"t={time.time() - start:3.0f}s  NameNode says: {state:5} lastContact={last}s")
finally:
    sh("docker", "unpause", DN)
time.sleep(6)
print("after unpause:", nn_view(host))

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 5.5 in one screen

  • Heartbeat: DataNode → NameNode every 3 s: alive + capacity/load. Reply carries commands.
  • The NameNode never calls DataNodes; it only measures silence.
  • Stale after 30 s; dead after 2 × recheck (5 min) + 10 × 3 s = 10 min 30 s.
  • Full block report: all replicas, at registration and every 6 h. Incremental: changes in between.
  • Block reports build the block map (not saved in the fsimage).
  • Commands: transfer (replicate), invalidate (delete), recover, re-register.
  • Check: “Last contact”, “Last Block Report” in dfsadmin -report; /jmx LiveNodes/DeadNodes.
  • hdfs dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport host:9867 forces a report.

Interview and viva questions

What is a heartbeat in Hadoop?

A small periodic message (every 3 seconds by default) from each DataNode to the NameNode saying it is alive, with its storage capacity, usage and load. The NameNode uses the reply to send the DataNode commands.

What happens if the NameNode stops receiving heartbeats from a DataNode?

After 30 seconds the node is marked stale; after 10 minutes 30 seconds (default) it is marked dead. Its replicas are then removed from the block map and the affected blocks are re-replicated from other copies.

What is a block report?

A list of all the block replicas stored on a DataNode, sent when it registers and then every 6 hours by default. Incremental block reports announce new and deleted replicas in between. The NameNode uses them to know where each block is.

Why does a DataNode need to send block reports if the NameNode chose where blocks go?

Because reality can differ: disks fail, files are corrupted or deleted, nodes return with old replicas, and block locations are not stored in the fsimage. Reports are the source of truth for which replicas exist.

How is the dead-node timeout calculated?

2 × dfs.namenode.heartbeat.recheck-interval + 10 × dfs.heartbeat.interval. With defaults: 2 × 300 s + 10 × 3 s = 630 s.

Can the NameNode contact a DataNode directly?

No. In the DataNode protocol the DataNode always initiates the RPC (heartbeats, reports); the NameNode responds, putting any commands in its replies.

The NameNode UI shows one node dead several times a day, each time followed by re-replication and then excess deletions.

The interviewer is checking: systematic debugging.

Show a strong answer
  • Correlate dead times with the DataNode's GC log (long pauses), kernel/disk logs (hung I/O), and network errors.
  • Common fixes: right-size the DataNode heap and GC settings, replace the failing disk, fix the NIC/switch port.
  • Meanwhile, consider decommissioning the node so it stops causing re-replication storms.
Design · protocolExperienced

Why do DataNodes call the NameNode, not the other way round?

A system design follow-up question.

The interviewer is checking: scalability reasoning.

Show a strong answer
  • With thousands of DataNodes, the NameNode would need many outgoing connections and timeouts to poll them; instead each node pushes a tiny message on a schedule.
  • Failure detection becomes trivial: silence. No connection management for dead nodes.
  • Commands piggyback on replies, so there is no extra round trip.
  • The same push model is used by YARN NodeManagers to the ResourceManager.
Operations · repairFresher

After replacing a disk, the NameNode still shows old block counts

A disk on a DataNode was replaced and its data restored from another copy, but the NameNode's numbers look out of date.

The interviewer is checking: block report timing.

Show a strong answer
  • The next full block report may be hours away (every 6 h).
  • Run hdfs dfsadmin -triggerBlockReport host:9867, then check “Last Block Report” and the block counts in dfsadmin -report.
  • Verify with hdfs fsck / that no blocks are missing or corrupt.
