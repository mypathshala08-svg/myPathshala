2 Definition

Hadoop security: Kerberos and Ranger

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Hadoop security rests on four pillars: authentication (proving identity), authorization (deciding what an identity may do), audit (recording who did what) and data protection (encryption on the wire and at rest). By default Hadoop uses simple authentication, in which the client's claimed user name is trusted. In secure mode (hadoop.security.authentication=kerberos) every user and service proves its identity to a Kerberos Key Distribution Center (KDC): a principal obtains a ticket-granting ticket (TGT) from the Authentication Service with a password or a keytab, then obtains service tickets from the Ticket-Granting Service for each Hadoop service, such as nn/host@REALM; jobs then use Hadoop delegation tokens. Apache Ranger provides centralized, fine-grained authorization: administrators define resource-based or tag-based policies in Ranger Admin, plugins inside services such as the NameNode, HiveServer2, HBase and YARN enforce them locally, and every access decision is written to an audit store.

In simple words

Kerberos is the ID card: no card, no entry, and you cannot borrow someone else's name. Ranger is the rule book and the CCTV: one place says who may open which door, every building follows it, and every opening is recorded.

Security at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner The office building

  • Authentication is the ID card at the gate. Without Kerberos, the guard asks “what's your name?” and believes the answer: that is what HADOOP_USER_NAME exploited.
  • Authorization is which rooms your card opens: HDFS permissions, ACLs and Ranger policies.
  • Audit is the visitor register and CCTV. Encryption is locking the files themselves, so a stolen disk is useless.

Intermediate How a Kerberos login works in Hadoop

  • kinit riya sends an AS_REQ; the KDC returns a TGT encrypted with a key derived from riya's password. Only the right password can open it, so the password never crosses the network. A wrong one gives Password incorrect.
  • The first hdfs dfs command uses the TGT to send a TGS_REQ for nn/nn-secure. Our KDC log shows 4 of them for riya's commands; Java keeps those service tickets in memory, which is why klist showed only the TGT.
  • The NameNode decrypts the ticket with its own key from the keytab, learns “this is riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA”, maps it to the short name riya (auth_to_local) and then checks permissions as before.
  • A MapReduce or Spark job obtains delegation tokens at submission and ships them to its containers, so thousands of tasks don't each hit the KDC.

Advanced Running it in production

  • Every daemon gets its own principal and keytab (nn/host, dn/host, rm/host, nm/host, HTTP/host for SPNEGO web UIs); keytabs are root-only files.
  • Secure DataNodes need privileged ports or SASL data-transfer protection with HTTPS; that is why our secure lab ran only a NameNode and created empty files.
  • Kerberos needs DNS (forward and reverse) and clocks within 5 minutes of each other; most “GSS initiate failed” incidents are clock skew, DNS or an expired keytab (wrong KVNO).
  • Companies usually use Active Directory as the KDC, run Ranger for policies and audit, encrypt RPC (hadoop.rpc.protection=privacy) and data transfer, and use HDFS encryption zones with a KMS for sensitive data. Apache Knox can give a single HTTPS entry point.

Exam tip: For “Explain Kerberos authentication in Hadoop”, give the three exchanges (AS: TGT; TGS: service ticket; client to service: ticket), name the keytab and the principal format service/host@REALM, and say why simple authentication is unsafe (the client's user name is trusted).

HDFS ACL or Ranger policy?

HDFS permissions + ACLs

Stored on each file or folder in the NameNode. Good for a few exceptions (“kabir may also read /finance”). Spread across thousands of paths, hard to review, HDFS only.

built in

Ranger resource policy

“Group finance-analysts: read on /finance/*, select on db finance”. Defined once in Ranger Admin, cached by plugins, audited centrally, works across HDFS, Hive, HBase, Kafka.

central

Ranger tag policy

Columns tagged PII in Apache Atlas are masked for everyone except approved groups, wherever the data is copied.

by classification

Ranger masking / row filter

Hive shows XXXX-XXXX-1234 instead of a card number, or only the rows of the analyst's own state.

Kerberos, Ranger and audit together 6 steps
Animated example
  1. Pretending: the intern claims to be hr_admin. On a Kerberos cluster that is ignored: no ticket, no connection.
  2. Logging in: with kinit she proves who she really is and gets a TGT.
  3. Asking: she queries the salary table; HiveServer2 knows exactly who is asking.
  4. Ranger decides: no policy lets the interns group read hr tables, so the plugin denies it.
  5. Audit: the denial is recorded with user, resource, time and client address for the security team.
  6. The fix: instead of more exceptions, a masking policy gives interns the table with the salary column hidden.

Pick the lightest tool that works: owner/group permissions for most paths, an ACL for a rare exception, and Ranger once you have many users, several services, or auditors asking “who could read this column last March?”.

4 Step by step

From “anyone can be hadoop” to tickets and ACLs

Part 1 runs on the Module 8 lab. Part 2 uses three new containers (kerberos/docker-compose.yaml in “Run it on your PC”): an Ubuntu KDC, a Kerberos-secured NameNode and a client.

  1. Unsecured: claim to be the owner

    HADOOP_USER_NAME=riya   hdfs dfs -cat /secure_demo/salaries.csv
HADOOP_USER_NAME=hadoop hdfs dfs -rm -r -skipTrash /secure_demo
    Permission denied: user=riya, access=EXECUTE, inode="/secure_demo":hadoop:supergroup:drwx------ Deleted /secure_demo
    With hadoop.security.authentication=simple the client simply sends a user name, taken from the OS or from HADOOP_USER_NAME. HDFS checks permissions against that name faithfully, which protects against mistakes, never against intent.

  2. Build a KDC and secure the NameNode

    kdb5_util create -s -r LAB.MYPHATSHALA
kadmin.local -q "addprinc -randkey nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA"
kadmin.local -q "addprinc -pw riya123 riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA"
kadmin.local -q "ktadd -k /keytabs/nn.keytab nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA HTTP/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA"
CORE-SITE.XML_hadoop.security.authentication=kerberos
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.namenode.kerberos.principal=nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.namenode.keytab.file=/keytabs/nn.keytab
    INFO UserGroupInformation - Login successful for user nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA using keytab file nn.keytab INFO Server - IPC Server listener on 8020: starting
    Our first start failed for real: the image formats the NameNode when the container starts, before the KDC had written the keytab, giving KerberosAuthException: failure to login … Unable to obtain password from user. The fix was to wait until kinit -kt works, then format and start.

  3. No ticket, no entry

    hdfs dfs -ls /
HADOOP_USER_NAME=nn hdfs dfs -ls /
    AccessControlException Client cannot authenticate via:[TOKEN, KERBEROS] Client cannot authenticate via:[TOKEN, KERBEROS]
    The client offered neither a Kerberos ticket nor a delegation token, so the NameNode refused the connection before any file was checked. The environment variable is ignored in secure mode.

  4. Log in as riya and look at the ticket

    kinit riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA
klist
hdfs dfs -touchz /user/riya/notes.txt
hdfs dfs -ls /user/riya
    Default principal: riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA Valid starting Expires Service principal 10/22/2026 10:08:23 10/22/2026 20:08:23 krbtgt/LAB.MYPHATSHALA@LAB.MYPHATSHALA -rw-r--r-- riya /user/riya/notes.txt
    The TGT lasts 10 hours (ticket_lifetime = 10h in krb5.conf). Hadoop's Java client then asks the KDC for a service ticket for nn/nn-secure and keeps it in memory. We used touchz because the secure lab has no DataNode: secure DataNodes need privileged ports or SASL with HTTPS.

  5. Try the payroll folder, and try to pretend

    hdfs dfs -cat /payroll/salaries.csv
HADOOP_USER_NAME=nn hdfs dfs -cat /payroll/salaries.csv
kdestroy; kinit riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA      # with a wrong password
    Permission denied: user=riya, access=EXECUTE, inode="/payroll":nn:supergroup:drwx------ Permission denied: user=riya, access=EXECUTE, inode="/payroll":nn:supergroup:drwx------ kinit: Password incorrect while getting initial credentials
    Now the user name comes from the ticket, so both attempts are riya. Note the owner of /payroll is nn: the short name of nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA after the default auth_to_local rule.

  6. Read the KDC's log

    docker logs lab12k-kdc-1 | grep -E "AS_REQ|TGS_REQ"
    1 AS_REQ 172.23.0.3: ISSUE: riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA for krbtgt/LAB.MYPHATSHALA@LAB.MYPHATSHALA 4 TGS_REQ 172.23.0.3: ISSUE: riya@LAB.MYPHATSHALA for nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA 1 AS_REQ 172.23.0.3: ISSUE: nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA for krbtgt/LAB.MYPHATSHALA@LAB.MYPHATSHALA 4 TGS_REQ 172.23.0.3: ISSUE: nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA for nn/nn-secure@LAB.MYPHATSHALA
    Exactly the two exchanges from the definition: one AS request for a TGT, then TGS requests for the NameNode's service ticket (one per hdfs command, since each is a new JVM). The KDC never sees a password; it only encrypts with the key derived from it.

  7. Authorization beyond owner and group: an ACL

    hdfs dfs -chmod 750 /finance
hdfs dfs -setfacl -m user:kabir:r-x /finance
hdfs dfs -getfacl /finance
    user::rwx user:kabir:r-x group::r-x mask::r-x other::--- -- kabir (named in the ACL): 1 file visible -- riya (not in the ACL): Permission denied: user=riya drwxr-x---+ hadoop /finance
    The + after the permission bits means “has an ACL”. Ranger generalises this idea: policies by user or group, kept in one place, across services, with audit. (This step ran on the unsecured lab, so the names are only as trustworthy as step 1.)

Keytabs are as powerful as passwords that never expire. Keep them readable only by the service user, never in Git, and rotate them; a copied keytab lets anyone become that service.

Tickets, not names 6 steps
What you see
  1. Without a ticket the very first command is refused.
  2. Claiming to be the NameNode's user changes nothing.
  3. riya logs in with her password; klist shows her TGT, valid for 10 hours.
  4. Now commands work, and the file she creates is owned by riya.
  5. She tries to pretend again to read payroll: still riya, still denied.
  6. A wrong password never gets a usable ticket.
Behind the screen
  1. The NameNode's RPC layer demands Kerberos or a delegation token during the connection handshake.
  2. The user name variable is simply not used in secure mode; identity comes only from credentials.
  3. kinit sends an AS request; the KDC returns a TGT that only riya's password can unlock.
  4. For each command the client asks the TGS for an nn/nn-secure ticket; the NameNode reads riya from it.
  5. Authentication done, the usual HDFS permission check denies riya on /payroll.
  6. With a wrong password the client cannot decrypt the KDC's reply, so it never gets a TGT.
6 Real-world use

Security at work

1. Banking: masking card numbers

Analysts query transaction tables in Hive; a Ranger masking policy shows only the last four digits of card numbers to everyone outside the fraud team, and every query is audited.

Analyst query→Ranger mask policy→Masked result + audit

2. Hospitals: tag-based policies on patient data

Columns such as patient name and diagnosis are tagged PII in Atlas; one Ranger tag policy protects them in every table and copy, so researchers get de-identified data.

Atlas tags→Ranger tag policy→De-identified views

3. Telecom: one login for thousands of employees

The corporate Active Directory acts as the KDC; employees' Windows logins give them Kerberos tickets, and leaving the company disables cluster access the same day.

Employee login→AD Kerberos→Cluster access

4. Government schemes: encryption zones for beneficiary data

Folders holding beneficiary records are HDFS encryption zones; files are encrypted with keys held by the KMS, so a disk taken out for repair reveals nothing.

Beneficiary files→Encryption zone + KMS→Encrypted blocks

5. E-commerce: audit trails for compliance reviews

Ranger audit records who read customer address tables; a monthly report flags unusual access, for example a service account suddenly reading data it never read before.

Audit store→Monthly review→Alerts

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 adds legal duties for personal data; ask your legal team what applies, this lesson is not legal advice.

7 Run the code

Simulate a Kerberos login

A toy Kerberos with real ideas and fake cryptography: each principal has a secret key, tickets are “sealed” with the key of whoever must read them, and every ticket has an expiry time. Watch how riya gets into the NameNode and why an impostor cannot.

import hashlib
def key(secret):                                  # a "key" derived from a password or keytab
    return hashlib.sha256(secret.encode()).hexdigest()[:8]
def seal(k, data):  return {"sealed_with": k, "data": data}
def open_(k, box):
    if box["sealed_with"] != k:
        raise PermissionError("cannot decrypt: wrong key")
    return box["data"]

KDC = {"riya@LAB": key("riya123"), "krbtgt@LAB": key("kdc-secret"), "nn/nn-secure@LAB": key("from-keytab")}

def as_req(user, now):                           # 1. AS: password -> TGT
    tgt = seal(KDC["krbtgt@LAB"], {"user": user, "expires": now + 10})
    return seal(KDC[user], {"tgt": tgt})          # only the user's key opens the reply

def tgs_req(tgt, service, now):                   # 2. TGS: TGT -> service ticket
    t = open_(KDC["krbtgt@LAB"], tgt)
    if now > t["expires"]:
        raise PermissionError("TGT expired")
    return seal(KDC[service], {"user": t["user"], "expires": t["expires"]})

def namenode(ticket, claimed_name=None):          # 3. service: reads the user from the ticket
    t = open_(KDC["nn/nn-secure@LAB"], ticket)
    return f"NameNode sees user={t['user'].split('@')[0]} (claimed {claimed_name})"

reply = as_req("riya@LAB", now=0)
tgt = open_(key("riya123"), reply)["tgt"]         # riya types the right password
ticket = tgs_req(tgt, "nn/nn-secure@LAB", now=1)
print(namenode(ticket, claimed_name="nn"))
try:
    open_(key("wrongpass"), reply)
except PermissionError as e:
    print("wrong password:", e)
NameNode sees user=riya (claimed nn) wrong password: cannot decrypt: wrong key
  • return seal(KDC[user], {"tgt": tgt}) # only the user's key opens the replyThe KDC answers anyone, but the answer is sealed with the user's key. That is why a wrong password gave "Password incorrect" without the password crossing the network.
  • return seal(KDC[service], {"user": t["user"], "expires": t["expires"]})A service ticket is sealed with the service's key, which the NameNode has in its keytab.
  • t = open_(KDC["nn/nn-secure@LAB"], ticket)The NameNode takes the user from the ticket. The claimed name is ignored, as HADOOP_USER_NAME was in our lab.

Quick revision

Lesson 12.5 in one screen

  • Four pillars: authentication (Kerberos), authorization (permissions, ACLs, Ranger), audit, encryption (wire and at rest).
  • Simple auth trusts the client's user name: in our lab HADOOP_USER_NAME=hadoop deleted a 700 folder.
  • Kerberos: AS_REQ → TGT (kinit), TGS_REQ → service ticket (nn/host@REALM), service decrypts with its keytab.
  • Lab: no ticket → “Client cannot authenticate via:[TOKEN, KERBEROS]”; riya's ticket → files owned by riya; pretending does nothing.
  • auth_to_local maps principals to short names; jobs use delegation tokens, renewed by the RM.
  • ACL: hdfs dfs -setfacl -m user:kabir:r-x; Ranger: central resource/tag policies, masking, row filters, audit.
  • Production: AD as KDC, keytabs locked down, clocks synced (5 min skew), RPC privacy, encryption zones + KMS, Knox.

Interview and viva questions

Why is Kerberos needed in Hadoop?

Without it Hadoop uses simple authentication: the client's user name (from the OS or HADOOP_USER_NAME) is trusted, so anyone with network access can act as any user, including the HDFS superuser. Kerberos makes every user and service prove its identity with tickets from a KDC, so permissions, ACLs and Ranger policies apply to the real person.

How does Kerberos authentication work in Hadoop?

The user runs kinit and the KDC's Authentication Service returns a ticket-granting ticket sealed with a key derived from the password. The client then asks the Ticket-Granting Service for a service ticket for a Hadoop service such as nn/host@REALM, and presents it to that service, which decrypts it with the key in its keytab. Jobs then use Hadoop delegation tokens so tasks do not contact the KDC.

What is Apache Ranger?

A central authorization and audit framework for the Hadoop ecosystem. Administrators define policies in Ranger Admin (by user, group, resource or Atlas tag); plugins inside HDFS, Hive, HBase, YARN, Kafka and others download the policies, enforce them locally and send audit events to Solr or HDFS. Ranger also supports column masking and row filtering for Hive.

What is the difference between HDFS ACLs and Ranger?

ACLs are stored on each file or directory in the NameNode and only cover HDFS. Ranger policies are defined centrally, can cover many paths and services with one policy, support groups from LDAP/AD, tags, masking and row filters, and keep a searchable audit of every decision.

What is a keytab and why is it sensitive?

A keytab stores a principal's long-term keys so a service (or a scheduled job) can log in to Kerberos without typing a password. Anyone who can read it can act as that principal until the keys are changed, so keytabs must be readable only by the service user, kept out of source control and rotated.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Security questions come up in Hadoop admin, data engineering and platform interviews, usually with a story.

Concepts · KerberosFresher

“Explain Kerberos in Hadoop in two minutes”

A standard opening question.

The interviewer is checking: the three exchanges and why Hadoop needs it.

Show a strong answer
  • Without Kerberos, Hadoop trusts the user name the client sends; anyone can be the superuser.
  • kinit → TGT from the AS; TGT → service ticket for nn/host@REALM from the TGS; the NameNode decrypts it with its keytab.
  • Jobs get delegation tokens at submission so tasks don't hit the KDC; the RM renews them.
Troubleshooting · GSSExperienced

All jobs on one worker fail with “GSS initiate failed”

Other workers are fine; nothing was deployed.

The interviewer is checking: a methodical Kerberos checklist.

Show a strong answer
  • Check the clock (more than 5 minutes of skew), forward and reverse DNS of the host, and that the keytab's KVNO matches the KDC (klist -kt).
  • Try kinit -kt with the service keytab on that host; enable sun.security.krb5.debug=true for the details.
  • Check krb5.conf (realm, KDC address, allowed encryption types) against the working workers.
Design · RangerExperienced

Analysts need customer data, but not phone numbers

An e-commerce company must restrict PII without blocking reports.

The interviewer is checking: masking, tags and audit, not more copies.

Show a strong answer
  • Tag PII columns in Atlas; one Ranger tag policy masks them (hash or last 4 digits) for analysts, clear for an approved group.
  • Row-level filters if analysts should see only their region.
  • Keep HDFS paths of the raw tables closed to analysts so they cannot bypass Hive; review Ranger audit monthly.
Basics · permissionsFresher

“Our folder is 700, so it's secure, right?”

A teammate on an unsecured cluster.

The interviewer is checking: that you know permissions need authentication.

Show a strong answer
  • Not on simple auth: anyone can set HADOOP_USER_NAME to the owner's name, as our lab showed with a delete.
  • Permissions protect against mistakes; against people you need Kerberos (and a firewall around the cluster).
  • Then add Ranger for central policies and audit, and encryption for data at rest.
