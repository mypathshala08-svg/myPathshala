Cluster sizing with real numbers

Sizing is arithmetic with a few honest assumptions. This lesson walks through the whole calculation for a payments company, from terabytes per day to the number of servers, NameNode heap and containers, and measures our own lab with TestDFSIO so you can see where real throughput numbers come from and why a laptop's numbers must not be copied into a production plan.

1 The story

“How many servers should we buy?”

Kabir's internship has moved to a payments startup. Its UPI switch writes about 2 TB of transaction logs a day, the regulator wants three years kept, and the fraud reports must be ready by 6 AM. The CFO wants one number by Friday: how many servers. Kabir's first guess comes from the lab: “Our three containers wrote 210 MB/s, so…” Riya stops him.

Riya

Kabir, those 210 MB/s came from three containers sharing one laptop disk. A real server has twelve disks and its own network card. You can't multiply a laptop.

Kabir

Then where do I even start?

Arjun Sir

From the data, not from the hardware. How much arrives each day, how small it gets on disk, how long you keep it, how many copies. That gives the storage, and storage usually decides the server count. Then you check three things against it: can the nightly work finish in its window, can the NameNode hold all the file and block objects, and does the network keep up. Today we do the whole calculation and also benchmark our own lab properly, so you know what a benchmark number really means.

2 Definition

Cluster sizing

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Cluster sizing (capacity planning) is the process of estimating the hardware a cluster needs to meet its storage, processing, availability and growth requirements. For Hadoop it combines: storage = data ingested per period × compression ratio × retention × replication factor (or erasure-coding overhead), plus space for temporary and intermediate data and headroom to keep disks below a target fill level; compute = the number of concurrent containers needed so that the workload, expressed in container-seconds, completes within its time window; memory and CPU per node derived from container sizes after reserving resources for the operating system and daemons; NameNode heap estimated from the expected number of files, directories and blocks; network bandwidth for ingest, replication and shuffle; and the master and edge nodes required for high availability and access. Estimates rest on stated assumptions and on benchmarks (such as TestDFSIO) of representative hardware.

In simple words

Work out how much data you will keep and how much work you must finish in how much time, add safety margins, and turn that into servers. Write down every assumption, and measure real hardware before trusting a number.

Sizing at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Building a godown

  • A grain trader asks: how big a godown? You ask how many sacks arrive a day, how long they stay, and whether every sack must be stored three times (copies) in different corners.
  • You never fill a godown to the roof: you need aisles and space for a sudden delivery (headroom and temp space).
  • Then you check the loading crew: can they move tonight's sacks before morning? That is compute against the batch window.

Intermediate The calculation, in order

  • Storage: 2 TB × 0.25 = 0.5 TB/day → × 1,095 = 547.5 TB → × 3 = 1,642.5 TB → × 1.25 ÷ 0.70 × 1.3 = 3,812.9 TB → ÷ 96 = 40 workers.
  • NameNode: 2,048 files of 256 MB a day → 2,242,560 files and 4,485,120 blocks in 3 years → about 0.9 GB of objects at 150 B each. Plan a heap several times larger.
  • Compute: 60 TB scanned nightly at 20 MB/s per container = 3,145,728 container-seconds ÷ 10,800 s = 291 containers, against 40 × 30 = 1,200.
  • Per node: 256 GB − 32 GB reserved = 224 GB for YARN; 30 vcores → about 7.5 GB per vcore (Lesson 11.4).

Advanced What experienced planners add

  • Tiering: keep 90 hot days with 3 copies and older data in erasure coding: 888.8 TB of data and 22 workers instead of 40 (Lesson 7.3).
  • Network: 20 workers × 10 Gb = 200 Gb against 80 Gb of uplink is 2.5:1 oversubscription; replication and shuffle cross racks, so keep it modest.
  • Failure budget: losing one 96 TB node means re-replicating up to 96 TB; headroom and network decide how long that takes.
  • Benchmark, then re-plan: run TestDFSIO and a real job on one candidate server, replace the assumed throughput, and review against actual growth every quarter.

Exam tip: For “How will you size a Hadoop cluster for X TB per day?”, write the storage formula (daily × ratio × retention × replication × temp ÷ fill × growth ÷ disk per node), then one line each for compute (container-seconds ÷ window), NameNode heap (objects × ~150 B) and masters (2 NN, 3 JN, 3 ZK, 2 RM).

Animated example: sizing the payments cluster

From 2 TB a day to 40 servers 6 steps
Animated example
  1. Compress: 2 TB of raw logs a day becomes about 0.5 TB as Parquet with zstd (an assumption based on Lesson 10.2).
  2. Keep and copy: 1,095 days × 0.5 TB = 547.5 TB, three copies = 1,642.5 TB.
  3. Margins: +25% temp, keep disks 70% full, +30% growth = 3,812.9 TB; with 96 TB per server that is 40 workers.
  4. NameNode: 256 MB files give 2.2 million files and 4.5 million blocks: under 1 GB of objects, so the heap is comfortable.
  5. Compute: the nightly 60 TB scan needs 291 containers at once; 40 workers offer 1,200. Storage decides, compute fits.
  6. Tiering: keep 90 hot days with three copies and the rest in erasure coding: 22 workers.

Size from the data, check with benchmarks. A sizing that starts from “we have budget for 20 servers” and works backwards is a budget, not a plan.

4 Step by step

Benchmark first, then size

Run on the Module 8 lab with dfsio_lab.sh (in “Run it on your PC”), then do the arithmetic for the payments example.

  1. Benchmark writes with one task

    yarn org.apache.hadoop.fs.TestDFSIO -libjars $T -write -nrFiles 1 -fileSize 64MB
    Total MBytes processed: 64 Throughput mb/sec: 113.48 Test exec time sec: 17.6
    One map task writes one 64 MB file with three replicas. 113 MB/s is what one writer gets from our laptop's disk, through the DataNode pipeline.

  2. Add writers

    write, 3 files: Throughput mb/sec: 61.82
write, 6 files: Throughput mb/sec: 34.99
    Per task the number drops; the total grows from about 113 to 185 to 210 MB/s and then flattens. Three “workers” sharing one disk can't add disk speed. On real servers, each node adds its own disks and the total keeps growing.

  3. Benchmark reads

    read, 1 file:  Throughput mb/sec: 320
read, 3 files: Throughput mb/sec: 175.34
read, 6 files: Throughput mb/sec: 130.3
    Reads look faster because the files were written seconds earlier and are still in memory (page cache). For an honest read benchmark, use files much larger than RAM or drop the caches first.

  4. Storage for the payments company

    2 TB × 0.25 = 0.5 TB/day × 1,095 days = 547.5 TB
× 3 copies = 1,642.5 TB × 1.25 temp ÷ 0.70 fill × 1.3 growth = 3,812.9 TB
÷ 96 TB per worker (12 × 8 TB) = 39.7 → 40 workers
    Storage decides the cluster size. The compression ratio and disk per node are assumptions; measure the ratio on a sample of your real data (Lesson 10.2 measured about 0.14 for Parquet + zstd on our CSV, so 0.25 is a cautious choice).

  5. Check NameNode heap and compute

    files: 2,048 a day × 1,095 = 2,242,560; blocks: 4,485,120
objects × 150 B ≈ 0.9 GB  (rule of thumb; plan a much larger heap)
nightly work: 60 TB ÷ 20 MB/s = 3,145,728 container-seconds
÷ 10,800 s (3 h) = 291 containers at once; available 40 × 30 = 1,200
    Both fit with room to spare. If either did not, it would add nodes (compute), larger files (heap) or a second namespace (Lesson 7.2).

  6. Masters, edge and network

    masters: 3 servers (2 NN, 2 RM, 3 JN, 3 ZK, JobHistory), one per rack
edge: 2 servers
racks: 45 servers ÷ 20 per rack = 3 racks
oversubscription: 20 × 10 Gb ÷ 80 Gb uplink = 2.5 : 1
    Spreading masters across racks means a single rack failure can't take the NameNode pair or the JournalNode quorum down (Lesson 7.1).

Never copy a benchmark from a laptop, a VM or another company's blog into a purchase order. Run TestDFSIO and one real job on a candidate server first, and treat every typical value in this lesson as a placeholder for your own measurement.

6 Real-world use

Sizing at work

1. Payments: regulation drives retention

Payment companies keep transaction records for years by regulation. Retention × copies decides most of the cluster, so tiering old data to erasure coding or archive storage is the biggest cost lever.

Retention rule→Storage formula→Workers + tiering plan

2. E-commerce: the sale day sets the compute

Clickstream volume can be many times normal during a festival sale. Teams size compute for the peak night's window, and often burst extra capacity in the cloud for those days.

Peak volume→Container-seconds ÷ window→Containers needed

3. Telecom: format is the cheapest saving

Call records compress very well in columnar formats. Converting raw CDR text to ORC or Parquet often reduces the hardware more than any server discount.

Raw CDR text→ORC conversion→Fewer disks

4. Healthcare: file counts, not bytes

Hospitals store millions of small reports and image metadata files. Their sizing worry is NameNode heap, solved by packing files into containers or object storage, not by more disks.

Millions of files→Objects × 150 B→Pack or federate

5. Government: phased buying and growth

Public schemes grow as enrolment grows and data is rarely deleted. Planners model growth year by year and buy hardware in phases, reviewing against real usage before each purchase.

Growth model→Yearly sizing→Phased purchase

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies. Hardware figures in this lesson are assumptions for illustration.

7 Run the code

A cluster sizing calculator

The assumptions are at the top. Change one and see which part of the cluster moves. Storage usually decides the number of workers; compute checks that the nightly work fits the window.

import math
# --- assumptions (change these) ---
RAW_TB_DAY, RATIO, DAYS = 2.0, 0.25, 3 * 365     # raw data per day, Parquet+zstd size ratio, retention
REPL, TEMP, FILL, GROWTH = 3, 1.25, 0.70, 1.3     # replication, +25% temp/shuffle, keep disks 70% full, +30%
DISK_TB_PER_NODE = 12 * 8                         # 12 disks of 8 TB
NIGHTLY_SCAN_TB, MB_S_PER_CONTAINER, WINDOW_H = 60, 20, 3

stored = RAW_TB_DAY * RATIO * DAYS                # TB kept, one copy
data = stored * REPL
disk = data * TEMP / FILL * GROWTH
workers = math.ceil(disk / DISK_TB_PER_NODE)
print(f"one copy {stored:.1f} TB -> x{REPL} = {data:.1f} TB -> disk to buy {disk:.1f} TB -> {workers} workers")

files = RAW_TB_DAY * RATIO * 1024 * 1024 / 256 * DAYS   # 256 MB files
blocks = files * 2                                      # 128 MB blocks
print(f"{files:,.0f} files, {blocks:,.0f} blocks -> about {(files + blocks) * 150 / 2**30:.1f} GB of NameNode heap")

containers = NIGHTLY_SCAN_TB * 1024 * 1024 / MB_S_PER_CONTAINER / (WINDOW_H * 3600)
print(f"nightly work needs {containers:.0f} containers at once; {workers} workers x 30 = {workers * 30}")

Quick revision

Lesson 12.3 in one screen

  • Storage: raw/day × compression ratio × retention days × copies (3, or about 1.5 with EC) × 1.25 temp ÷ 0.70 fill × growth.
  • Workers = disk needed ÷ disk per node, rounded up. Example: 2 TB/day, 3 years → 3,812.9 TB → 40 workers (22 with cold data on RS-6-3).

Interview and viva questions

How do you calculate the size of a Hadoop cluster?

Start from storage: daily data volume × compression ratio × retention period × replication factor (3, or about 1.5 with erasure coding), add 20–30% for temporary and shuffle data, divide by the target disk fill (around 70%) and add growth. Divide by usable disk per worker to get the number of workers. Then check compute against the batch window, size the NameNode heap from the expected number of files and blocks, and add masters and edge nodes.

How much memory does a NameNode need?

Roughly 150 bytes per file, directory and block object as a rule of thumb, often summarised as about 1 GB of heap per million blocks. The real driver is the number of objects, so a cluster full of small files needs far more heap than one with the same bytes in large files. Leave plenty of headroom for growth and garbage collection.

What is TestDFSIO?

A benchmark in the Hadoop MapReduce tests jar. Each map task writes or reads one file in HDFS, and the job reports throughput per task and average I/O rate. It measures the storage path end to end (client, DataNodes, disks, network) and is used before and after hardware or configuration changes.

Why keep HDFS disks below 70–80% full?

Jobs need temporary space for map output, staging and new data; the balancer needs room to move blocks; when a node or disk fails, its replicas are re-created on the others; and nearly full disks make DataNodes and NodeManagers unhealthy. Headroom is what keeps a failure from turning into an outage.

Is it cheaper to run Hadoop in the cloud?

It depends on utilisation. Steady, always-busy clusters are often cheaper on owned or reserved hardware; spiky or experimental workloads benefit from cloud clusters that are created for a job and deleted, with data kept in object storage. Compare the full cost: servers, power, space and staff on one side; compute hours, storage, data transfer and support on the other.

print(f"break-even at {own_per_day / cloud_per_hour:.1f} cluster-hours a day")
break-even at 0.9 cluster-hours a day

How it works: About 0.9 hours a day with these made-up prices. Plug in real quotes before any decision; the shape of the calculation is what matters.

A sizing function

Write size(raw_tb_day, ratio, days, copies) returning the number of 96 TB workers (25% temp, 70% fill, 30% growth). Check it against the UPI example.

Input
2.0, 0.25, 1095, 3
Expected output
40 20
Show solution and explanation 
import math
def size(raw_tb_day, ratio, days, copies, node_tb=96):
    disk = raw_tb_day * ratio * days * copies * 1.25 / 0.70 * 1.3
    return math.ceil(disk / node_tb)
print(size(2.0, 0.25, 1095, 3), size(2.0, 0.25, 1095, 1.5))
40 20

How it works: 40 and 20. The 22 in the lesson keeps the last 90 days with 3 copies; putting everything on erasure coding would give 20.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Sizing questions are common for experienced roles. Interviewers look for a method and stated assumptions, not a memorised number.

Sizing · methodExperienced

“We ingest 5 TB a day and keep it 2 years. How many nodes?”

The classic open question.

The interviewer is checking: a method with assumptions out loud.

Show a strong answer
  • Ask: format and compression, replication or EC, growth, node hardware.
  • Storage: 5 × ratio × 730 × 3 × 1.25 ÷ 0.7 × growth; divide by usable disk per node.
  • Then check compute against the processing window and NameNode heap against file counts.
NameNode · capacityExperienced

The NameNode needs a restart every few weeks: heap is full

Storage is only 40% used.

The interviewer is checking: objects vs bytes.

Show a strong answer
  • Heap tracks files and blocks, so check FilesTotal and BlocksTotal and the average file size per folder.
  • Fix the writers producing small files; compact old data (Lesson 7.5).
  • Short term: raise heap with GC tuning; long term: federation if one namespace is too big (Lesson 7.2).
Benchmarks · honestyFresher

“Our test cluster writes 200 MB/s. Is that good?”

A trainee ran TestDFSIO on three virtual machines.

The interviewer is checking: understanding what was measured.

Show a strong answer
  • Ask what is shared: if all VMs use one physical disk, adding writers won't add throughput.
  • Look at per-task vs total, and whether reads came from page cache.
  • Benchmark the real hardware with realistic file sizes and concurrency.
Planning · growthExperienced

Finance asks for a 3-year hardware budget

Data grows 40% a year and nobody deletes anything.

The interviewer is checking: retention and tiering as levers.

Show a strong answer
  • Model year by year with compounding growth; buy in phases, not all at once.
  • Propose levers: retention policies, better formats, erasure coding or archive storage for cold data.
  • Present a range with assumptions, and review it every quarter against real usage.
