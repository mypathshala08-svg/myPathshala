Failures are normal

On your laptop a dead disk is a disaster. In a cluster of thousands of disks it is a normal Tuesday. This lesson does the arithmetic of how often hardware fails at scale, walks through every kind of failure a Hadoop cluster meets, and shows the mechanisms that make them harmless: three copies on two racks, heartbeats, automatic re-replication, checksums and task retries. Then you get to break the playground cluster on purpose.

2 Definition

Failures are normal: fault tolerance

Formal definition (write this in exams)

In a large cluster of commodity hardware, component failures (disks, nodes, network links, racks) are expected events rather than exceptions, because the probability of at least one failure grows with the number of components. Fault tolerance is the ability of a system to continue operating correctly when some of its components fail. Hadoop achieves it through data replication across nodes and racks, failure detection using heartbeats and timeouts, automatic re-replication and task re-execution, checksums to detect corrupted data, and high-availability masters.

In simple words

With thousands of parts, something is always breaking. So instead of buying perfect machines, Hadoop keeps several copies of everything, notices quickly when something dies, and automatically re-copies data and re-runs work somewhere else. Users usually never notice.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

Fault toleranceKeep working correctly even when some parts fail.

MTBFMean time between failures: the average time a device runs before it fails.

Re-replicationMaking new copies when some are lost, until every block is back to its replication factor.
ChecksumA small number computed from data. If the data changes, the checksum no longer matches, so corruption is detected.
Failure domainA group of parts that can fail together: a disk, a node, a rack, a data centre.
StragglerA node that is alive but very slow. Can hold up a whole job.
Retry / re-executionRunning a failed task again, usually on another node.

Typical numbers (approximate)

Failures at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner The tiffin delivery team

  • A delivery boy's bike gets a puncture every few weeks. With 500 delivery boys, several punctures happen every day.
  • The company doesn't buy puncture-proof bikes (too expensive). It keeps spare boys nearby, so a tiffin is handed over and still arrives.
  • The office knows where every boy is (heartbeat from the app). If one goes silent, his tiffins are reassigned.
  • That is Hadoop: cheap parts, spares, tracking and automatic reassignment.

Intermediate The arithmetic of “normal”

  • Expected failures per year = number of devices × AFR. 3,000 disks × 1.5% ≈ 45 a year, almost one a week.
  • Chance of at least one failure among n devices = 1 − (1 − p)n. It climbs quickly as n grows.
  • A long job is more likely to see a failure than a short one: a 6-hour job on 500 nodes will often see a task fail.
  • With 3 replicas, losing data needs all three copies to die before re-replication finishes. That is very unlikely if copies are on independent nodes and racks.

Advanced Failures that are harder than “a node dies”

  • Correlated failures: a rack's power strip or switch takes 20–40 nodes down at once. That's why HDFS puts replicas on at least two racks.
  • Silent data corruption: a disk returns wrong bytes without an error. HDFS stores a checksum with every 512 bytes; a mismatch marks that replica corrupt, and a good copy is used and re-replicated.
  • Gray failures / stragglers: a node is “alive” but very slow (failing disk, overheating). Speculative execution runs a backup copy of its slow task.
  • Network partitions: nodes are alive but can't reach the master. The master must decide whether to treat them as dead, which is why timeouts are not too short.
  • Master failure and split-brain: with two NameNodes, they must never both act as active. HDFS HA uses ZooKeeper and fencing to guarantee only one is active.
  • Human error: a wrong rm -r on HDFS is copied to all replicas instantly. Replication is not backup: use HDFS trash, snapshots and copies in another cluster.

Exam tip: Explain “failure is the norm, not the exception” with the formula expected failures = devices × failure rate and a worked number. Then list Hadoop's fault-tolerance mechanisms: replication (3, rack-aware), heartbeats, re-replication, task re-execution, checksums, NameNode HA.

3 Types and ways to use it

What fails, and how Hadoop survives it

Four ways to tolerate faults

Redundancy

Keep extra copies of data (replicas or erasure-coded parity) and extra machines (standby masters, spare nodes).

Data survives

Detection

Heartbeats, timeouts, checksums and block reports find failures quickly and automatically.

Notice fast

Recovery

Re-replicate lost blocks, re-run lost tasks, fail over to a standby master, without a human.

Self-healing

Isolation

Spread copies across failure domains (nodes, racks, data centres) so one event can't hit them all.

Limit blast radius

Replication is not a backup. If someone deletes a file or a buggy job overwrites good data, HDFS faithfully applies that change to all three copies. Protect important data with trash, snapshots and a copy in another cluster.

4 Step by step

A disk dies at 3 am: what happens, minute by minute

  1. 03:00 · The disk stops responding

    One of the 12 disks in dn17 fails. The DataNode's reads and writes to that disk return errors.

    If the DataNode is set to tolerate a failed volume (dfs.datanode.failed.volumes.tolerated), it keeps running on its other 11 disks. With the default of 0, the whole DataNode shuts down, which is handled like a node failure.

  2. 03:00 · A job was reading from it

    A map task was reading block 42 from that disk. The read fails.

    The HDFS client simply switches to another replica of block 42 on another node and continues. The task may not even fail.

  3. 03:01 · The NameNode learns what was lost

    dn17 reports the failed volume; its blocks on that disk no longer count.

    Every block that had a replica on that disk is now under-replicated: 2 copies instead of 3. The NameNode puts them in its re-replication queue, most endangered first (blocks with only 1 copy left go to the front).

  4. 03:02 → 04:30 · Re-replication

    lost_tb = 12                       # one 12 TB disk, full of replicas
rate_mb = 40 * 50                  # 40 nodes copying at 50 MB/s each
print(round(lost_tb * 1024**2 / rate_mb / 3600, 1), "hours to restore 3 copies")
    1.7 hours to restore 3 copies
    Many nodes copy in parallel, each block from a surviving replica to a new node chosen with the rack rules. The NameNode limits how fast this goes so that normal jobs aren't starved.

  5. Morning · A technician swaps the disk

    The ops team gets an alert, replaces the disk, and the DataNode starts using it again (empty).

    No data was lost, no job failed, and the replacement is not urgent: the cluster was healthy again within two hours on its own. That is “failures are normal” in practice.
5 See it happen

A node dies: what Riya sees, and how HDFS heals

A file with 4 blocks is stored on 6 nodes in 2 racks. Node dn2 dies. Left: Riya's commands. Right: the replicas.

Losing a node, keeping the data 6 steps
What you see
  1. hdfs fsck checks the file: 4 blocks, all with 3 copies. HEALTHY.
  2. dn2 crashes. An alert fires, but reading the file still works: the client uses other replicas.
  3. After about 10.5 minutes without heartbeats, the NameNode lists dn2 as a dead DataNode.
  4. fsck now shows 2 under-replicated blocks (they had a copy on dn2), 0 missing. Data is safe, just less protected.
  5. Re-replication finishes: every block has 3 copies again. Nobody typed a single repair command.
  6. When dn2 comes back, its old copies are extra. HDFS deletes the surplus so each block has exactly 3.
Behind the screen
  1. Four blocks, three replicas each, spread over 6 nodes and both racks. dn2 holds copies of B2 and B3.
  2. dn2 crashes. Its copies of B2 and B3 are unreachable, but each block still has 2 other copies, so reads continue.
  3. The NameNode waits for the timeout, then marks dn2 dead and stops counting its replicas.
  4. B2 and B3 are under-replicated (2 of 3). The NameNode asks surviving holders to copy them to other nodes.
  5. New replicas land on dn1 (B2) and dn6 (B3), keeping copies on both racks. Every block is back to 3.
  6. When dn2 returns with its old copies, those blocks have 4 replicas; the NameNode removes the extra ones.

6 Real-world use

Living with failure every day

1. Disk replacement as routine work

Large companies replace failed disks every day as a normal ticket. The cluster has already re-copied the data, so the swap can wait for the next working day.

Disk fails→HDFS re-replicates→Swap tomorrow

2. A rack goes dark during maintenance

An electrician switches off the wrong power strip and a whole rack goes down. With rack-aware placement every block still has a copy on another rack: jobs slow a little, nothing is lost.

Rack offline→Copies on other racks→Jobs continue

3. Silent corruption caught by checksums

A worn disk returns a few wrong bytes without any error. The checksum check on read catches it; the client reads a good replica, reports the bad one, and HDFS replaces it. Without checksums, wrong numbers could reach a bank report.

Bad bytes→Checksum mismatch→Good replica used

4. The straggler that delays the report

A node with a dying disk runs tasks five times slower. Speculative execution starts a copy of its slow task elsewhere, and the report finishes on time. The monitoring team then removes the node for repair.

Slow task→Backup attempt→First one wins

5. A whole site down: disaster recovery

Regulated businesses such as banks and stock exchanges keep a disaster-recovery site in another city. Important HDFS data is copied there regularly, so if the main data centre is lost, work moves to the DR cluster.

Main DC→Scheduled copies→DR cluster ready

Examples are simplified. Exact procedures differ between companies.

Run it on your PC: five years of failures, simulated

This Monte Carlo simulation places 20,000 blocks on 200 nodes with 1, 2 or 3 copies, then lets nodes fail at random for five simulated years. A failed node is repaired within a day. Each “block-loss event” is a day on which every copy of some block was on a dead node. Save it as loss_sim.py and run it.

Why HDFS keeps three copies

# loss_sim.py: how likely is data loss with 1, 2 or 3 copies? (a Monte Carlo simulation)
import random

random.seed(42)
NODES, BLOCKS = 200, 20_000
AFR = 0.04            # chance that a node fails in one year (disk, power supply, board …)
REPAIR_DAYS = 1       # time to re-copy a dead node's blocks
YEARS = 5

def lost_blocks(copies):
    placement = [random.sample(range(NODES), copies) for _ in range(BLOCKS)]
    lost = 0
    for day in range(365 * YEARS):
        # which nodes are down today (failed within the last REPAIR_DAYS)?
        down = {n for n in range(NODES) if random.random() < AFR / 365 * REPAIR_DAYS}
        if len(down) < copies:
            continue
        for p in placement:
            if all(n in down for n in p):
                lost += 1
    return lost

for c in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(f"{c} cop{'y' if c == 1 else 'ies'}: {lost_blocks(c):>6} block-loss events in {YEARS} years")

# In a terminal:  python loss_sim.py
1 copy: 4850 block-loss events in 5 years 2 copies: 4 block-loss events in 5 years 3 copies: 0 block-loss events in 5 years

Recorded with random.seed(42), so you will get the same numbers. Change the seed or the failure rate and run again: one copy always loses data, two copies sometimes do, three almost never. This model is simplified (it ignores racks and whole-rack failures), which is exactly why real HDFS also spreads copies over racks.

  • Chance of ≥1 failure among n devices: 1 − (1 − p)n. Grows fast with n.
  • HDFS: 3 replicas, on at least 2 racks. Lose a node → read another copy.

Interview and viva questions

Why is “failure is the norm” in big data systems?

Because a cluster has thousands of disks, nodes, cables and switches. Even if each part rarely fails, the chance that some part fails on a given day is high: 3,000 disks at 1.5% a year is about 45 disk failures a year. Systems like HDFS are therefore designed to expect failures and recover automatically.

How does HDFS achieve fault tolerance?

It splits files into blocks and stores each block on several DataNodes (3 by default) across at least two racks. DataNodes send heartbeats and block reports; when a node or disk is lost, the NameNode re-replicates under-replicated blocks from surviving copies. Checksums detect corrupt replicas. Clients reading data simply switch to another replica if one fails. The NameNode itself is protected by High Availability.

What is the difference between replication and backup?

Replication keeps live copies of the current data to survive hardware failure. Every change, including a mistaken delete, is applied to all replicas. A backup is a separate, point-in-time copy (or a snapshot) that lets you go back to an earlier state after user errors, bugs or ransomware.

How does MapReduce handle a failed task?

The ApplicationMaster notices the failure (error, lost container, or no progress within the timeout) and reschedules the task attempt, preferably on another node. By default a task is tried up to 4 times; if all attempts fail the job fails. Completed map outputs on a lost node are re-run because they were stored on that node's local disk.

What is speculative execution?

When a task runs much slower than other tasks of the same job, the framework may launch a duplicate attempt on another node. Whichever attempt finishes first is used and the other is killed. It helps with stragglers caused by slow hardware, not with tasks that are slow because they have more data (skew).

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Failure situations interviewers love, because they reveal whether you understand how Hadoop actually behaves.

Platform team · incidentFresher

A DataNode died. Should we restart everything?

At 11 pm the monitoring shows one DataNode down out of 60. A junior admin wants to restart the whole cluster “to be safe”. You are on call. What do you do?

The interviewer is checking: do you trust and understand HDFS's self-healing, and avoid making things worse?

Show a strong answer
  1. Don't restart the cluster. That would stop all jobs and put the NameNode in safe mode while it waits for block reports.
  2. Check hdfs dfsadmin -report and hdfs fsck /: expect under-replicated blocks but 0 missing.
  3. After the timeout, HDFS re-replicates automatically. Watch the under-replicated count fall.
  4. Find out why the node died (logs, hardware alerts), and fix or replace it during working hours.

Avoid: panicking, or manually copying blocks around.

Banking · data safety1–3 years

“We have 3 replicas, so we don't need backups”

In a design review for a bank's reporting cluster, a manager says backups are unnecessary because HDFS already stores everything three times.

The interviewer is checking: do you know what replication protects against, and what it doesn't?

Show a strong answer
  • Replication protects against hardware failures: disks, nodes, a rack.
  • It does not protect against a user deleting data, a buggy job overwriting it, ransomware, or losing the whole data centre: every change reaches all replicas.
  • Use HDFS trash (deleted files kept for a while), snapshots of important directories, and regular copies to a second cluster or cloud storage (DistCp). Regulated industries usually require a DR site anyway.

Avoid: agreeing that replication equals backup.

E-commerce · incident1–3 years

fsck says “CORRUPT”: 12 missing blocks

After a power problem in one row of racks, hdfs fsck / reports 12 missing blocks in yesterday's sales folder. Five nodes are still down.

The interviewer is checking: can you investigate missing blocks calmly, and do you know they may come back?

Show a strong answer
  1. Missing usually means “no live replica right now”, not “destroyed”. Bring the five nodes back: when they send block reports, the blocks often reappear.
  2. Use hdfs fsck / -list-corruptfileblocks and -files -blocks -locations to see which files and nodes are involved.
  3. If nodes are truly lost, restore the affected files from the source system, a snapshot or the DR copy, then delete the broken files.
  4. Afterwards, ask why all copies were in the failed racks: is rack awareness configured correctly?

Avoid: running fsck -delete straight away. It removes the files before you know whether the blocks can come back.

“Why not just buy servers that never fail?”

The interviewer asks: “Wouldn't it be simpler to buy top-quality servers with RAID and dual everything, so failures don't happen?”

The interviewer is checking: do you understand the economics behind Hadoop's design?

Show a strong answer
  • No hardware never fails. With thousands of machines, even very reliable ones fail regularly.
  • Highly reliable servers cost much more per TB and per core, and you still need a plan for when they fail.
  • Hadoop's idea: use affordable commodity servers and make failures harmless in software (replicas, retries, re-replication). Adding capacity stays cheap.
  • Spend extra on the few components that matter most: master nodes, network core, power.

Avoid: saying commodity hardware is “bad hardware”. It is ordinary, well-supported hardware without a premium for extra reliability.
