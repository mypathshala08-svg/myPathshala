1 The story

A domain with nowhere to go

Before you start: Lessons 6.1 and 3.5.

🤔 TiffinGo bought tiffingo.in and pointed it at one server’s IP. Then the server was replaced (new IP), half the users still saw the old site for a day (TTL 86400), and on results day the Mumbai Region had trouble — with nowhere to send people.

Amazon Route 53 is AWS’s DNS service: it registers domains, hosts the records that turn names into addresses, checks the health of your endpoints, and decides which answer to give (weighted, latency, failover, geolocation…). This lesson follows a lookup end to end, explains records, alias and TTL, and builds weighted and failover routing with health checks — all testable with dig even without buying a domain.

  • DNS turns names into addresses; Route 53 is authoritative for your zone.
  • Alias records point the apex to AWS resources; TTL controls caching.
  • Routing policies + health checks decide which answer users get.

One A record with a day-long TTL meant stale answers after a server change and no escape route when the Region had trouble.

Riya

Sir, DNS is just “name to IP”. Why a whole lesson?

Arjun Sir

Because the answer can change by who asks, where they are, and whether your servers are healthy. And every answer is cached.

Kabir

Cached for how long?

Arjun Sir

The TTL you set. Yours was 86,400 seconds — one day. That is why half the users saw the old server.

Riya

And the apex, tiffingo.in, can it point to the load balancer?

Arjun Sir

Not with a CNAME. Route 53 has Alias records for that — free, and they follow the ALB’s IPs automatically. Let’s build it.

2 Definition

Amazon Route 53

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Amazon Route 53 is a highly available, scalable Domain Name System (DNS) web service that provides domain registration, authoritative DNS through public and private hosted zones containing resource record sets, Alias records for AWS resources, routing policies (simple, weighted, latency, failover, geolocation, geoproximity, multivalue answer, IP-based) and health checks that influence which records are returned.

In simple words

Route 53 is AWS’s phone book for the internet: it holds your domain’s entries, decides which address to give each visitor, and stops sending people to servers that are down.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “Apex and www are Alias records to the ALB or CloudFront; app records use TTL 60; I lower TTLs before migrations.”
  • “For DR I use failover routing with health checks — primary Mumbai, secondary Hyderabad — and weighted records for canaries.”

3 The big picture

Records, and the many ways to answer

New to computers? Start here. DNS is the internet’s phone book. The registrar is where you buy the name; the hosted zone is your page in the phone book; records are the entries (“www → this address”). The resolver is a helpful operator who looks numbers up for you and remembers them for a while (TTL). Route 53 can also act like a smart receptionist: “Mumbai office is closed today — please call Hyderabad.”

Jargon buster:

  • Domain / registrar — the name you buy, and from whom
  • Hosted zone — the container of records for a domain
  • Record — name + type + value (A, CNAME, MX…)
  • Alias — Route 53 pointer to an AWS resource
  • TTL — seconds a resolver may cache the answer
  • Routing policy — how Route 53 picks an answer

Type 1 · Domains, registrars and delegation

Definition (say it in an interview): A domain is registered for a yearly fee with a registrar (Route 53 Domains, or GoDaddy/BigRock etc.). Making Route 53 authoritative means setting the domain’s NS records at the registrar to the four name servers of your Route 53 hosted zone (e.g. ns-512.awsdns-00.net). The resolution chain is root → TLD (.in, .com) → your authoritative servers.

In plain words (no jargon): Buy a name, then point it to the servers that hold its records.

Analogy: Buying a shop name, then telling the city directory which office keeps your listing.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Registrar. Sells the name.
  2. NS. 4 Route 53 servers.
  3. Chain. Root → TLD → R53.
  4. Propagation. NS TTL up to 2 days.
  5. Transfer. Optional.

Words used above, in simple terms: registrar = company selling domains · TLD = top-level domain like .in · delegation = NS records pointing to authoritative servers

Type 2 · Hosted zones (public and private)

Definition (say it in an interview): A hosted zone is a container of records for a domain. A public hosted zone answers queries from the internet; a private hosted zone answers only from VPCs associated with it (requires enableDnsSupport/Hostnames), ideal for internal names like db.tiffingo.internal. Each zone gets NS and SOA records automatically and costs USD 0.50/month plus queries.

In plain words (no jargon): The place where all records for one domain are kept.

Analogy: Your page in the public phone book vs an office-only internal directory.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Public. Internet answers.
  2. Private. Associated VPCs.
  3. Auto records. NS + SOA.
  4. Cost. USD 0.50/month.
  5. Split-horizon. Same name both.

Words used above, in simple terms: authoritative = the source of truth for a zone · SOA = start of authority record · split-horizon = different answers inside vs outside

Type 3 · Record types (A, AAAA, CNAME, MX, TXT)

Definition (say it in an interview): Records map names to data: A (IPv4), AAAA (IPv6), CNAME (alias to another name; not allowed at the zone apex or alongside other records with the same name), MX (mail servers with priority), TXT (domain verification, SPF/DKIM/DMARC), CAA (which CAs may issue certificates), SRV, and the zone’s NS/SOA.

In plain words (no jargon): Different record types answer different questions about a name.

Analogy: Different kinds of entries in a directory: phone, address, “see also”, post-box.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. A/AAAA. IP addresses.
  2. CNAME. Another name.
  3. MX. Mail.
  4. TXT. Verification/SPF.
  5. CAA. Cert issuers.

Words used above, in simple terms: apex = the bare domain, e.g. tiffingo.in · SPF/DKIM = email authentication via TXT · CAA = certificate authority authorisation

Type 4 · Alias records

Definition (say it in an interview): An Alias record is a Route 53 extension of A/AAAA that points to an AWS resource — ALB/NLB, CloudFront, S3 website endpoint, API Gateway, Global Accelerator, another record in the zone — and returns that resource’s current IPs. Aliases work at the zone apex, queries to AWS targets are free, the TTL is the target’s, and “Evaluate target health” lets it take part in failover.

In plain words (no jargon): A free, apex-friendly pointer to an AWS resource.

Analogy: A “see the reception desk” sign that always shows the desk’s current number.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Targets. ALB, CF, S3, APIGW…
  2. Apex. Yes.
  3. Cost. Free queries.
  4. TTL. Target’s.
  5. Health. Evaluate target health.

Words used above, in simple terms: evaluate target health = use the target’s health for routing · S3 website endpoint = static site hosting endpoint · dualstack = IPv4 + IPv6 name of an ALB

Type 5 · TTL and caching

Definition (say it in an interview): Every record has a TTL (time to live, in seconds) telling resolvers how long to cache the answer. High TTLs (3600–86400) mean fewer queries and faster lookups but slow changes; low TTLs (30–60) allow quick failover and migrations at the cost of more queries. Negative answers (NXDOMAIN) are cached per the SOA’s minimum TTL.

In plain words (no jargon): How long others remember your DNS answer.

Analogy: The operator writes your number on a sticky note and only re-checks after the note expires.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Unit. Seconds.
  2. High. Fewer queries, slow change.
  3. Low. Fast change, more queries.
  4. Alias. Target TTL.
  5. Negative. SOA minimum.

Words used above, in simple terms: TTL = time to live · NXDOMAIN = name does not exist · propagation = caches expiring around the world

Type 6 · Routing policies

Definition (say it in an interview): Route 53 can return different answers for the same name: simple (one record), weighted (proportions, e.g. 90/10 canary), latency (lowest-latency Region for the user), failover (primary/secondary with health checks), geolocation (by continent/country/state), geoproximity (distance with bias; Traffic Flow), multivalue answer (up to 8 healthy records) and IP-based (by client CIDR). Records sharing a name are distinguished by a set identifier.

In plain words (no jargon): Rules for choosing which address to give each user.

Analogy: A receptionist who decides which branch to send each caller to.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Weighted. Proportions.
  2. Latency. Fastest Region.
  3. Failover. Primary/secondary.
  4. Geo. By location.
  5. Multivalue. Many healthy.

Words used above, in simple terms: set identifier = unique ID per record in a policy group · canary = small % to a new version · Traffic Flow = visual policy builder

Type 7 · Health checks and DNS failover

Definition (say it in an interview): A Route 53 health check monitors an endpoint (HTTP/HTTPS/TCP, optional string match) from multiple global locations every 30 s (or 10 s), or watches a CloudWatch alarm, or combines other checks (calculated). Associated with records, an unhealthy check removes those answers; with failover routing the secondary record is returned. Alarms on HealthCheckStatus notify you.

In plain words (no jargon): Route 53 keeps watching your servers and stops sending users to broken ones.

Analogy: Calling each branch every 30 seconds; if Mumbai stops answering three times, send callers to Hyderabad.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Types. Endpoint, alarm, calculated.
  2. Interval. 30 s / 10 s.
  3. Threshold. 1–10 failures.
  4. Locations. Global checkers.
  5. Cost. ≈ USD 0.50–0.75/month each.

Words used above, in simple terms: failure threshold = consecutive failures to mark unhealthy · calculated check = combines child checks · string matching = body must contain a string

5 How it works, step by step

One lookup — then a failover

Browser → resolver → root → .in → Route 53 → ALB → health check → Hyderabad.

Animated

What happens when someone types your domain

Follow one DNS lookup from the browser to Route 53 and to your load balancer — then watch Route 53 fail over to another Region.

From name to server 6 steps
Watch what happens
  1. Step 1 · The question — the browser asks a DNS resolver (your ISP’s, or 8.8.8.8). If the answer is cached and the TTL has not expired, it replies immediately.
  2. Step 2 · Delegation — otherwise it asks the root servers, then the .in servers, which reply: “tiffingo.in is served by ns-512.awsdns-00.net…” — the NS records you set at the registrar.
  3. Step 3 · Route 53 answers — Route 53’s hosted zone holds the records. www is an alias to the Mumbai ALB, TTL 60 seconds; the resolver caches it.
  4. Step 4 · Connect — the browser connects directly to the ALB’s IP. DNS is only the phone book — traffic never flows through Route 53.
  5. Step 5 · Health check fails — Route 53 health checkers probe /health from around the world. Three failures in a row mark the primary record unhealthy.
  6. Step 6 · Failover — with failover routing, Route 53 now answers with the Hyderabad ALB. As cached answers expire (TTL), users reach the healthy Region.

7 Learn at your level

From curious beginner to expert — pick your level

Find your level, read that card, do the “Try” and answer the check. Then move one level up. Everyone starts somewhere — Level 0 needs no computer background at all.

Level 0 · Curious no computer background

Understand: Computers find each other by numbers (IP addresses). DNS lets people use names like tiffingo.in instead — Route 53 is AWS’s DNS.

Try: Your phone’s contact list maps “Amma” to a number. DNS is the world’s contact list for websites.

Check yourself: If a website moves to a new number, why might some people still reach the old one?

Their contact list (resolver cache) still has the old number until the TTL runs out.

Level 1 · Beginner first steps on AWS

Understand: Hosted zones, records (A, CNAME, MX, TXT), Alias for AWS resources, TTL, routing policies and health checks.

Try: Do the simulator, then write the records for tiffingo.in: apex, www, api, mail, and a TXT verification.

Check yourself: Why can’t tiffingo.in be a CNAME to the ALB?

A CNAME cannot coexist with the apex’s NS/SOA records — use a Route 53 Alias.

Level 2 · Intermediate can do it alone

Understand: Create a zone, query it with dig @ns, build a weighted canary and a health-checked failover, and watch failover happen.

Try: Do the hands-on and record how many seconds failover took after stopping the instance.

Check yourself: Failover took 6 minutes instead of 2 — two likely causes?

High TTL on the record (or the resolver caching longer), and a large failure threshold/interval on the health check.

Level 3 · Advanced designs for others

Understand: Design multi-Region DNS: latency + failover trees, private zones shared across accounts, Resolver endpoints for hybrid DNS, DNSSEC and query logging.

Try: Design DNS for an app in Mumbai and Singapore with latency routing and per-Region failover.

Check yourself: How do on-prem servers resolve private hosted zone names?

Route 53 Resolver inbound endpoints in the VPC; on-prem DNS forwards the zone to them.

Level 4 · Expert architect / interviewer

Understand: Own naming as a platform: domain portfolio, delegation to teams’ subzones, IaC for records, DNS firewall, incident runbooks for DNS failures.

Try: Write the runbook for “primary Region down”: who flips what, expected RTO, how to verify.

Check yourself: Why use Application Recovery Controller instead of only health checks?

Health checks can flap or miss partial failures; ARC routing controls give a deliberate, highly available switch for Region failover.

Testing a zone before delegating it

You can create a hosted zone for any name and query its name servers directly — no domain purchase needed. That is exactly how the lab works: dig @ns-512.awsdns-00.net www.tiffingo-lab.in. Public resolvers will not find it until NS is set at a registrar.

Weighted maths

Each record’s share = weight ÷ sum of weights in the group. Weights 90 and 10 give 90% and 10% over many queries — but a single resolver caches one answer for the TTL, so individual users stick to one version for a while.

9 Under the hood

Anycast, shuffle sharding, checkers

How Route 53 stays up when everything else is down.

1 · Anycast name servers

Each hosted zone gets four name servers from different TLDs (.com, .net, .org, .co.uk). Each name is anycast from many edge locations worldwide, so queries reach the nearest healthy location — and a problem with one TLD does not take your zone down.

2 · Shuffle sharding

Every zone gets a different combination of four name servers out of thousands. A DDoS against one customer’s name servers rarely affects another customer, because almost no two zones share the same set.

3 · Data plane vs control plane

Answering queries and evaluating health checks (data plane) is designed for 100% availability. Creating zones and changing records (control plane) is centralised and less available — so DR plans should not depend on making DNS edits during a Region outage; pre-build failover records instead.

4 · Health checkers vote

Checkers in many Regions probe your endpoint; an endpoint is healthy if more than 18% of checkers report it healthy. That avoids failing over because of one checker’s network problem.

14 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

These labs run on your own AWS account — we cannot run them for you. Every step shows the UI way (console clicks) and the CLI way (commands for AWS CloudShell or your terminal), plus what you should see. Work as an IAM/Identity Center user, never root; use the Mumbai Region (ap-south-1); stay inside Free Tier/credits; and always do the Clean up step at the end. Uses the 6.1 web server (WEB, WEB_IP). No domain purchase needed — we query Route 53’s name servers directly. A hosted zone deleted within 12 hours is not charged; the health check costs about ₹40/month prorated.

  1. 0 · Get ready: signed in, budget, Region, CloudShell

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Sign in as your admin user (not root) — Lesson 1.2 shows how to create it.
    2. Billing and Cost Management → Budgets → check a monthly cost budget with e-mail alerts exists (e.g. $5).
    3. Top-right Region menu → Asia Pacific (Mumbai).
    4. Click the CloudShell icon (>_) in the top bar — the AWS CLI is already signed in there.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws sts get-caller-identity --query Arn --output text   # must NOT end in :root
aws configure set region ap-south-1
aws configure list | grep region

    You should see: an ARN ending in :user/… or :assumed-role/… and region ap-south-1.

    CloudShell is free, runs in your browser and is already signed in as the user you logged in with.

  2. 1 · A hosted zone you can test without buying a domain

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Route 53 → Hosted zones → Create hosted zone tiffingo-lab.in (public) → open it and copy one of the four NS names → Create record www, A, 13.233.41.20 (or your 6.1 web IP), TTL 60.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    Z=$(aws route53 create-hosted-zone --name tiffingo-lab.in --caller-reference lab-$(date +%s) --query HostedZone.Id --output text | cut -d/ -f3)
NS=$(aws route53 get-hosted-zone --id $Z --query 'DelegationSet.NameServers[0]' --output text); echo $Z $NS
cat > www.json <<EOF
{"Changes":[{"Action":"UPSERT","ResourceRecordSet":{"Name":"www.tiffingo-lab.in","Type":"A","TTL":60,"ResourceRecords":[{"Value":"$WEB_IP"}]}}]}
EOF
C=$(aws route53 change-resource-record-sets --hosted-zone-id $Z --change-batch file://www.json --query ChangeInfo.Id --output text)
aws route53 wait resource-record-sets-changed --id $C
dig +short @$NS www.tiffingo-lab.in
dig +short www.tiffingo-lab.in   # public resolvers: nothing (not delegated)

    You should see: the zone ID and a name server like ns-512.awsdns-00.net; the first dig prints your web IP; the second prints nothing.

    Route 53 is answering — the internet just does not know to ask it, because no registrar points tiffingo-lab.in here.

  3. 2 · Weighted records (a 90/10 canary)

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Create record api, A, value = IP 1, routing policy Weighted, weight 90, record ID v1 → again with IP 2, weight 10, record ID v2. Query the name server 100 times and count.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    w(){ echo "{\"Action\":\"UPSERT\",\"ResourceRecordSet\":{\"Name\":\"api.tiffingo-lab.in\",\"Type\":\"A\",\"SetIdentifier\":\"$1\",\"Weight\":$2,\"TTL\":60,\"ResourceRecords\":[{\"Value\":\"$3\"}]}}"; }
echo "{\"Changes\":[$(w v1 90 192.0.2.10),$(w v2 10 192.0.2.20)]}" > weighted.json
aws route53 change-resource-record-sets --hosted-zone-id $Z --change-batch file://weighted.json --query ChangeInfo.Status --output text
sleep 30; for i in $(seq 1 100); do dig +short @$NS api.tiffingo-lab.in; done | sort | uniq -c

    You should see: PENDING, then roughly 90 192.0.2.10 and 10 192.0.2.20 (it varies a little).

    Asking the authoritative server directly skips caching, so you see the weights. Real users behind one resolver would stick to one answer for the TTL.

  4. 3 · Health check + failover

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Health checks → Create health check mumbai-web, HTTP, your web IP, port 80, path / → Hosted zones → app record, Failover PRIMARY = web IP with that health check; second app record Failover SECONDARY = 192.0.2.99. Stop the web instance; after ~1–2 minutes dig returns the secondary.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    HC=$(aws route53 create-health-check --caller-reference hc-$(date +%s) --health-check-config IPAddress=$WEB_IP,Port=80,Type=HTTP,ResourcePath=/,RequestInterval=30,FailureThreshold=3 --query HealthCheck.Id --output text)
f(){ echo "{\"Action\":\"UPSERT\",\"ResourceRecordSet\":{\"Name\":\"app.tiffingo-lab.in\",\"Type\":\"A\",\"SetIdentifier\":\"$1\",\"Failover\":\"$2\",\"TTL\":60,\"ResourceRecords\":[{\"Value\":\"$3\"}]$4}}"; }
echo "{\"Changes\":[$(f mumbai PRIMARY $WEB_IP ",\"HealthCheckId\":\"$HC\""),$(f dr SECONDARY 192.0.2.99 '')]}" > failover.json
aws route53 change-resource-record-sets --hosted-zone-id $Z --change-batch file://failover.json >/dev/null
sleep 90; dig +short @$NS app.tiffingo-lab.in
aws ec2 stop-instances --instance-ids $WEB >/dev/null; sleep 150
aws route53 get-health-check-status --health-check-id $HC --query 'HealthCheckObservations[0].StatusReport.Status' --output text
dig +short @$NS app.tiffingo-lab.in

    You should see: first your web IP; after stopping it, a status like Failure: Connection timed out… and the answer 192.0.2.99.

    Start the instance again and within a minute or two the primary answer returns — failback is automatic.

Clean up and cost check

Delete the health check; delete all non-NS/SOA records (change batch with Action DELETE, or in the console select and delete); delete the hosted zone (aws route53 delete-hosted-zone --id $Z); start or terminate the web instance as you need.

2 Faster health check

3 Write the answer

Standard check (30 s × 3): DNS switched after ~95 s.
Fast check (10 s × 2): ~40 s.
User impact = switch time + up to TTL (60 s) of cached answers ⇒ ~2.5 min vs ~1.7 min.
Recommendation: fast checks for the results portal only (cost ≈ USD 1/month more).

Interview and viva questions

Do I have to buy my domain from AWS?

No. Keep it at any registrar and set its NS records to your Route 53 hosted zone.

How fast is DNS failover?

Health-check detection (≈ 30–90 s with standard checks) plus up to the record’s TTL for cached answers — usually 1–3 minutes.

No, but cheap: USD 0.50 per hosted zone per month plus queries; Alias queries to AWS resources are free.
