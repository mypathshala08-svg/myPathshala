What is big data? The 5 Vs explained

Big data is not just “a very big file”. In this lesson you follow a ₹20 chai payment and see the five things that make data “big”: how much there is, how fast it arrives, how mixed it is, how trustworthy it is, and what it is worth. Then you measure all five on real data with Python, watch them light up during a midnight sale, and go deeper into peaks, value decay and the cost of keeping data.

2 Definition

What is big data?

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Big data refers to data sets whose volume, velocity or variety is so large that traditional tools, such as a single relational database or a spreadsheet on one machine, cannot capture, store, process and analyse them within an acceptable time and cost. It is commonly described by the 5 Vs: Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity and Value.

In simple words

Big data is data that is too big, arrives too fast, or is too mixed for one computer and normal software to handle. It is often messy, and it's only worth collecting if you can get something useful out of it.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

RecordOne unit of data, such as one order, one payment or one click. A row in a table, or one JSON object.
Structured dataData with fixed columns and types, like a SQL table or a clean CSV.
Semi-structured dataData with labels but no fixed table shape: JSON, XML, log lines.
Unstructured dataData with no labels at all: photos, videos, audio, PDFs, free-text reviews.
ThroughputHow many records arrive or are processed per second. It measures velocity.
Data qualityHow correct, complete and consistent the data is. It measures veracity.

How big is big? Units of data

UnitSizeTo picture it
Byte8 bitsOne English letter, such as A
KB (kilobyte)1,024 bytesAbout 4 UPI payment records (≈ 250 bytes each)
MB (megabyte)1,024 KBOne phone photo is 2–5 MB
GB (gigabyte)1,024 MBA full-length movie, or about 43 lakh payment records
TB (terabyte)1,024 GBA typical laptop disk; about 440 crore payment records
PB (petabyte)1,024 TBThe data platforms of large banks, telecom and e-commerce companies
EB (exabyte)1,024 PBTotal storage of the very largest internet companies
ZB (zettabyte)1,024 EBIndustry estimates put the data the whole world creates in a year at well over 100 ZB
YB (yottabyte)1,024 ZBNo organisation stores anything close to this yet

Two ways of counting: computers count in powers of 2 (1 KB = 1,024 bytes; the official names are KiB, MiB, GiB), while disk makers count in powers of 10 (1 kB = 1,000 bytes). That is why a “1 TB” disk shows about 931 GB on your computer.

Quick memory trick: each step up is about a thousand times bigger. “Kids Make Great Tea, Papa Enjoys Zero Yelling” gives KB, MB, GB, TB, PB, EB, ZB, YB in order.

The 5 Vs at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Big data in your own phone

You already use big data systems every day. Each app below is dealing with all five Vs at once:

  • UPI: billions of payments a month (volume), each answered in about a second (velocity).
  • YouTube and Instagram: videos, photos, comments and likes together (variety).
  • Google Maps: live speed from millions of phones, some of them wrong or stale (veracity).
  • Shopping apps: “customers also bought” suggestions that increase sales (value).

Easy test: if one laptop or one database can store it and answer in time, it is not big data yet, however important it is.

Intermediate Putting a number on each V

Engineers don't say “we have a lot of data”. They measure each V:

VWhat engineers measureExample
VolumeTB stored, GB added per day, growth per year78 GB of new payments a day, growing 40% a year
VelocityEvents per second (average and peak), allowed delay (latency)4,000/s average, 30,000/s peak, reply within 1 s
VarietyNumber of sources and formats, how often their structure changes12 sources: tables, JSON, logs, images
Veracity% duplicates, % missing values, % failing checks0.4% duplicates from retries
ValueMoney saved or earned vs cost to store and processFraud blocked worth ₹2 crore vs ₹15 lakh platform cost

More Vs you may see: Variability (the rate or meaning changes, such as order spikes on festivals, or the word “cool” meaning cold or nice) and Visualisation (showing huge data so people can understand it). Some books list 7 or even 10 Vs; most university syllabi ask for 5.

Advanced Peaks, value decay and the cost of keeping data

  • Design for the peak: the peak-to-average ratio (peak events per second ÷ average) decides capacity. A system that copes with the average fails on Diwali night or after a cricket final.
  • Value falls with age: a payment record is worth most in the first second (fraud check), less after a day (disputes), and much less after a year (audits, model training). So data moves from hot (fast, costly) to warm to cold (cheap archive) storage, and is deleted or anonymised when retention ends.
  • Law sets minimums: Indian anti-money-laundering rules require banks to keep transaction records for at least five years, and the RBI requires payment-system data to be stored in India. Retention and location are design inputs, not afterthoughts.
  • Working set vs total data: a company may store 2 PB but query only the last 30 days (40 TB) daily. Size the compute for the working set and the storage for the total.
  • Don't over-engineer: a 5 GB problem on a Spark cluster is slower and costlier than one good machine. The right question is always “which V actually breaks our current tool?”

Exam tip: The first three Vs (Volume, Velocity, Variety) were described by analyst Doug Laney in 2001. Veracity and Value were added later by industry. If a question asks for “3 Vs”, write the first three; for “5 Vs”, write all five with one example each.

3 Types and ways to use it

The 5 Vs, one by one

1. Volume: how much

The total amount of data. Terabytes and petabytes, not megabytes. A bank stores every transaction for years because regulators require it.

Size

2. Velocity: how fast

The speed at which data arrives and must be handled. Thousands of payments per second at lunchtime, each needing an answer in a second or two.

Speed

3. Variety: how mixed

Different shapes of data: tables, JSON, logs, photos, voice notes, reviews. One system must store and use all of them.

Formats

4. Veracity: how trustworthy

Real data is messy: duplicates, missing fields, wrong values, fake accounts. You must clean and check before you trust a number.

Quality

5. Value: how useful

Data costs money to store. It is only worth it if it leads to decisions: stopping fraud, recommending products, planning stock.

Is it big data? A quick test

Not big data

A school's marks of 2,000 students. One MySQL table or one Excel sheet handles it easily, even with complex reports.

Small

Borderline

The canteen’s 13.5 lakh orders. Too big for Excel, but one laptop with Python or one database handles it in seconds.

Medium

Big data

All UPI payments in India, every second, with fraud checks in real time, stored for years. No single machine can do this.

Big

Rule of thumb: data is “big” when you need more than one machine to store it or process it in time. That's exactly what the next two lessons explain.

4 Step by step

Follow one ₹20 chai payment through the 5 Vs

  1. One payment becomes one record

    {"txn_id": "T1001", "payer": "riya@okbank", "payee": "canteen@upi",
 "amount": 20, "time": "2026-10-19 08:01:07", "status": "SUCCESS"}
    This record is about 130 characters. With extra fields that real systems keep (device, location, bank codes), a payment record is a few hundred bytes. Small. On its own, this is not big data.

  2. Volume: multiply by everyone

    per_record = 250                    # bytes
per_month = 10_000_000_000          # 10 billion payments
print(per_record * per_month / 1024**4, "TB per month")
    2.2737367544323206 TB per month
    About 2.3 TB a month just for the basic records, and banks keep them for years, with copies for safety. Add logs, fraud scores and app clicks, and you are into petabytes.

  3. Velocity: they don't arrive evenly

    per_second_avg = 10_000_000_000 / (30 * 24 * 3600)
print(round(per_second_avg), "payments per second on average")
    3858 payments per second on average
    Nearly 4,000 per second on average. At lunch, on salary day or during a sale, the peak is several times higher. Each one needs a fraud check and a reply in about a second.

  4. Variety: the payment is not alone

    Around the same payment, the systems also receive the app's click logs (JSON), an SMS (text), the canteen's menu photo, and Riya's 5-star review with a comment.

    One table cannot hold all of these. Big data platforms store raw files of any format in a data lake, and process each kind with suitable tools.

  5. Veracity: some records are wrong

    Riya's network dropped once, so the app retried and the same T1001 arrived twice. Another record has "amount": -20, and one has no payer at all.

    If you sum blindly, the canteen's income is wrong. Pipelines remove duplicates, reject invalid rows and log them for checking. Clean data first, numbers later.

  6. Value: turn records into decisions

    After cleaning, patterns appear: chai sells most at 8 am and 4 pm; samosas run out by 1 pm; one account is paying from five cities in ten minutes.

    The canteen stocks more samosas before 1 pm, and the bank blocks the suspicious account. Storing data has a cost; value is what pays for it.
5 See it happen

A ₹20 payment: what Riya sees, and what the data platform sees

Step through the six frames. Left: Riya's phone. Right: the same moment for the systems behind it.

One payment, five Vs 6 steps
What you see
  1. Riya scans the canteen's QR code and pays ₹20 for a cutting chai.
  2. A green tick: Payment successful. For her, the story ends here.
  3. Her bank sends an SMS and the app shows a notification, both within a second.
  4. She rates the chai five stars, writes a short review and adds a photo.
  5. Earlier that week, a payment showed Pending because the network was weak, and the app retried it.
  6. Next day the app offers cashback, and the canteen announces fresh samosas at the time they usually run out.
Behind the screen
  1. The payment becomes one record: a few hundred bytes of semi-structured JSON.
  2. Volume: about 10 billion such records a month is roughly 2.3 TB, kept for years with backup copies. That needs many disks on many machines.
  3. Velocity: payments arrive at about 4,000 per second on average, and far more at peak. Every one is checked for fraud before the reply goes back.
  4. Variety: the same moment produces a table row, JSON logs and text, and an unstructured photo and review.
  5. Veracity: the retry created a duplicate, and other records are broken. Adding them blindly gives a wrong total. Pipelines clean the data first.
  6. Value: clean data, grouped by hour, shows when samosas run out and who the regular customers are. Decisions follow.

6 Real-world use

Real-time scenarios: the 5 Vs under pressure

Three live scenarios: velocity breaking a single server on match night, all five Vs lighting up in the first seconds of a midnight sale, and an advanced one about how a record's value falls with age and where it should be stored at each stage.

Real-time scenario 1

Cricket final night at a food-delivery app

On a normal evening, orders arrive steadily. During a big cricket final, they come in waves: at the toss, in the innings break, and after the last ball. Watch velocity break a single database, and a big data setup absorb it.

Velocity on a match night 5 steps
Animated example
  1. Normal evening: orders arrive at a steady rate. One database server handles them comfortably.
  2. Toss: a small spike as people order snacks before the match. Still fine.
  3. Innings break: lakhs of people order at the same minute. The single server's queue starts to grow.
  4. Trouble: orders time out and customers see errors. A bigger server helps only up to a point, and is costly all year for one night's peak.
  5. Big data design: orders flow into a stream such as Kafka, and many machines process them in parallel. More machines can be added for big nights. Numbers here are illustrative.
Real-time scenario 2

Midnight sale: all five Vs in the first ten minutes

An online shop's big festive sale starts at 00:00. The data team sits in front of a live dashboard. Watch each V light up as crores of shoppers arrive at once.

Live 5 Vs dashboard 5 steps
Animated example
  1. 23:58: a normal night at about 3,000 orders per second. All five tiles on the dashboard are calm.
  2. 00:00:05: the sale opens. Orders jump to about 60,000 per second (velocity), and around a crore cart events pile up in ten minutes (volume).
  3. Variety: in the same second the platform receives payment JSON, search text, product photos and support chats. Each needs different storage and processing.
  4. Veracity: bots hammer the add-to-cart button and network retries send duplicate payments. They are filtered in the stream before any number is shown.
  5. Value: the clean, live numbers show Phone X selling out in Pune but not Mumbai. The team moves stock and pauses ads within minutes. (All numbers are illustrative.)
Real-time scenario 3 · advanced

The life of one payment record: value falls, so cost must fall too

Riya's ₹20 chai payment is stored for years. But it is not equally useful all that time. Follow the record as it ages and see where a sensible platform keeps it at each stage.

Value decay and storage tiers 5 steps
Animated example
  1. First second: the record sits in the stream processor's memory while the fraud check runs. Memory is the costliest storage, but this is when the record is most valuable.
  2. First days: Riya may check her history or raise a dispute, so the record stays in a fast hot database.
  3. After a month: it is used mainly for statements and analytics, so it moves to warm storage in the warehouse or lake.
  4. After a year: it is rarely read, but needed for audits and for training fraud models. It moves to a cold archive that costs a small fraction per GB.
  5. End of retention: once the legal minimum has passed (for example, at least five years for bank transaction records under India's anti-money-laundering rules), it is deleted or anonymised according to policy.

Where else the 5 Vs show up at work

1. Banks: catching fraud in real time

Every card and UPI payment is scored for fraud within moments: is this device new, is the location unusual, is the amount strange for this customer? That's velocity (answer fast), volume (years of history per customer) and veracity (don't block genuine payments).

Payment→Fraud model + customer history→Allow / block

2. E-commerce: “customers also bought”

Shopping sites record every search, click, cart and purchase from crores of users. Big data jobs find patterns overnight, and recommendations update as you browse. Variety matters: product photos, reviews and clicks are used together.

Clicks + orders→Spark jobs find patterns→Recommendations

3. Ride-hailing: surge pricing and ETAs

Cabs send GPS locations every few seconds. The platform combines millions of location points with ride requests to estimate arrival times and set prices zone by zone. That's velocity and volume at once.

GPS pings→Stream processing by zone→ETA + price

4. Telecom: network health from call records

Mobile operators collect a record for every call, SMS and data session, plus logs from every tower. Analysing them finds congested towers, dropped-call hotspots and customers likely to switch operators.

Tower logs + CDRs→Hadoop / Spark analysis→Fix towers, offers

5. Agriculture and weather

Satellite images (unstructured, huge) and weather-station readings are combined to estimate crop health, forecast rain and plan insurance payouts for farmers. Each image alone can be hundreds of megabytes.

Satellite + sensors→Image + time-series processing→Crop advice

Examples are simplified to show where each V matters. Real setups vary by company.

7 Run the code

Measure the 5 Vs yourself

A tiny slice of the canteen's data: six payment records as JSON text, two photos and a review. The program measures each V. Read it first, then try the challenges, which include an Excel-limit calculator, a size converter and a record that breaks the program.

import json

# Six payment events, as they arrive from the app (JSON text)
events = [
    '{"id": "T1", "item": "chai", "amount": 20, "sec": 1}',
    '{"id": "T2", "item": "vada pav", "amount": 25, "sec": 1}',
    '{"id": "T3", "item": "chai", "amount": 20, "sec": 2}',
    '{"id": "T2", "item": "vada pav", "amount": 25, "sec": 1}',
    '{"id": "T4", "item": "samosa", "amount": -15, "sec": 2}',
    '{"id": "T5", "item": "chai", "amount": 20, "sec": 3}',
]
data = {"json": events, "image": ["chai.jpg", "menu.png"], "text": ["Best chai!"]}

# VOLUME: how much data?
size = sum(len(e) for e in events)
print("Volume  :", len(events), "records,", size, "bytes")

# VELOCITY: how fast does it arrive?
records = [json.loads(e) for e in events]
seconds = len({r["sec"] for r in records})
print("Velocity:", len(records) / seconds, "records per second")

# VARIETY: how many kinds of data?
print("Variety :", len(data), "kinds ->", ", ".join(data))

# VERACITY: how many records can we trust?
seen, clean, bad = set(), [], 0
for r in records:
    if r["id"] in seen or r["amount"] <= 0:
        bad += 1
        continue
    seen.add(r["id"])
    clean.append(r)
print("Veracity:", bad, "bad records out of", len(records))

# VALUE: what does the clean data tell us?
sales = {}
for r in clean:
    sales[r["item"]] = sales.get(r["item"], 0) + r["amount"]
print("Value   : income Rs", sum(sales.values()), "| best seller:", max(sales, key=sales.get))
Volume : 6 records, 323 bytes Velocity: 2.0 records per second Variety : 3 kinds -> json, image, text Veracity: 2 bad records out of 6 Value : income Rs 85 | best seller: chai
  • json.loads(e)Turns JSON text into a Python dictionary. Real pipelines do this millions of times per second, which is part of why velocity is hard.
  • len({r["sec"] for r in records})A set keeps only unique values, so this counts how many different seconds the records arrived in.
  • r["id"] in seenThe retry sent T2 twice. Without this check the income would be counted twice: a veracity problem.
  • r["amount"] <= 0A payment of −15 is impossible, so it is rejected instead of silently reducing the total.
  • max(sales, key=sales.get)Finds the item with the highest sales: a small example of turning data into a decision (value).

Read all 15 lakh rows with Python

import csv, time
from collections import Counter

start = time.time()
sales = Counter()

Example 1BeginnerRetail · Volume

How much data does a supermarket chain create?

Problem statement

A supermarket chain has 2,000 stores. Each store prints about 400 bills a day, and each bill record is about 600 bytes. How much bill data does one store create in a year, and how much does the whole chain create?

Input
600 bytes × 400 bills × 365 days × 2000 stores
Expected output
One store per year : 83.5 MB
Whole chain per year: 163.2 GB

Approach

  1. Multiply bytes per bill by bills per day and days per year for one store.
  2. Multiply by the number of stores for the chain.
  3. Convert bytes to MB (÷ 1024²) and GB (÷ 1024³) so the numbers are readable.

Example 4AdvancedFintech · capacity planning

How much storage will we need in five years?

Problem statement

A payments company adds 78 GB of new records a day today, and the daily amount grows 40% a year. Records are kept for at least five years. The platform keeps 3 copies of every file for safety, and the Parquet format compresses the data about 4 times. How much disk is needed at the end of each year?

Input
78 GB/day, 40% growth, 3 copies, 4× compression, 5 years
Expected output
Year 1: raw   27.8 TB -> disk   20.9 TB
Year 2: raw   66.7 TB -> disk   50.0 TB
Year 3: raw  121.2 TB -> disk   90.9 TB
Year 4: raw  197.5 TB -> disk  148.1 TB
Year 5: raw  304.3 TB -> disk  228.2 TB

Approach

  1. Year 1 adds 78 × 365 GB. Each later year adds 40% more than the year before: multiply by 1.4 each time.
  2. Keep a running total of raw data, because old data is not deleted within five years.
  3. Stored size = raw total × copies ÷ compression. Convert GB to TB by dividing by 1024.

Quick revision

Lesson 1.1 in one screen

  • Big data = too big, too fast or too varied for traditional single-machine tools.
  • Volume (how much) · Velocity (how fast) · Variety (how mixed) · Veracity (how trustworthy) · Value (how useful).
  • First 3 Vs: Doug Laney, 2001. Veracity and Value added later.
  • Structured (tables) · semi-structured (JSON, logs, XML) · unstructured (photos, video, audio, free text).
  • Units: KB → MB → GB → TB → PB → EB, each × 1024.
  • Excel holds 10,48,576 rows per sheet. Too big for Excel ≠ big data.
  • Rule of thumb: it is big data when you need more than one machine to store or process it in time.
  • Engineers measure each V: TB and growth (volume), events per second and latency (velocity), sources and formats (variety), % bad records (veracity), money gained vs cost (value).
  • Design for the peak, not the average. Peak-to-average ratio = peak rate ÷ average rate.
  • Value falls with age: memory → hot → warm → cold → delete or anonymise when retention ends.
  • Extra Vs: Variability (rate or meaning changes) and Visualisation.

Interview and viva questions

What is big data? Explain the 5 Vs with examples.

Big data is data whose size, speed or variety exceeds what traditional single-machine tools can handle in acceptable time. Volume: UPI's billions of monthly payments. Velocity: thousands of payments per second needing fraud checks. Variety: tables, JSON logs, photos and reviews together. Veracity: duplicate and broken records that must be cleaned. Value: the decisions the data enables, such as blocking fraud or planning stock.

What is the difference between structured, semi-structured and unstructured data?

Structured data has a fixed schema of columns and types (SQL tables). Semi-structured data has labels or tags but a flexible shape (JSON, XML, logs). Unstructured data has no predefined model (images, video, audio, free text). Each needs different storage and processing.

Is a 10 GB CSV file big data?

Usually not. A single modern machine can process 10 GB with tools like Python, Pandas (in chunks) or a database. It becomes big data when the size, arrival speed or processing deadline needs a cluster of machines.

Why is veracity important?

Because wrong data leads to wrong decisions. Duplicates inflate totals, missing values bias averages and fake records hide fraud. Big data pipelines spend a lot of effort on validation, de-duplication and cleaning before analysis.

Why can't traditional RDBMS handle big data well?

A traditional RDBMS usually runs on one server, so it is limited by that machine's disk, memory and CPU, and scaling up gets very expensive. It also expects a fixed schema, while big data includes semi-structured and unstructured data. Big data systems spread storage and processing across many cheaper machines.

Are there more than 5 Vs of big data?

Yes. Some authors add Variability (the rate or meaning of data changes over time), Visualisation (presenting huge data so people can understand it), Validity and Volatility, giving 7 or 10 Vs. Most syllabi and interviews expect the 5 Vs: Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity and Value. Mention Variability as the common sixth.

Why are systems designed for peak load and not average load?

Because customers arrive in bursts: festival sales, salary days, the end of a cricket match. If capacity matches the average, the system fails exactly when demand is highest. Engineers measure the peak-to-average ratio and design for the peak with some headroom, often scaling machines up automatically for known peaks.

Practice problems

Small programs about measuring and cleaning data. The last three are business tasks you could meet in a data engineering interview.

P1Easy

TB to GB

A company stores 5 TB of logs. Print how many GB that is (1 TB = 1024 GB).

Input
5 TB
Expected output
5120 GB
Show solution and explanation 
tb = 5
print(tb * 1024, "GB")
5120 GB

How it works: Each step up the unit ladder is × 1024.

P2Easy

Count records and bytes

Given a list of three log lines, print how many records there are and their total size in bytes (one character = one byte here).

Input
3 log lines
Expected output
Records: 3
Bytes: 54
Show solution and explanation 
logs = ["08:01 INFO login riya", "08:02 INFO pay 20", "08:02 WARN retry"]
print("Records:", len(logs))
print("Bytes:", sum(len(x) for x in logs))
Records: 3 Bytes: 54

How it works: len of a string is its number of characters; sum adds them up.

Room left in Excel

A report already has 2,50,000 rows. How many more rows fit in one Excel sheet (limit 1,048,576)?

Input
250000
Expected output
Rows left: 798576
Show solution and explanation 
used = 250_000
print("Rows left:", 1_048_576 - used)
Rows left: 798576

How it works: Underscores in numbers are allowed in Python and make big numbers readable.

P4Medium

Remove duplicate orders

Remove duplicate order ids but keep the first occurrence and the original order.

Input
T1, T2, T1, T3, T2
Expected output
['T1', 'T2', 'T3']
Show solution and explanation 
orders = ["T1", "T2", "T1", "T3", "T2"]
seen, unique = set(), []
for o in orders:
    if o not in seen:
        seen.add(o)
        unique.append(o)
print(unique)
['T1', 'T2', 'T3']

How it works: The set gives fast “already seen?” checks; the list keeps the order.

P5Medium

Count missing values

Count how many records are missing a city (the value is None or an empty string).

Input
4 customer records
Expected output
Missing city: 2 of 4
Show solution and explanation 
customers = [
    {"name": "Riya", "city": "Pune"},
    {"name": "Aman", "city": None},
    {"name": "Neha", "city": ""},
    {"name": "Kabir", "city": "Mumbai"},
]
missing = sum(1 for c in customers if not c["city"])
print("Missing city:", missing, "of", len(customers))
Missing city: 2 of 4

How it works: Both None and "" are “falsy”, so not c["city"] catches both.

P6Advanced

Peak-to-average ratio

Orders per minute during one evening are given. Print the average, the peak and the peak-to-average ratio (one decimal place).

Input
[120, 135, 980, 140, 110, 1250, 130]
Expected output
Average: 409.3
Peak: 1250
Peak-to-average ratio: 3.1
Show solution and explanation 
per_minute = [120, 135, 980, 140, 110, 1250, 130]
average = sum(per_minute) / len(per_minute)
peak = max(per_minute)
print("Average:", round(average, 1))
print("Peak:", peak)
print("Peak-to-average ratio:", round(peak / average, 1))
Average: 409.3 Peak: 1250 Peak-to-average ratio: 3.1

How it works: The ratio tells you how much spare capacity the system needs above the average. A ratio above 3 already means a system sized for the average will fail during the rush.

P7Advanced

Data quality score

Check each payment record: a missing id, an amount of zero or less, and a repeated id are problems. Count each problem and print the percentage of good records.

Input
6 records
Expected output
{'missing id': 1, 'bad amount': 1, 'duplicate': 1}
Quality score: 50% good records
Show solution and explanation 
records = [
    {"id": "T1", "amount": 20}, {"id": "T2", "amount": -5},
    {"id": "T1", "amount": 20}, {"id": None, "amount": 30},
    {"id": "T3", "amount": 45}, {"id": "T4", "amount": 15},
]
seen = set()
problems = {"missing id": 0, "bad amount": 0, "duplicate": 0}
good = 0
for r in records:
    if r["id"] is None:
        problems["missing id"] += 1
    elif r["amount"] <= 0:
        problems["bad amount"] += 1
    elif r["id"] in seen:
        problems["duplicate"] += 1
    else:
        seen.add(r["id"])
        good += 1
print(problems)
print(f"Quality score: {good / len(records) * 100:.0f}% good records")
{'missing id': 1, 'bad amount': 1, 'duplicate': 1} Quality score: 50% good records

How it works: Each record is checked in order and counted under its first problem. Real pipelines publish this score every day so a sudden drop (for example, a broken app release) is noticed before anyone trusts the numbers.

P8Business problem

Monthly cloud storage bill

A fintech stores 2.3 TB of new payment data every month and keeps all of it. Cloud storage costs Rs 1.80 per GB per month (illustrative). Print the bill for month 1 and month 12.

Input
2.3 TB per month, Rs 1.80/GB-month
Expected output
Month 1 : Rs 4239
Month 12 : Rs 50872
Show solution and explanation 
new_gb = 2.3 * 1024
rate = 1.80
for month in [1, 12]:
    stored = new_gb * month
    print("Month", month, ": Rs", round(stored * rate))
Month 1 : Rs 4239 Month 12 : Rs 50872

How it works: Data accumulates, so the bill grows every month. This is why companies set retention rules: Volume has a cost, so it must create Value.

P9Business problem

Average payments per second

A payments company processed 1,800 crore transactions last month (30 days). Print the average per second.

Input
18,000,000,000 in 30 days
Expected output
6944 per second on average
Show solution and explanation 
txns = 18_000_000_000
seconds = 30 * 24 * 3600
print(round(txns / seconds), "per second on average")
6944 per second on average

How it works: 30 × 24 × 3600 = 25,92,000 seconds in the month. Peaks are several times the average.

P10Business problem

Busiest hour for staffing

A canteen logs the hour of each order. Print the hour with the most orders so the owner can put an extra person on the counter.

Input
hours: 8, 9, 13, 13, 16, 13, 8, 16, 13
Expected output
Busiest hour: 13 with 4 orders
Show solution and explanation 
hours = [8, 9, 13, 13, 16, 13, 8, 16, 13]
count = {}
for h in hours:
    count[h] = count.get(h, 0) + 1
busiest = max(count, key=count.get)
print("Busiest hour:", busiest, "with", count[busiest], "orders")
Busiest hour: 13 with 4 orders

How it works: Count with a dictionary, then pick the key with the highest value. Data → decision is Value.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Situations freshers and junior data engineers really face. Decide your answer first, then open the model answer.

Retail · tool choiceFresher

“Let's set up Hadoop for our sales data”

A mid-sized retailer has 5 GB of sales data, growing by about 1 GB a year. A manager read about big data and wants to set up a Hadoop cluster. You are asked for your opinion.

The interviewer is checking: can you judge whether data is really “big”, instead of picking tools because they are popular?

Show a strong answer

5 GB growing at 1 GB a year is not big data. One database server (PostgreSQL or MySQL) or Python with Pandas handles it comfortably, and reports will run in seconds.

  • A cluster adds cost, setup and maintenance with no benefit at this size.
  • Put the data in a proper database, index it, and build reports or a BI dashboard on top.
  • Revisit if the data grows by orders of magnitude, for example if clickstream or real-time data is added.

Avoid saying: “More data tools are always better.” Use the simplest tool that meets the need.

Fintech · data qualityFresher

The dashboard shows more money than the bank

The daily revenue dashboard shows ₹12.4 lakh, but the bank settlement says ₹11.9 lakh. Engineering says the app sometimes retries payments when the network is slow.

The interviewer is checking: do you recognise a veracity problem and know how to fix it at the source and in the pipeline?

Show a strong answer

Retries are probably creating duplicate records for the same payment, so they are counted twice.

  1. Check for repeated transaction ids in the raw data for that day, and compare totals after removing them.
  2. De-duplicate in the pipeline on a unique key (transaction id) before calculating revenue.
  3. Fix the source: make the payment API idempotent, so a retry with the same id doesn't create a new record.
  4. Add a daily reconciliation check against the bank settlement that alerts when they differ.

Avoid: “adjusting” the dashboard number by hand without finding the cause.

E-commerce · storage cost1–3 years

“Keep everything forever, just in case”

Your team stores every click from the app forever. The storage bill grows every month, and nobody has used clicks older than a year. Your lead asks what you would propose.

The interviewer is checking: do you understand that Volume has a cost and must be balanced against Value, and that personal data has legal limits?

Show a strong answer

Data should be kept as long as it creates value or is legally required, and no longer.

  • Agree a retention policy with the business: for example, raw clicks for 90 days, daily summaries for years.
  • Move older data to cheaper archive storage, and compress it into columnar formats like Parquet.
  • Remove or anonymise personal data that isn't needed. Privacy laws such as India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 expect personal data not to be kept longer than necessary.

Avoid: deleting data without checking legal and audit requirements first.

Telecom · customer support1–3 years

Analysing call recordings with sales data

The support head wants to know why customers cancel. The evidence is spread across a sales database (tables), chat transcripts (JSON) and call recordings (audio files). How would you bring them together?

The interviewer is checking: can you handle Variety: different shapes of data that must be linked?

Show a strong answer

Store all three in a data lake in their raw form, then convert each into something that can be joined.

  • Audio → text with a speech-to-text service, then text → topics or sentiment.
  • Chat JSON → flatten into rows: customer id, time, message, topic.
  • Join everything on customer id with the sales tables, and compare customers who cancelled with those who stayed.

Avoid: forcing audio and JSON into one rigid table before you know which fields matter.
