Big data at Indian scale: UPI, IRCTC, live cricket and sale days

UPI moves around 80 crore payments a day, IRCTC has sold over 37,000 tickets in a single minute, and 5.9 crore people once watched the same cricket final at the same moment. In this lesson you turn those headline numbers into requests per second, follow one UPI payment through the network, watch a waiting room tame the Tatkal rush, see why a retry must never debit twice, and learn how live streams reach crores of phones.

2 Definition

Big data at Indian scale

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Large-scale systems serve very high numbers of users and transactions with strict requirements on throughput, latency, availability and correctness. Designing them involves estimating load (average and peak requests per second, data volume), and applying distributed-systems techniques: horizontal scaling, partitioning, replication, caching, content delivery networks, asynchronous messaging, rate limiting and load shedding, together with streaming and batch data platforms that capture every event for monitoring, fraud detection, analytics and audit.

In simple words

A system is at India scale when crores of people use it, often at the same moment. To survive, it spreads work over many machines, keeps copies, answers repeated questions from caches, makes people wait in fair queues during rushes, and records every action as data for later analysis.

Words you’ll meet in this lesson

TPS / RPSTransactions or requests per second: the basic unit of load.
Peak-to-average ratioHow many times busier the busiest second is than the average second.
ConcurrencyHow many users or requests are active at the same moment.
CDNContent delivery network: servers near users that cache and deliver content such as video segments.
Waiting roomA queue in front of a system that admits users at a rate the system can handle.
IdempotencyDoing the same request twice has the same effect as doing it once (no double debit).
Hot keyA single key (train, match, product) receiving a disproportionate share of traffic.
Load sheddingDeliberately rejecting or simplifying some work to protect the core under overload.

India-scale systems at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner What “India scale” means

  • Many users at the same moment: Tatkal at 10 am, the last over of a final, midnight sale launches. The problem is the peak, not the daily average.
  • Every request matters: a lost payment or a double-booked berth is a serious failure, so correctness is as important as speed.
  • Huge variety of devices and networks: cheap phones, 2G to 5G, many languages. Systems must be light and tolerant of slow connections.
  • All of it creates data: each payment, booking and video second becomes an event for fraud checks, dashboards, recommendations and reports.

Intermediate The patterns behind each system

  • UPI: a central switch (NPCI) routes requests between payment apps and banks; it is an online transaction system that needs strong consistency for money, with streaming fraud checks beside it.
  • IRCTC: a short, extreme peak; standard answers are waiting rooms, rate limits, caching of read-heavy pages (availability, fares), and a carefully protected seat-allocation core.
  • Live streaming: video is cut into small segments and pushed through CDNs (servers close to viewers), with adaptive bitrate; event data streams into analytics for ads and quality.
  • E-commerce sales: catalogue and search served from caches and search clusters, inventory and orders sharded and protected, clickstream into Kafka and a lakehouse for real-time dashboards and recommendations.

Advanced Design trade-offs at peak

  • Peak-to-average ratio: design capacity for the peak (often several times the average) plus headroom; pre-scale for known events instead of relying only on autoscaling, which may react too slowly.
  • Backpressure and load shedding: admit at a safe rate (queues, tokens), degrade gracefully (turn off recommendations before checkout), and fail fast instead of piling up timeouts.
  • Consistency where it matters: money and seats need strong consistency and idempotency (a retried payment must not debit twice); counters, views and recommendations can be eventually consistent.
  • Hot keys: one train, one match or one product can overload a single partition; caching, request coalescing and splitting hot keys spread the load.
  • Data platform behind it: every event goes to streams (for fraud, live dashboards) and to the lake (for reconciliation, reports, ML), often under data-residency rules.

Exam tip: For a case study, give scale figures (with source and date), convert them to TPS at average and peak, then explain the techniques used: horizontal scaling, partitioning, replication, caching/CDN, queues and rate limiting, and how events flow to a streaming and batch analytics platform.

4 Step by step

From a headline number to a design

  1. Start from a published number

    per_month = 24.07e9
per_day = per_month / 30
print(f"{per_day / 1e7:.1f} crore a day")
print(f"{per_day / 86_400:,.0f} per second on average")
    80.2 crore a day 9,286 per second on average
    Monthly or daily totals sound huge, but systems are designed in requests per second. 24 billion a month is about 9,300 per second on average.

  2. Estimate the peak

    avg_tps = 9_286
for ratio in [2, 3, 5]:
    print(f"peak x{ratio}: {avg_tps * ratio:,} TPS")
    peak x2: 18,572 TPS peak x3: 27,858 TPS peak x5: 46,430 TPS
    Traffic is not flat: evenings, salary days, festivals and sale launches are much busier. Designers use a peak-to-average ratio from history and add headroom on top.

  3. Find the critical path

    For UPI: app → switch → payer bank → payee bank → confirmations. For IRCTC: search → availability → seat allocation → payment. Only these steps must succeed in real time.

    Everything else (rewards, notifications, analytics) can be asynchronous through queues, so it never slows the critical path.

  4. Spread and protect the load

    Partition data and traffic (by account, train, region), replicate for failures, cache what is read often, and put a queue or rate limiter in front of anything that can be overwhelmed.

    These are the Module 3 ideas: scale out, shard, replicate, choose consistency per data type.

  5. Size the data platform

    tps, bytes_per_event = 9_286, 1_000
gb_per_day = tps * 86_400 * bytes_per_event / 1e9
print(f"{gb_per_day:,.0f} GB of events a day, {gb_per_day * 365 / 1e3:,.0f} TB a year")
    802 GB of events a day, 293 TB a year
    Each transaction creates events for fraud checks, dashboards and audit. Even small events add up to terabytes a year, before replication and logs.

  6. Rehearse the big day

    Load-test at the expected peak plus margin, pre-scale capacity before the event, prepare switches to turn off non-essential features, and watch live dashboards during the event.

    Known peaks (Tatkal, a final, a sale) are planned like launches, with game days and runbooks, not left to chance.
5 See it happen

One UPI payment: what Riya sees, and what happens behind

Step through the six frames. Left: Riya paying a tiffin shop. Right: a simplified view of the request moving through the UPI network.

What Riya sees, and what happens behind 6 steps
What you see
  1. Riya scans the tiffin shop’s QR code and taps Pay for ₹120.
  2. She enters her UPI PIN.
  3. The app shows “Processing”.
  4. Still processing, under two seconds so far.
  5. Payment successful, with a reference number.
  6. The shop’s speaker announces the payment and her bank sends an SMS.
Behind the screen
  1. Her UPI app creates a payment request with a unique transaction ID, so a network retry can never debit her twice.
  2. The request, with the PIN encrypted, goes to NPCI’s UPI switch, which routes it to Riya’s bank based on her UPI handle.
  3. Riya’s bank checks the PIN and balance and debits ₹120, then responds to the switch.
  4. The switch sends a credit request to the shop’s bank. If the credit fails or times out, the debit is reversed: money must never vanish.
  5. Confirmations go back to both sides. This is one of roughly 9,300 UPI payments in an average second in September 2026, and many more at peak.
  6. Every step is recorded. These events feed fraud and risk models, live monitoring, daily settlement between banks and dispute handling. (Simplified flow; real UPI has more checks and messages.)
6 Real-world use

Real-time scenarios: rush, retry and live stream

Three live scenarios: the Tatkal waiting room (real numbers from the program below), a payment retry at peak that must not debit twice, and an advanced look at serving crores of live cricket viewers.

Real-time scenario 1

Tatkal at 10 am: a waiting room protects the booking system

These figures come from the program in “Run it on your PC” below: 30,000 users arrive in one minute, but the booking system admits only 250 per second. Watch the queue absorb the spike.

Virtual waiting room 5 steps
Animated example
  1. Just before 10 am the booking system is ready. Load tests say it can safely handle about 250 users entering the booking flow per second (an illustrative number).
  2. At 10:00 thousands arrive every second. Instead of letting them all hit the servers (which would make everything time out), a waiting room queues them and admits 250 per second.
  3. After one minute 30,000 users have arrived. Half have been admitted; the rest see a progress message with their position.
  4. No new users arrive after the first minute, and the queue drains at a steady 250 per second.
  5. Everyone is admitted after 120 seconds. The median user waited 30 seconds and the unluckiest 60, while the booking servers never saw more than 250 users a second. A fair, predictable wait beats a crash.
Real-time scenario 2

A retry at peak must not debit twice

At peak, slow links and timeouts are normal. Watch how a unique transaction ID makes a retry safe, which is the core reason payment systems can scale out without losing money.

Idempotent payments 5 steps
Animated example
  1. It is a salary-day evening and traffic is far above average. A ₹500 request with transaction ID 7781 goes from the app through the switch to the bank.
  2. The bank debits ₹500, but its response is delayed by congestion somewhere on the path.
  3. The app (or switch) times out and retries the request, carrying the same transaction ID.
  4. The bank stores processed IDs; it recognises 7781 and returns the earlier result instead of debiting again.
  5. The user is charged exactly once. In real UPI, status-check and reversal flows plus daily reconciliation catch any remaining mismatch. Idempotency keys are a standard pattern in every payment system.
Real-time scenario 3 · advanced

Crores join the same match in minutes

Live streaming is the opposite of payments: everyone reads the same data. The hard parts are bandwidth, sudden joins and keeping quality up. Watch the standard design at work.

Live streaming at peak 5 steps
Animated example
  1. The live video is encoded into short segments (a few seconds each) at several qualities. Edge servers of content delivery networks cache segments close to viewers, so the origin serves each segment only a few times.
  2. Big moments are predictable, so capacity is reserved in advance across more than one CDN, instead of waiting for autoscaling to react.
  3. In the 2023 ICC World Cup final, Disney+ Hotstar reported a peak of 5.9 crore concurrent viewers. At even 2.5 Mbps each, that is roughly 148 Tbps of video (our program’s estimate), which only a wide CDN footprint can deliver.
  4. If one edge or network is overloaded, traffic is steered elsewhere and adaptive bitrate lowers quality instead of freezing. Non-essential features (comments, extra stats) can be switched off.
  5. Every player sends events (start, buffering, bitrate changes, ad views) into a streaming pipeline, powering live concurrency dashboards, quality alerts and ad billing, and later the lake for analysis.

Four systems, four lessons

1. UPI: correctness at thousands of TPS

A central switch connects hundreds of banks and many payment apps. Each payment is small but must be exactly-once in effect; streams of events feed fraud checks and settlement.

App→NPCI switch→Banks + settlement

2. IRCTC: survive a fixed-time rush

Tatkal creates a predictable spike on limited seats. Waiting rooms, caching of availability, bot controls and a protected booking core keep it fair; IRCTC has announced upgrades for higher booking rates.

Users at 10 am→Queue + rate limit→Seat allocation

3. Live cricket: bandwidth and sudden joins

Segmented video and multi-CDN delivery serve crores of identical reads. Playback events stream into analytics for quality and ad billing.

Encoder→Multi-CDN edges→Crores of phones

4. Sale days: protect checkout

E-commerce sales (such as Flipkart’s Big Billion Days and Amazon’s Great Indian Festival) cache the catalogue, shard inventory and orders, queue order processing and switch off extras, while clickstream feeds live dashboards.

Browse (cached)→Cart + checkout→Orders queue

Run it on your PC: India-scale load and a Tatkal waiting room

This program converts published UPI, IRCTC and live-streaming figures into per-second load and bandwidth, then simulates a virtual waiting room: 30,000 users arrive in one minute and are admitted at 250 per second. Change the arrival count or admit rate and run it again. Save it as indian_scale.py.

From headlines to TPS, and a waiting room

"""India-scale numbers turned into per-second rates, and a Tatkal-style
virtual waiting room that admits users at a safe rate."""
import random

published = {
    # figure, unit, source (see the lesson's Sources list)
    "UPI, September 2026": (24.07e9, "transactions in the month"),
    "IRCTC, peak minute (16 Aug 2025)": (37_410, "tickets in one minute"),
    "Live cricket, 2023 World Cup final": (5.9e7, "concurrent viewers"),
}

upi_per_s = 24.07e9 / (30 * 86_400)
irctc_per_s = 37_410 / 60
viewers = 5.9e7
bitrate_mbps = 2.5                       # assumption: an average mobile stream
print(f"UPI average           : {upi_per_s:,.0f} transactions per second")
print(f"IRCTC peak            : {irctc_per_s:,.0f} tickets per second")
print(f"Live final, bandwidth : {viewers * bitrate_mbps / 1e6:,.0f} Tbps if every viewer streams {bitrate_mbps} Mbps")

# ---- a virtual waiting room for a 10:00 am rush
random.seed(3)
arrivals = sorted(random.uniform(0, 60) for _ in range(30_000))   # 30,000 users in the first minute
ADMIT_PER_SECOND = 250                                            # what the booking system can safely take
admitted_at = []
next_slot = 0.0
for t in arrivals:
    start = max(t, next_slot)
    admitted_at.append(start)
    next_slot = start + 1 / ADMIT_PER_SECOND
waits = [a - t for a, t in zip(admitted_at, arrivals)]
print(f"\nWaiting room: {len(arrivals):,} users arrive in 60 s, admitted at {ADMIT_PER_SECOND}/s")
print(f"  last user admitted after {admitted_at[-1]:.0f} s")
print(f"  median wait {sorted(waits)[len(waits) // 2]:.0f} s, worst wait {max(waits):.0f} s")
print("  the booking servers never see more than 250 users per second")

# In a terminal:  python indian_scale.py

Quick revision

Lesson 4.7 in one screen

  • Plan for the peak (peak-to-average ratio) plus headroom; pre-scale for known events.
  • Separate the critical path (pay, book, play, order) from extras; queue or switch off extras under load.

Interview and viva questions

How does UPI handle so many transactions?

UPI connects payment apps and banks through NPCI’s central switch, which routes each request to the right banks. Scale comes from horizontally scaled, replicated systems at NPCI and at banks, short and well-defined messages, unique transaction IDs that make retries safe, timeouts with reversal flows, and daily settlement and reconciliation. Detailed internal designs are not fully public.

Why does the IRCTC site slow down at Tatkal time?

Tatkal booking opens at fixed times (10 am for AC classes, 11 am for non-AC), so a very large number of users arrive within seconds for limited seats. The peak is many times the daily average. Systems handle it with queues or waiting rooms, rate limits, caching of availability and fares, bot controls and capacity upgrades; IRCTC has announced plans to handle over one lakh bookings a minute.

How do streaming apps handle crores of concurrent viewers?

Video is encoded into short segments at multiple bitrates and delivered through content delivery networks with servers close to viewers, often several CDNs at once. Capacity is reserved before big events, players adapt quality to network conditions, and non-essential features can be switched off. Player events stream into analytics for quality monitoring and ad delivery.

What is idempotency and why is it important in payments?

An operation is idempotent if doing it more than once has the same effect as doing it once. In payments, each request carries a unique ID; if a timeout causes a retry, the server recognises the ID and returns the original result rather than debiting again. It makes retries, which are unavoidable in distributed systems, safe.

How do you estimate load in a system design interview?

Start from users or a published total, convert to per-second averages (divide by 86,400 for a day), apply a peak-to-average ratio from experience or history, add headroom, and estimate data size per request and per day. State every assumption clearly and round sensibly; interviewers care more about the reasoning than exact numbers.

Expected output
['t1', 't2', 't3']
Show solution and explanation 
ids = ["t1", "t2", "t1", "t3", "t2"]
print(list(dict.fromkeys(ids)))

How it works: dict.fromkeys keeps first occurrences in order; this is deduplication by ID.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Questions about India-scale systems from interviews and real projects. Decide your answer first, then open the model answer.

System design · estimationFresher

“Estimate the load on UPI”

An interviewer says: UPI handles about 24 billion transactions a month. Estimate average and peak TPS and daily event data.

The interviewer is checking: can you turn a headline number into engineering numbers, stating assumptions?

Show a strong answer
  1. Per day: 24 billion ÷ 30 ≈ 80 crore.
  2. Average TPS: 80 crore ÷ 86,400 ≈ 9,300.
  3. Peak: assume 3–5× average at busy hours and festivals → roughly 30,000–45,000 TPS to design for, plus headroom.
  4. Events: if each transaction produces ~10 events of ~1 KB across systems, that is ~8 TB a day before replication.
  5. State assumptions clearly and say how you would validate them (published peak figures, traffic history).

Avoid: giving one number with no assumptions, or designing only for the average.

System design · peaks1–3 years

Design for Tatkal-style rush hour

Your company runs a ticket sale that opens at a fixed time; last year the site crashed in the first minute.

The interviewer is checking: do you know how to protect a system from a predictable spike?

Show a strong answer
  • Estimate peak arrivals from last year and load-test above that.
  • Put a virtual waiting room and rate limits in front of the booking flow.
  • Cache read-heavy pages (availability, fares) and serve static content from a CDN.
  • Pre-scale capacity before opening time; don’t rely only on autoscaling.
  • Protect inventory with strong consistency and short seat holds; stop bots with captcha and limits.
  • Prepare switches to turn off non-essential features; watch live dashboards.

Avoid: just adding more servers to the database.

Payments · correctness1–3 years

A customer was charged twice

During a peak, a few customers were debited twice for one order after network timeouts.

The interviewer is checking: do you understand idempotency and reconciliation?

Show a strong answer

Retries after a timeout re-sent the payment as a new request. Fix: generate an idempotency key (unique transaction ID) on the client, send it with every retry, and store processed keys on the server so a repeat returns the original result. Add a status-check call before retrying, automatic reversal for failed flows, and a daily reconciliation job comparing your records with the bank or gateway settlement files to catch and refund mismatches.

Avoid: removing retries entirely, which turns network blips into failed payments.

Case study · interviewFresher

“How does Hotstar handle crores of live viewers?”

A common case-study question for data and platform roles.

The interviewer is checking: can you explain the standard pattern honestly, without claiming insider knowledge?

Show a strong answer

Explain that internal details are only partly public, then describe the standard design: video encoded into short segments at multiple bitrates (adaptive streaming), cached and delivered by multiple CDNs close to viewers, origin protected by caching, capacity pre-provisioned before big matches, graceful degradation (lower bitrate, switch off extras) and player events streamed into analytics for concurrency, quality and ad billing. Quote a published figure with source, such as the 5.9 crore concurrent peak in the 2023 World Cup final.

Avoid: inventing exact server counts or claiming to know their internal architecture.
