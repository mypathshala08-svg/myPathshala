2 Definition

The data engineering interview

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A technical interview for data engineering evaluates a candidate’s ability to query and transform data (SQL), write correct code (usually Python), explain distributed data processing and storage concepts, design reliable pipelines and platforms under constraints, and communicate decisions. A typical process includes an online assessment, one or more technical rounds, a design round, a project or managerial discussion and a behavioural/HR round, each assessed against a rubric of correctness, depth, trade-off reasoning and communication.

In simple words

Interviews check whether you can write SQL and Python correctly, explain how big data tools work and why, design a pipeline that won’t break, and talk clearly about your own work. Each round looks at one of these.

Words you’ll meet in this lesson

Online assessmentA timed test (SQL, Python, multiple choice) used to shortlist candidates.
Live codingSolving a problem while sharing your screen and explaining your thinking.
Design roundDesigning a pipeline or data platform for a scenario, discussing trade-offs.
RubricThe criteria an interviewer scores: correctness, depth, communication, trade-offs.
Follow-upA deeper question building on your answer: “what if the data were 10× bigger?”
Clarifying questionA question you ask before solving, to confirm requirements and edge cases.
STARSituation, Task, Action, Result: structure for behavioural answers.
Bar raiserAn interviewer from another team whose job is to keep the hiring standard high.

Interview preparation at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Fresher: the must-knows

  • SQL: joins (inner, left, full, self), GROUP BY with HAVING, subqueries, window functions (ROW_NUMBER, RANK, DENSE_RANK, LAG, LEAD, running totals), deduplication.
  • Python: lists, dicts, sets, string handling, file reading, functions, exceptions, comprehensions, simple algorithms (counting, grouping, sorting).
  • Concepts: 5 Vs, batch vs streaming, OLTP vs OLAP, ETL vs ELT, data lake vs warehouse vs lakehouse, row vs columnar formats.
  • Your project: explain it in 2 minutes with architecture, scale, one hard problem and results.

Intermediate 1–3 years: depth and trade-offs

  • Spark: driver and executors, lazy evaluation, transformations vs actions, narrow vs wide, shuffles, partitions, broadcast joins, skew, caching, AQE.
  • Kafka and streaming: topics, partitions, offsets, consumer groups, delivery semantics, watermarks and late data.
  • Modelling and warehousing: star vs snowflake schema, facts and dimensions, slowly changing dimensions (SCD type 1 and 2), partitioning and clustering.
  • Pipelines: Airflow DAGs, idempotency, backfills, incremental loads, data quality checks, CDC.

Advanced Senior: design and ownership

  • System design: requirements, estimates, architecture, storage and processing choices, failure handling, cost and governance, in a structured 45 minutes.
  • Distributed systems: CAP, consistency, replication, exactly-once end to end, schema evolution, small-files problems.
  • Operations: SLAs, observability, on-call, incident post-mortems, cost optimisation.
  • Leadership: design reviews, mentoring, disagreeing constructively, data contracts across teams.

Exam tip: For any interview question: clarify (inputs, scale, edge cases), state assumptions, answer step by step, give a small example, then add trade-offs or limits. This structure also earns full marks in long-answer exam questions.

4 Step by step

How to answer any technical question

  1. Clarify the question

    “Top orders per city: should ties share a rank? Should duplicate rows count once? Any date range?”

    Two or three clarifying questions show care and often reveal the edge case the interviewer is waiting for.

  2. State assumptions and scale

    “I’ll assume order_id is unique after deduplication, and the table has around 10 crore rows partitioned by date.”

    Assumptions make your answer checkable and set up good follow-ups.

  3. Solve simply first

    rows = [("Pune", 900), ("Pune", 450), ("Pune", 320), ("Thane", 610)]
best = {}
for city, amt in rows:
    best[city] = max(best.get(city, 0), amt)
print(best)
    {'Pune': 900, 'Thane': 610}
    Get a correct, simple solution, then improve it. A working brute-force answer beats an unfinished clever one.

  4. Test with edge cases

    Walk through NULL amounts, ties, a city with one order and an empty table. Fix anything that breaks.

    Interviewers note whether you test without being asked.

  5. Discuss scale and trade-offs

    “On 10 crore rows I’d filter by date partition first; in Spark, this window needs a shuffle by city, and a hot city could cause skew.”

    Connecting a small answer to big data behaviour is what separates data engineers from general developers.

  6. Summarise

    End with one sentence: what you did, its complexity or cost, and what you’d do with more time.

    A clean summary leaves a strong final impression.

Question bank with model answers

Open each question only after answering it aloud yourself. Model answers are short; in an interview, add an example from your own work.

SQL

Difference between WHERE and HAVING?

WHERE filters rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after aggregation, so it can use aggregates like COUNT(*) > 1.

ROW_NUMBER vs RANK vs DENSE_RANK?

For ties on 610, 610, 450: ROW_NUMBER gives 1, 2, 3 (arbitrary order for ties); RANK gives 1, 1, 3 (gaps); DENSE_RANK gives 1, 1, 2 (no gaps).

How do you remove duplicate rows but keep one?

Use ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY the key columns ORDER BY a tiebreaker such as load time DESC) and keep rows where the number is 1; or SELECT DISTINCT when entire rows are identical.

INNER vs LEFT vs FULL OUTER JOIN?

INNER keeps matching rows only; LEFT keeps all rows from the left table with NULLs where there is no match; FULL OUTER keeps all rows from both sides.

What happens to NULLs in COUNT, SUM and joins?

COUNT(column) and SUM ignore NULLs, COUNT(*) counts rows; NULL never equals NULL, so NULL keys don’t match in joins unless handled explicitly.

How would you find the second highest salary?

SELECT MAX(salary) FROM t WHERE salary < (SELECT MAX(salary) FROM t), or DENSE_RANK() and filter rank = 2. Ask how ties should be handled.

Python

List vs tuple vs set vs dict?

List: ordered, mutable. Tuple: ordered, immutable (usable as a dict key). Set: unordered unique items with fast membership. Dict: key-value pairs with fast lookup by key.

How do you read a 20 GB file that doesn’t fit in memory?

Stream it line by line or in chunks (a for loop over the file, pandas read_csv with chunksize, or pyarrow batches), process each chunk and aggregate; or use Spark or DuckDB.

What is a generator and why use it in data pipelines?

A function that yields values one at a time with yield, keeping memory low because items are produced lazily, ideal for streaming through large files or API pages.

How do you handle errors in a pipeline script?

Catch specific exceptions, log context (file, row, ID), retry transient errors with backoff, send bad records to a quarantine location and fail loudly on unexpected errors rather than silently continuing.

Count word frequency in a text file.

Use collections.Counter over words from each line: Counter(w for line in f for w in line.lower().split()).most_common(10).

Big data concepts

Explain the 5 Vs.

Volume (size), Velocity (speed of arrival), Variety (structured, semi-structured, unstructured), Veracity (quality and trust) and Value (business benefit). Give an example for each from a real system.

ETL vs ELT?

ETL transforms data before loading into the target; ELT loads raw data first and transforms inside a scalable warehouse or lakehouse, which is common in the cloud because storage is cheap and engines are powerful.

Data lake vs data warehouse vs lakehouse?

A lake stores raw files of any type cheaply; a warehouse stores structured, modelled data for fast SQL; a lakehouse adds table formats (Delta, Iceberg, Hudi) to lake storage for ACID transactions, schema enforcement and warehouse-like performance.

Why are columnar formats like Parquet faster for analytics?

Queries read only the needed columns, similar values compress well, and min/max statistics let engines skip row groups, so far less data is read.

What is partitioning and what is the small-files problem?

Partitioning splits data by a column (often date) so queries skip irrelevant folders. Too many tiny files, from over-partitioning or frequent small writes, slow down listing and reading; fix with compaction and sensible partition choices.

Explain the CAP theorem.

During a network partition a distributed system must choose between consistency (every read sees the latest write) and availability (every request gets a response). Payments favour consistency; feeds and counters often favour availability.

Spark

Driver vs executor?

The driver plans the job and schedules tasks; executors on worker nodes run tasks on partitions and hold cached data.

Transformations vs actions; what is lazy evaluation?

Transformations (select, filter, join) build a plan; actions (count, write, collect) trigger execution. Laziness lets Spark optimise the whole plan, for example pushing filters down.

Narrow vs wide transformations?

Narrow ones (map, filter) work within a partition; wide ones (groupBy, join, distinct) need a shuffle to bring rows with the same key together.

repartition vs coalesce?

repartition does a full shuffle to any number of partitions with even sizes; coalesce reduces partitions by merging without a full shuffle, cheaper but possibly uneven.

What is a broadcast join?

Sending a small table to every executor so the big table can be joined without shuffling it. Useful when one side fits comfortably in memory.

How do you handle data skew?

Find hot keys in the Spark UI and key counts, then filter bad keys, salt hot keys, broadcast the small side, or enable adaptive query execution’s skew handling.

cache vs persist?

cache stores a DataFrame in memory (default storage level); persist lets you choose levels such as memory and disk. Use it only when the result is reused several times, and unpersist after.

Kafka and streaming

Topic, partition, offset, consumer group?

A topic is a named stream; partitions split it for parallelism and ordering within a partition; an offset is a message’s position; consumers in a group share partitions, so each message is processed by one consumer in the group.

At-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once?

At-most-once may lose messages; at-least-once may duplicate them; exactly-once means each message affects results once, achieved with idempotent producers, transactions or idempotent sinks plus checkpoints.

What are watermarks?

A threshold for how late event-time data may arrive; events older than the watermark are dropped or handled separately so windowed state can be finalised and freed.

How does Kafka keep messages in order?

Order is guaranteed only within a partition, so events that must stay ordered (for example, for one order ID) use the same key, which maps to the same partition.

Batch or streaming for a daily finance report?

Batch: it needs complete, reconciled data once a day. Streaming fits fraud alerts or live dashboards where seconds matter.

Data modelling and pipelines

Star vs snowflake schema?

A star schema has a central fact table joined directly to denormalised dimension tables; a snowflake normalises dimensions into more tables. Star is simpler and faster for BI.

What is a slowly changing dimension type 2?

A dimension that keeps history by inserting a new row with valid_from/valid_to (or is_current) when an attribute such as a customer’s city changes, instead of overwriting it (type 1).

What makes a pipeline idempotent?

Running it twice for the same input gives the same result: overwrite a date partition or MERGE on keys instead of blindly appending.

Full load vs incremental load vs CDC?

Full reloads everything; incremental loads only new or changed rows using a watermark column; CDC reads the database’s change log to capture inserts, updates and deletes reliably.

What is a backfill?

Re-running a pipeline for past dates, for example after a bug fix or a new column. Airflow supports it when tasks are parameterised by date and idempotent.

Behavioural and HR

Tell me about a time something broke in production.

Use STAR: the situation (dashboard 100× wrong), your task, the actions (traced lineage, found a unit change, fixed and backfilled, added a check and a contract) and the result, plus what you learnt.

Tell me about a disagreement with a teammate.

Show that you listened, used data or a small experiment to decide, and kept the relationship good. Avoid blaming.

Why data engineering?

Connect a real experience (building a pipeline others relied on) to what you enjoy: reliable systems, scale and enabling decisions.

Where do you see yourself in a few years?

Be honest and role-related: deeper expertise in streaming or platforms, owning systems end to end, mentoring others.

 ('O1','Asha','Pune',320,'2026-10-01'), ('O2','Ravi','Pune',450,'2026-10-01'),
 ('O3','Meera','Thane',610,'2026-10-02'), ('O4','Asha','Pune',150,'2026-10-02'),

    "Q1. Second highest order amount (handles ties)":

Interview and viva questions

What are the most common data engineering interview questions?

SQL problems with joins, aggregation and window functions (top-N per group, duplicates, running totals); Python data-handling questions; concepts such as ETL vs ELT, data lake vs warehouse, Parquet and partitioning; Spark questions on shuffles, partitions, joins and skew; Kafka basics; data modelling with star schemas and slowly changing dimensions; a pipeline design scenario; and questions about your projects and behaviour.

How should I prepare for a data engineering interview in 30 days?

Practise SQL daily (window functions especially), revise Python data structures and file handling, review Spark and Kafka concepts with diagrams, practise two or three design scenarios with a fixed structure, prepare a two-minute project story and five STAR stories, and do mock interviews where you explain your thinking aloud.

What Spark questions are asked in interviews?

Typical questions cover the driver and executors, lazy evaluation, transformations and actions, narrow and wide dependencies, shuffles, partitioning and repartition versus coalesce, broadcast joins, handling data skew, caching, adaptive query execution, file formats and debugging slow or failing jobs using the Spark UI.

How do I answer a system design question for data pipelines?

Clarify requirements (latency, volume, history, correctness, consumers), estimate scale with simple arithmetic, propose an architecture from ingestion to storage, processing and serving, explain failure handling (retries, idempotency, late data, reconciliation), and discuss cost, security and governance, ending with a short summary and possible improvements.

What if I don’t know the answer to an interview question?

Say so honestly, then reason from what you do know, relate it to a similar tool or concept, and describe how you would find out. Interviewers value clear reasoning and honesty over confident guessing.

Practice problems

Interview-style problems in Python and SQL. The last two are business-flavoured design questions.

P1Easy

Reverse words

Print the words of "big data is fun" in reverse order.

Input
"big data is fun"
Expected output
fun is data big
Show solution and explanation 
print(" ".join("big data is fun".split()[::-1]))
fun is data big

How it works: Split, reverse the list, join.

P2Easy

Second largest

Print the second largest distinct number in [5, 9, 9, 3, 7].

Input
list
Expected output
7
Show solution and explanation 
print(sorted(set([5, 9, 9, 3, 7]))[-2])
7

How it works: Remove duplicates first so a tie for the maximum doesn’t count twice.

P3Easy

Count with HAVING

With SQLite, print the cities with more than 1 order from [Pune, Pune, Thane].

Input
3 rows
Expected output
[('Pune',)]
Show solution and explanation 
import sqlite3
db = sqlite3.connect(":memory:")
db.executescript("CREATE TABLE o (c TEXT); INSERT INTO o VALUES ('Pune'),('Pune'),('Thane');")
print(db.execute("SELECT c FROM o GROUP BY c HAVING COUNT(*) > 1").fetchall())
[('Pune',)]

How it works: HAVING filters after grouping.

P4Medium

First non-repeating character

Print the first character that appears only once in "aabbcdc".

Input
"aabbcdc"
Expected output
d
Show solution and explanation 
from collections import Counter
s = "aabbcdc"
c = Counter(s)
print(next(ch for ch in s if c[ch] == 1))
d

How it works: Count first, then scan in order.

P5Medium

Merge two sorted lists

Merge [1, 4, 7] and [2, 3, 8] into one sorted list without calling sort.

Input
two sorted lists
Expected output
[1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8]
Show solution and explanation 
a, b, out, i, j = [1, 4, 7], [2, 3, 8], [], 0, 0
while i < len(a) and j < len(b):
    if a[i] <= b[j]:
        out.append(a[i]); i += 1
    else:
        out.append(b[j]); j += 1
print(out + a[i:] + b[j:])
[1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8]

How it works: The merge step of merge sort; external sorting in big data works the same way.

P6Advanced

Latest status per order

From [("O1",1,"placed"),("O1",3,"delivered"),("O1",2,"shipped")], print the status with the highest version.

Input
versioned records
Expected output
delivered
Show solution and explanation 
r = [("O1", 1, "placed"), ("O1", 3, "delivered"), ("O1", 2, "shipped")]
print(max(r, key=lambda x: x[1])[2])
delivered

How it works: The same logic as ROW_NUMBER() ... ORDER BY version DESC = 1, or a MERGE in a lakehouse.

P7Advanced

Sessionise clicks

Click times (minutes) [0, 5, 12, 50, 52, 120]. A new session starts after a gap over 30. Print the number of sessions.

Input
times
Expected output
3
Show solution and explanation 
t = [0, 5, 12, 50, 52, 120]
print(1 + sum(1 for a, b in zip(t, t[1:]) if b - a > 30))
3

How it works: Sessionisation is a common streaming and SQL interview problem (LAG + gap rule).

P8Business problem

Estimate for a design round

50 lakh orders a day, 2 KB each, kept 3 years, 3 copies. Print storage in TB.

Input
as given
Expected output
32.9 TB
Show solution and explanation 
print(round(50e5 * 2e3 * 365 * 3 * 3 / 1e12, 1), "TB")
32.9 TB

How it works: Quick estimates like this anchor every design discussion.

P9Business problem

Peak throughput

Lunch (12–2 pm) carries 40% of 50 lakh daily orders. Print average orders per second in that window.

Input
40% in 2 hours
Expected output
278
Show solution and explanation 
print(round(50e5 * 0.40 / (2 * 3600)))
278

How it works: Peak windows, not daily averages, decide capacity.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

More interview situations to practise. Say your answer aloud first, then open the model answer.

SQL · live codingFresher

“Find customers who ordered on consecutive days.”

An interviewer gives an orders table (customer, order_date) and asks for customers with orders on two consecutive days.

The interviewer is checking: can you use window functions beyond ranking?

Show a strong answer

Deduplicate to one row per customer per day, then use LAG(order_date) OVER (PARTITION BY customer ORDER BY order_date) and keep rows where the difference from the previous date is exactly one day. Mention edge cases: several orders on the same day, time zones, and performance (partition by date range on large tables).

Avoid: self-joining every row with every other row without explaining the cost.

Spark · concepts1–3 years

“Your Spark job fails with OutOfMemory.”

An executor fails with an out-of-memory error during a join.

The interviewer is checking: can you reason from symptoms to causes?

Show a strong answer
  • Check the Spark UI: which stage, how large are partitions, is one task much bigger (skew)?
  • Look for collect() or toPandas() pulling data to the driver.
  • Fixes: more partitions, broadcast only genuinely small tables, salt skewed keys, select fewer columns, avoid caching large unused data, increase executor memory as a last resort.

Avoid: immediately doubling memory without finding the cause.

System design1–3 years

“Design a daily sales pipeline from 200 stores.”

Each store uploads a CSV of sales every night. Head office needs a dashboard by 7 am.

The interviewer is checking: can you structure a batch design with failure handling?

Show a strong answer
  1. Requirements: ~200 files a night, sizes, 7 am SLA, late or missing files.
  2. Land raw files in object storage by date and store (bronze).
  3. Airflow DAG: sensor for files with a deadline, validation and quality checks, Spark or dbt into silver and gold, idempotent overwrite per date.
  4. Alerts for missing stores, quarantine for bad files, backfill support.
  5. Serve gold tables to the dashboard; document owners and definitions.

Avoid: jumping to Kafka and streaming for a once-a-day requirement.

BehaviouralFresher

“What is your biggest weakness?”

Asked in many HR and manager rounds.

The interviewer is checking: can you be honest and show growth?

Show a strong answer

Pick a real, non-critical weakness and show what you are doing about it: “I used to jump into coding before clarifying requirements. After a pipeline I built had to be redone, I now write a one-page design note and confirm it before starting.” Keep it short and specific.

Avoid: a disguised strength like “I work too hard”.
