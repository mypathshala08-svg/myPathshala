1 The story

Forty minutes on a Friday night

Before you start: 5.1 (AZs) and 5.3.

🤔 AWS promises 99.99% for EC2. TiffinGo was down for 40 minutes on a Friday night. Does AWS owe it money?

Probably a service credit — not compensation for lost orders. An SLA is a contract with precise definitions: 99.99% allows only 4.3 minutes of downtime a month, but only for deployments in two or more Availability Zones, and a miss returns 10% of that month’s EC2 bill (30% below 99%, 100% below 95%). Also, three 99.99% / 99.99% / 99.95% components in a chain give only 99.93% together. This lesson teaches how to read an SLA, calculate availability and design to meet your own targets.

2 Definition

Service level agreements (and SLOs and SLIs)

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A service level agreement (SLA) is a formal, usually contractual commitment between a service provider and a customer that defines the expected level of service — such as monthly uptime — how it is measured, the remedies (typically service credits) if it is not met, and exclusions. It is supported internally by service level objectives (SLOs), target values for service levels, measured by service level indicators (SLIs).

In simple words

An SLA is the provider’s written promise about reliability and what you get back if it breaks the promise. SLOs are your own targets; SLIs are the measurements.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “An SLA is a contractual commitment — for example EC2 promises 99.99% monthly uptime for multi-AZ deployments — with service credits if it is missed and a list of exclusions. It limits the provider’s liability; it doesn’t cover my lost revenue.”
  • “Internally we set SLOs, stricter than any SLA, measured by SLIs such as the share of successful requests under 300 ms, and manage risk with an error budget.”
  • “Composite availability multiplies in series — web, app and DB at 99.99, 99.99 and 99.95 give about 99.93% — so we add redundancy across AZs where it is cheap.”

Type 1 · Service-based SLA (public cloud SLA)

Definition (say it in an interview): A service-based SLA is a single agreement that applies the same commitments to all customers of a particular service — for a cloud provider, the published service level agreement for each service, specifying the availability commitment, how it is measured, the service credits and the exclusions.

In plain words (no jargon): The cloud company publishes, for each service, how reliable it promises to be and what it gives back if it fails. Everyone gets the same promise.

Analogy: The warranty card that comes with a TV: every buyer gets the same terms, printed in advance; you cannot negotiate it at the shop.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Published per service. Each service has an SLA document with a version and effective date.
  2. Commitment. A monthly uptime percentage (e.g. 99.99%).
  3. Measurement. A precise definition of “unavailable” and how minutes are counted.
  4. Remedy. Service credit tiers as a percentage of that service’s monthly charge.
  5. Exclusions. Situations not covered (your errors, force majeure, previews).
  6. Claim process. How and by when to request a credit.

Words used above, in simple terms: SLA = service level agreement: a contractual promise · commitment = the promised uptime percentage · service credit = money off future bills, not a cash refund

The AWS EC2 SLA (as of Nov 2026) commits to 99.99% Region-level monthly uptime for instances in two or more AZs and 99.5% for an individual instance, with credits of 10%, 30% or 100% depending on how far uptime falls.

Real scenario (TiffinGo)

Use it when: using any public cloud service — know the SLA of every component on your critical path.
Avoid / be careful when: assuming a service has an SLA at all (some features, previews and free tiers have none) or that the headline number applies to your design.

Type 2 · Customer-based and multi-level SLA

Definition (say it in an interview): A customer-based SLA covers all the services provided to one particular customer under a single negotiated agreement; a multi-level SLA structures commitments in layers — corporate (all customers), customer (one customer) and service (each service for that customer).

In plain words (no jargon): A special agreement written for one big customer, often in layers: general rules, rules for this customer, and targets for each service they use.

Analogy: A wedding caterer’s contract with one family: general terms everyone signs, plus special terms for this family, plus details for each event (mehendi, sangeet, reception).

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Negotiate. Customer and provider agree targets, measurements, penalties and reporting.
  2. Layer. Corporate terms + customer terms + per-service targets.
  3. Back with OLAs. Internal operational level agreements and supplier SLAs must support each promise.
  4. Report. Regular SLA reports and reviews.
  5. Penalties/credits. Applied as agreed if targets are missed.

Words used above, in simple terms: ITIL = a widely used framework for IT service management · OLA = operational level agreement between internal teams · MSP = managed service provider

For the experienced — did you know? Your SLA can never safely exceed the composite availability of everything beneath it (type 6). A 99.99% customer promise on top of a single-AZ 99.5% instance is a promise you cannot keep.

Use it when: you provide services to large customers or receive managed services from partners.
Avoid / be careful when: promising more than your providers’ SLAs and your architecture can support — your SLA must be backed by your design.

Type 3 · SLO (service level objective)

Definition (say it in an interview): A service level objective is an internal target value or range for a service level, measured by an SLI over a period — for example, “99.95% of order requests succeed in a rolling 30 days” — used by engineering teams to make reliability decisions; it is usually stricter than any external SLA.

In plain words (no jargon): A goal your own team sets for how reliable a service should be — usually a bit stricter than what you promise customers, so you have room for mistakes.

Analogy: Your own exam target: the college passes you at 40% (SLA), but you aim for 75% (SLO) so a bad day still keeps you safely above 40.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Pick what users feel. Availability and latency of key user journeys.
  2. Choose an SLI. How you will measure it (type 4).
  3. Set the target and window. e.g. 99.95% over 30 days.
  4. Derive the error budget. 100% − SLO (type 5).
  5. Review. Weekly SLO reports drive priorities.

Words used above, in simple terms: SLO = service level objective: internal target · rolling window = the last N days, moving every day · user journey = a task a user performs, like placing an order

Avoid / be careful when: setting 100% (impossible) or SLOs nobody measures or acts on.

Type 4 · SLI (service level indicator)

Definition (say it in an interview): A service level indicator is a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the service level, typically expressed as the ratio of good events to valid events — for example, the proportion of requests served successfully within a latency threshold.

In plain words (no jargon): The number that tells you how well the service actually performed — for example, the percentage of orders that went through quickly.

Analogy: A speedometer: the number on the dial is the indicator; the speed limit is the objective; the fine for overspeeding is the agreement.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Define good. Status codes, latency threshold, correctness.
  2. Choose where to measure. Load balancer, client, synthetic probes from outside.
  3. Collect. Count good and total events per window.
  4. Compute. good ÷ total, per window and per month.
  5. Alert. Alert on fast SLO burn, not on every error.

Words used above, in simple terms: metric = a number recorded over time · synthetic check = a robot that regularly calls your site like a user · latency = how long a response takes · p99 = the time within which 99% of requests finish

Avoid / be careful when: measuring only server metrics, or only from inside the system — users experience DNS, network and CDN too.

Type 5 · Error budget

Definition (say it in an interview): An error budget is the amount of unreliability a service may have within an SLO window — 100% minus the SLO — which teams consciously “spend” on releases, experiments and incidents, and which, when exhausted, triggers a shift of effort from features to reliability.

In plain words (no jargon): The amount of failure you are allowed in a month. While some is left, the team can take risks; when it is used up, the team focuses on making things stable.

Analogy: A mobile data pack: 2 GB a day. Use it as you like; when it runs out, you stop streaming until tomorrow.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Compute. Budget = (1 − SLO) × events or minutes in the window.
  2. Track burn. How fast failures consume the budget.
  3. Alert on burn rate. e.g. budget would be gone in 2 days at the current rate.
  4. Policy. Agreed actions at 50%, 75%, 100% spent.
  5. Reset. New window, new budget.

Words used above, in simple terms: burn rate = how quickly the error budget is being used · release freeze = pausing deployments · window = the period the budget covers

Use it when: teams argue about “stability vs speed” — the budget turns it into a number.
Avoid / be careful when: nobody has the authority to freeze releases when the budget is spent — then it is just a chart.

Type 6 · Composite availability (composite SLA)

Definition (say it in an interview): The composite availability of a system is derived from its components: for components that must all work (in series), multiply their availabilities; for redundant components where any one suffices (in parallel), availability = 1 − the product of their unavailabilities, assuming independent failures.

In plain words (no jargon): A system is only as reliable as the combination of its parts: chaining parts together lowers reliability; having spare copies raises it.

Analogy: A train journey with three connections: if each is 99% on time, catching all three is only about 97% likely (series). Having two possible trains for one leg makes that leg far more reliable (parallel).

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Draw the request path. List every component a request must pass.
  2. Series: multiply. A = A1 × A2 × … × An.
  3. Parallel: complement. A = 1 − (1 − A1)(1 − A2).
  4. Combine. Compute parallel groups first, then multiply the series.
  5. Convert to minutes. Downtime = (1 − A) × minutes in month.
  6. Challenge independence. Shared AZs, code or dependencies break the parallel assumption.

Words used above, in simple terms: series = one after another; all needed · parallel = alternatives; any one is enough · correlated failure = several parts failing for the same reason

Products and cloud services

For the experienced — did you know? The parallel formula assumes independence. Two instances in the same AZ, running the same buggy release, are not independent — real availability will be far below 99.9975%.

Use it when: designing or promising availability for a multi-component system.
Avoid / be careful when: failures are correlated (same AZ, same deployment, same dependency) — the parallel formula then overstates availability.

Type 7 · Service credits and exclusions

Definition (say it in an interview): A service credit is the remedy in most cloud SLAs: a percentage of the monthly charge for the affected service, credited against future bills when measured uptime falls below the commitment, subject to eligibility rules, exclusions (events not covered) and a claim process within a deadline.

In plain words (no jargon): If the cloud company fails to meet its promise, it gives you a discount on a future bill — but only if your setup qualified, the cause was theirs, and you ask in time.

Analogy: A late-delivery coupon from a food app: if the order is late you get ₹50 off your next order — not the cost of the dinner party it spoiled, and only if you claim within the app’s rules.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Measure. Uptime per the SLA’s own definition of “unavailable”.
  2. Find the tier. Map uptime to a credit percentage.
  3. Check eligibility. Deployment requirements (e.g. multiple AZs) and exclusions.
  4. Collect evidence. Times, resource IDs, logs.
  5. Claim before the deadline. Support case with the required subject and data.
  6. Receive credit. Applied to future bills of that service.

Words used above, in simple terms: exclusion = a situation the SLA does not cover · force majeure = events beyond anyone’s control, e.g. natural disasters · billing cycle = one monthly bill period

Use it when: an incident clearly breaches an SLA you qualified for — claim it; it also documents the incident.
Avoid / be careful when: treating credits as insurance — they rarely cover more than a fraction of the bill and never lost revenue.

Availability vs durability

Availability = can I use it right now? (S3 Standard: 99.9% SLA). Durability = will my data still exist? (S3 is designed for 99.999999999% — eleven nines — durability). A service can be briefly unavailable without losing any data. Exams mix these on purpose.

RTO and RPO: the disaster-recovery cousins

  • RTO (recovery time objective): how long you may be down after a disaster.
  • RPO (recovery point objective): how much recent data you may lose.
  • Multi-AZ gives small RTO/RPO for AZ failures; multi-Region DR is needed for Region-wide disasters (Lesson 7.2).

Google Cloud: per-product SLAs; credits requested through Cloud Billing support; Google Cloud Service Health; Cloud Monitoring uptime checks and SLO monitoring with burn-rate alerts.

9 Real proof

Proof: the arithmetic, and your own SLI measurement

Steps 1–3 are calculations with tiers from the EC2 SLA page (Nov 2026); step 4 uses the moto AWS emulator.

  1. Step 1 · The nines, calculated

    M = 30 * 24 * 60
for a in [99.0, 99.5, 99.9, 99.95, 99.99, 99.999]:
    print(a, (100 - a) / 100 * M, 'min/month')
    == 1) what each number of nines allows 99.000% → 432.0 min/month 87.60 h/year 99.500% → 216.0 min/month 43.80 h/year 99.900% → 43.2 min/month 8.76 h/year 99.950% → 21.6 min/month 4.38 h/year 99.990% → 4.3 min/month 0.88 h/year 99.999% → 0.4 min/month 0.09 h/year
    Pure arithmetic — but the table every architect should know by heart.

  2. Step 2 · Composite availability

    s = 0.9999 * 0.9999 * 0.9995        # web × app × db (series)
p = 1 - (1 - 0.995) ** 2             # two instances in parallel
    == 2) chain of components (all must work): web 99.99 × app 99.99 × db 99.95 composite = 99.930% → 30.2 min/month (lower than the weakest link) == 3) redundancy (either copy may work): two instances, each 99.5% (EC2 instance-level SLA) composite = 99.9975% → 1.08 min/month (if failures are independent)
    Chains lose availability; redundancy gains it — if failures are independent.

  3. Step 3 · From uptime to service credit (EC2 tiers)

    credit = 0 if up >= 99.99 else (10 if up >= 99.0 else (30 if up >= 95.0 else 100))
    == 4) EC2 service credit for a bad month (Region-level SLA, 99.99% commitment) uptime 99.995% → credit 0% of the affected EC2 bill = $ 0.00 of $1000 uptime 99.950% → credit 10% of the affected EC2 bill = $ 100.00 of $1000 uptime 98.500% → credit 30% of the affected EC2 bill = $ 300.00 of $1000 uptime 94.000% → credit 100% of the affected EC2 bill = $1000.00 of $1000
    Tiers from the EC2 Region-level SLA (Nov 2026). The credit applies to the affected EC2 charges only.

  4. Step 4 · Measure your own SLI: health check + alarm (emulator)

    aws route53 create-health-check --caller-reference tiffingo-$(date +%s) \
  --health-check-config Type=HTTPS,FullyQualifiedDomainName=tiffingo.in,ResourcePath=/health,RequestInterval=30,FailureThreshold=3
aws cloudwatch put-metric-alarm --alarm-name tiffingo-down --namespace AWS/Route53 \
  --metric-name HealthCheckStatus --dimensions Name=HealthCheckId,Value=$HC \
  --statistic Minimum --period 60 --evaluation-periods 1 --threshold 1 --comparison-operator LessThanThreshold
    == 1) health check: HTTPS GET /health every 30 s from AWS checkers around the world HealthCheckId: aadcc349-0179-4a34-8b58-26fd765bd5c0 HTTPS tiffingo.in /health 30 3 == 2) alarm when the site is unhealthy for 1 minute tiffingo-down HealthCheckStatus 1.0 LessThanThreshold
    An outside-in check every 30 s and an alarm after one unhealthy minute. Output from the moto emulator.
10 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

Every step has a UI way and a CLI way. Steps 1–2 need only a browser or spreadsheet; steps 3–4 use AWS (CLI outputs from the moto emulator). Route 53 health checks have a small monthly charge on a real account — delete them after practice.

  1. 1 · Build the “nines” table yourself

    UI way — spreadsheet

    1. Open Excel/Google Sheets: column A = 99, 99.5, 99.9, 99.95, 99.99, 99.999.
    2. Column B formula: =(100-A2)/100*30*24*60 → minutes per month.
    3. Add series: =0.9999*0.9999*0.9995; parallel: =1-(1-0.995)^2.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    python3 l54_sla.py        # script in lab/jobs/m5
    == 1) what each number of nines allows 99.000% → 432.0 min/month 87.60 h/year 99.500% → 216.0 min/month 43.80 h/year 99.900% → 43.2 min/month 8.76 h/year 99.950% → 21.6 min/month 4.38 h/year 99.990% → 4.3 min/month 0.88 h/year 99.999% → 0.4 min/month 0.09 h/year == 2) chain of components (all must work): web 99.99 × app 99.99 × db 99.95 composite = 99.930% → 30.2 min/month (lower than the weakest link)
    Keep this sheet; you will reuse it in every architecture review.

  2. 2 · Read a real SLA

    UI way — browser

    1. Search “Amazon Compute Service Level Agreement” and open the AWS page.
    2. Find: the commitment, the definition of “Region-Level Unavailable”, the credit table, the claim deadline, the exclusions.
    3. Do the same for “Azure SLA Virtual Machines” and “Compute Engine SLA”.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    curl -s https://aws.amazon.com/ec2-sla/ | grep -o -i -E "99\.99%|99\.5%" | sort | uniq -c
    Write a 5-line summary per SLA: commitment, condition, credits, deadline, top exclusion.

  3. 3 · Create an outside-in health check (your SLI)

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Route 53 → Health checks → Create health check.
    2. Name tiffingo-health, endpoint by Domain name tiffingo.in, protocol HTTPS, path /health.
    3. Advanced configuration: Request interval Standard (30 seconds), Failure threshold 3 → Next → Create alarm: Yes → SNS topic with your e-mail → Create health check.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws route53 create-health-check --caller-reference tiffingo-$(date +%s) \
  --health-check-config Type=HTTPS,FullyQualifiedDomainName=tiffingo.in,ResourcePath=/health,RequestInterval=30,FailureThreshold=3
    == 1) health check: HTTPS GET /health every 30 s from AWS checkers around the world HealthCheckId: aadcc349-0179-4a34-8b58-26fd765bd5c0 HTTPS tiffingo.in /health 30 3
    Health checkers in several AWS locations probe your site — the user’s view, not the server’s.

  4. 4 · Alarm when it fails

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. CloudWatch (Region N. Virginia — Route 53 metrics live there) → Alarms → Create alarm → Select metric → Route 53 → Health Check Metrics → HealthCheckStatus.
    2. Statistic Minimum, period 1 minute, condition Lower than 1 → notification to an SNS topic → Create alarm.
    3. AWS Health Dashboard → Your account health: provider-side events affecting you.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws cloudwatch put-metric-alarm --region us-east-1 --alarm-name tiffingo-down \
  --namespace AWS/Route53 --metric-name HealthCheckStatus --dimensions Name=HealthCheckId,Value=$HC \
  --statistic Minimum --period 60 --evaluation-periods 1 --threshold 1 --comparison-operator LessThanThreshold
    == 2) alarm when the site is unhealthy for 1 minute tiffingo-down HealthCheckStatus 1.0 LessThanThreshold
    Now you have evidence (times, durations) if you ever need to claim a credit.

Clean up and cost check

Console: Route 53 → Health checks → select → Delete health check; CloudWatch → Alarms → select → Delete. CLI: aws route53 delete-health-check --health-check-id $HC; aws cloudwatch delete-alarms --alarm-names tiffingo-down --region us-east-1.

11 Mistakes to avoid

Common mistakes, why they happen, how to fix them

Mistake or symptomWhy it happensHow to fix or avoid
Promising customers the provider’s SLA number.Copying 99.99% from the EC2 page.Compute your composite availability; promise below your SLO.
Single-AZ design, expecting the 99.99% SLA.Not reading the definition.Run in ≥2 AZs; know which SLA (region vs instance) applies.
Treating credits as insurance.Misreading “compensation”.Credits are capped bill discounts; plan resilience and business insurance separately.
Not claiming within the deadline.Credits are not automatic.Record incidents with evidence; claim by the end of the second billing cycle.
Confusing availability with durability.Both are percentages.Availability = reachable now; durability = data not lost (S3: 11 nines designed).
Measuring SLIs only from inside.Server metrics are easy.Add outside-in checks (health checks, synthetics) that see DNS/TLS/CDN problems.
Setting a 100% SLO.It sounds ambitious.Impossible and blocks all change; choose a realistic SLO with an error budget.

15 Quiz and revision

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 5.4 in one screen

  • SLA = promise (contract); SLO = internal target (tighter); SLI = measurement.
  • Nines: 99.9% ≈ 43 min, 99.95% ≈ 22 min, 99.99% ≈ 4.3 min a month.
  • Error budget = 100% − SLO; spend it on releases, stop when it is gone.
  • Series multiplies availability down; parallel redundancy pushes it up.
  • Credits are a % of the service bill, not compensation; you must claim them.
  • EC2: 99.99% multi-AZ, 99.5% single instance; S3 Standard 99.9%; RDS Multi-AZ 99.95%.

Interview and viva questions

Does a higher SLA mean the service never fails?

No — it means the provider pays a credit when it fails more than allowed. Design for failure anyway.

Do I get credits automatically?

Usually not — you must open a support case with evidence within the stated window (for EC2, by the end of the second billing cycle).

Should my SLA equal my provider’s?

No — yours must be at or below the composite of everything beneath it, and below your own SLO.
