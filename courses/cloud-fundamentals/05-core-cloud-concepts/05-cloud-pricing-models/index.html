Cloud pricing models

The same server can cost half as much under another pricing model. Learn On-Demand, Savings Plans / Reserved Instances, Spot, free tier, tiered pricing, licensing and data transfer with real Mumbai prices — then set a budget alert yourself.

1 The story

The bill that doubled after Diwali

Before you start: 5.3 Elasticity and 5.4 SLAs.

🤔 The same m7i.large server in Mumbai can cost ₹6,813 a month — or ₹2,839. Same hardware, same Region. What changes?

Only the pricing model. On-Demand is the full price; a 3-year Savings Plan cuts it by about half; Spot uses spare capacity at a deep discount but can be taken back with a two-minute warning. On top of that, data leaving the cloud is billed per GB, storage gets cheaper in tiers, and licences can double a Windows bill. This lesson uses real Mumbai prices to show every model — and when each one is the right choice.

  • Why the cheapest option is often the wrong one.

After the Diwali rush, TiffinGo’s AWS bill doubled. Everything ran On-Demand, and a mysterious line called “data transfer” had appeared. Time to learn how the cloud actually charges.

Riya

Sir, our AWS bill doubled after Diwali. Everything is On-Demand. Can we just switch to Spot? It is 58% cheaper!

For the nightly report job, yes. For the order API, no — Spot capacity can be taken back with two minutes’ notice.

Kabir

Then how do we save on servers that run all the time?

Arjun Sir

Commit. A Savings Plan says “we will spend $X per hour for 1 or 3 years” and AWS gives a lower rate. Our two base servers are on 24×7 — perfect fit.

And the ₹18,000 line called “data transfer”?

That is 2 TB of tiffin photos leaving AWS to customers’ phones. Data in is free; data out is charged per GB. A CDN and smaller images fix that. Let’s go model by model.

2 Definition

Cloud pricing models

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A cloud pricing model is the set of rules by which a provider charges for resources: the unit (per second, GB-month, GB transferred, request), the rate per Region, and the terms that change that rate — no commitment (On-Demand), a 1- or 3-year commitment (Savings Plans, Reserved Instances, committed use discounts), interruptible spare capacity (Spot), free allowances, volume tiers, licensing options and data transfer charges.

In simple words

How you pay for the cloud: per use, cheaper if you promise long-term use, cheapest if you accept interruptions — plus charges for storage and for data going out.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “Cloud pricing is consumption-based: compute per second or hour, storage per GB-month, data transfer out per GB — ingress is free. On top, there are levers: On-Demand for flexibility, Savings Plans or Reserved Instances for steady 1–3 year usage, and Spot for interruptible work at up to 90% off.”
  • “I cover the baseline with commitments, peaks with On-Demand, batch with Spot, and watch the bill with tags, Cost Explorer and Budgets.”

Type 1 · On-Demand (pay-as-you-go)

Definition (say it in an interview): On-Demand (pay-as-you-go) pricing charges a fixed published rate for each unit of a resource — per second, hour, GB or request — only while you use it, with no upfront payment and no long-term commitment. It is the default model for every cloud service.

In plain words (no jargon): You switch it on, you pay for the time it is on, you switch it off. No contract. It is the most flexible and the most expensive per hour.

Analogy: An auto-rickshaw meter: you pay for exactly the distance travelled, at the posted rate, and can get out any time. Convenient — but daily commuting by auto costs more than a monthly bus pass.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Publish. The provider publishes a rate per usage type per Region (e.g. Mumbai m7i.large Linux = $0.10605/h).
  2. Meter. The service records usage — seconds running, GB stored, requests.
  3. Multiply. Usage × rate for the billing period.
  4. Stop. When you stop or delete the resource, that meter stops; related meters (disks, IPs) may continue.
  5. Invoice. Charged monthly in arrears to your card or invoice.

Words used above, in simple terms: pay-as-you-go = pay only for what you use, after you use it · per second billing = charged by the second instead of rounding up to the hour · in arrears = billed after the month ends

Real scenario (TiffinGo)

TiffinGo’s Diwali surge: 10 extra m7i.large servers for 6 evening hours × 7 days = 420 instance-hours = $44.54 (≈ ₹3,920) On-Demand. Buying those 10 servers for the year would mean paying for 87,600 hours of capacity.

Products and cloud services

EC2 On-Demand, Azure pay-as-you-go VMs, Google Compute Engine on-demand; also S3, Lambda and most managed services by default.

For the experienced — did you know? On-Demand is not just “the expensive option” — it is the only model with no capacity risk and no commitment risk. Mature teams deliberately keep 20–30% of compute On-Demand so they can change instance families without stranding commitments.

Use it when: workloads are new, spiky, short-lived or unpredictable — experiments, peaks, anything you may turn off soon.
Avoid / be careful when: servers run 24×7 for months — you are paying the highest rate for a steady load (use type 2).

Type 2 · Commitment discounts: Savings Plans and Reserved Instances

Definition (say it in an interview): Commitment-based pricing gives a lower rate in exchange for committing to a consistent amount of usage for a 1- or 3-year term. On AWS: Savings Plans (commit to $/hour of compute) and Reserved Instances (commit to an instance configuration); on Azure: Reservations and savings plans for compute; on Google Cloud: committed use discounts (CUDs).

In plain words (no jargon): You promise to keep using (and paying for) a certain amount for 1 or 3 years. In return you get a big discount. Longer promise and fewer choices = bigger discount.

Analogy: A monthly metro pass: you pay less per trip because you promise to travel every day. If you fall sick for a week, the pass is still paid.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Choose the type. Compute Savings Plan (any family, size, Region, also Fargate/Lambda) or EC2 Instance Savings Plan (one family in one Region) or a Reserved Instance.
  2. Choose term and payment. 1 or 3 years; All Upfront, Partial Upfront or No Upfront — more upfront = slightly lower rate.
  3. Commit. E.g. $0.10/hour of compute. You are billed this every hour of the term.
  4. Automatic matching. Each hour billing applies the discount to eligible usage, highest savings first.
  5. Overflow and waste. Usage above the commitment is On-Demand; commitment not used that hour is wasted.

Words used above, in simple terms: term = the length of the promise: 1 or 3 years · upfront = paying part or all of it in advance · instance family = a group of similar servers, e.g. m7i · utilisation = how much of your commitment you actually used

Real scenario (TiffinGo)

Products and cloud services

AWS Savings Plans (Compute, EC2 Instance, SageMaker), EC2/RDS Reserved Instances; Azure Reservations and Azure savings plan for compute; Google Cloud resource-based and spend-based CUDs.

For the experienced — did you know? Savings Plans are applied to the usage with the highest discount percentage first, not to a server you choose — so a Compute SP bought for EC2 may end up covering Fargate. And RIs/Savings Plans do not reserve capacity; for guaranteed capacity you need On-Demand Capacity Reservations (paid even when idle).

Use it when: part of your usage is steady and predictable for at least a year — size the commitment to the minimum you always run.
Avoid / be careful when: usage is uncertain or shrinking, or you might move to another architecture (e.g. serverless) soon — an unused commitment is pure loss.

Type 3 · Spot (preemptible) pricing

Definition (say it in an interview): Spot pricing sells a provider’s spare capacity at a steep discount — on AWS up to about 90% off On-Demand — on the condition that the provider can reclaim it when it needs the capacity back, with a short warning (two minutes on AWS, about 30 seconds on Azure and Google Cloud).

In plain words (no jargon): The cloud company sells its empty servers cheaply. The catch: when it needs them back, it gives you a short warning and takes them. Great for work that can pause and continue.

Analogy: Standby airline tickets: the empty seats are sold cheaply at the gate, but if a full-fare passenger turns up, you wait for the next flight.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Spare pools. Each instance type in each AZ is a separate capacity pool with its own Spot price.
  2. Request. You launch Spot (directly, via Auto Scaling or a fleet), optionally setting a maximum price.
  3. Price moves slowly. The Spot price changes gradually with long-term supply and demand — you pay the current Spot price, not a bid.
  4. Interruption notice. When capacity is needed, AWS sends a 2-minute notice (instance metadata + EventBridge) and often an earlier rebalance recommendation.
  5. Reclaim. The instance is terminated, stopped or hibernated; diversified fleets replace it from other pools.

Words used above, in simple terms: capacity pool = one instance type in one AZ · interruption = the provider taking the server back · checkpoint = saving progress so work can resume · fleet = a group of instances requested together, across types

Products and cloud services

EC2 Spot Instances (and Spot in EC2 Auto Scaling / EKS / EMR / Batch), Azure Spot Virtual Machines, Google Cloud Spot VMs (successor to preemptible VMs).

For the experienced — did you know? Since 2017 AWS Spot is not an auction any more: you pay the current Spot price, which changes gradually. Setting a high max price does not protect you from interruption — capacity, not price, triggers most interruptions. Diversify pools and use the price-capacity-optimized allocation strategy instead.

Use it when: work is fault-tolerant, stateless or checkpointed, and flexible in time and instance type — CI builds, batch, big data, rendering, ML training with checkpoints.
Avoid / be careful when: the workload must not stop — databases, payment APIs, a single critical server — or when the job cannot restart from a checkpoint.

Type 4 · Free tier and credits

Definition (say it in an interview): A free tier lets customers use limited amounts of services at no charge — as credits for new accounts, trial periods, or always-free monthly allowances. Since July 2025, new AWS customers get up to $200 in credits and can choose a free plan for up to 6 months; Azure offers $200 credit for 30 days; Google Cloud offers $300 credit for 90 days, each with always-free products.

In plain words (no jargon): New customers get some free money (credits) and some services are free up to a small limit every month. Good for learning. Not for running a business.

Analogy: Free samples at a sweet shop: a fixed amount to taste, a few items always free with every visit, and beyond that you pay the normal price.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Eligibility. New accounts (or always-free offers for everyone).
  2. Credits applied first. Eligible usage on the bill is paid from credits.
  3. Always-free allowances. Some services have a free monthly quota (e.g. Lambda requests) that resets each month.
  4. Guardrails. AWS’s free plan blocks some expensive services and closes the account when credits or 6 months end, unless you upgrade.
  5. Alerts. Free-tier usage alerts and Budgets warn you near the limits.

Words used above, in simple terms: credits = money added to your account that pays the bill first · always-free = a small allowance that never expires · trial = a time-limited free period

Use it when: learning, prototypes and proofs of concept — and to check the real cost of a design before committing.
Avoid / be careful when: you assume “free tier” means a production system is free, or forget resources running after credits or the trial end.

Type 5 · Tiered, volume and sustained-use pricing

Definition (say it in an interview): Tiered (volume) pricing lowers the unit price as monthly usage passes thresholds — each tier’s rate applies only to the usage within that tier. Sustained-use discounts (Google Cloud) automatically reduce the price of eligible VMs that run for a large part of the month. Neither needs a commitment.

In plain words (no jargon): The more you use, the cheaper each extra unit becomes — automatically. Big users pay a little less per GB than small users.

Analogy: Income-tax slabs, in reverse: the first slab is charged at one rate, only the amount above it at the next — and here each slab gets cheaper.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Thresholds. The price list defines ranges per month (0–50 TB, 50–500 TB, 500 TB+).
  2. Fill in order. Usage fills the first tier, then the next.
  3. Rate per tier. Each tier’s portion is multiplied by that tier’s rate.
  4. Sum. The tier amounts add up to the line item.
  5. Sustained use (GCP). For eligible VMs, the share of the month a VM runs sets an automatic discount, up to 30%.

Words used above, in simple terms: tier = a usage range with its own price · GB-month = one GB stored for one month · storage class = a type of storage with a different price and access speed

Products and cloud services

S3 and Azure Blob storage tiers by volume, data-transfer-out tiers, CloudFront price classes; Google Cloud sustained use discounts (N1, N2, N2D, C2 and others).

That is one reason companies use AWS Organizations with consolidated billing: usage of all accounts is added together to reach cheaper tiers and share Savings Plans.

Use it when: usage is large and growing — the discount arrives automatically; still compare storage classes and commitments.
Avoid / be careful when: you expect tiers to make a small workload cheap — at small scale, choosing the right class or model matters far more.

Type 6 · Licensing models: licence-included, BYOL and subscription

Definition (say it in an interview): Software licensing models decide how commercial software is paid for in the cloud: licence-included (the licence cost is built into the hourly price), bring your own licence (BYOL) (reuse licences you already own, where the vendor’s terms allow), and subscription (per user or per tier per month, typical of SaaS and Marketplace products).

In plain words (no jargon): Some software (like Windows or SQL Server) needs a paid licence. You can pay for it inside the cloud price, bring a licence you already bought, or rent finished software per user per month.

Analogy: Renting a car with or without a driver: “driver included” costs more per day; if you already have a driver on your payroll, you rent only the car.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Licence-included. The usage type includes the licence; billing shows a higher rate (e.g. “Windows” instead of “Linux/UNIX”).
  2. BYOL. You prove eligibility (e.g. Software Assurance), launch with the base rate, and track licences (AWS License Manager).
  3. Dedicated hardware if required. Some licences are per physical core/socket — they need Dedicated Hosts or sole-tenant nodes.
  4. Subscription. SaaS or Marketplace products bill per user, per hour or per tier, often through the cloud bill.
  5. Audit. Vendors can audit licence use — keep records.

Words used above, in simple terms: licence = legal permission to use paid software · BYOL = bring your own licence · Software Assurance = a Microsoft add-on that gives licence-mobility rights · Marketplace = an online store of third-party software inside the cloud console

Use it when: commercial software is part of the stack — compare licence-included vs BYOL vs a managed/SaaS alternative before migrating.
Avoid / be careful when: you assume on-prem licences can be moved freely — many vendors restrict cloud use or require dedicated hardware.

Type 7 · Data transfer (egress) pricing

Definition (say it in an interview): Data transfer pricing charges per GB for data moving out of a cloud Region — to the internet (egress), to another Region, and in many cases between Availability Zones — while data transfer in (ingress) is generally free. Rates fall in volume tiers and vary by Region.

In plain words (no jargon): Putting data into the cloud is free. Taking data out — to users, to another region, sometimes even to another data centre in the same region — is charged by the GB.

Analogy: A hotel where checking in is free but every bag you take out of the building costs a fee — and taking bags between the hotel’s own wings costs a little too.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Metered at the edge of each boundary. Bytes leaving a Region (or crossing AZs) are counted per account.
  2. Direction matters. In is free; out to the internet, to another Region, or across AZs is charged.
  3. Free allowance. First 100 GB/month out to the internet is free, aggregated across Regions (AWS, since Dec 2021).
  4. Tiers. Mumbai: $0.1093/GB for the next 10 TB, then $0.085, $0.082, $0.080.
  5. Cheaper paths. S3/EC2 → CloudFront is free; CloudFront then bills its own (usually lower) per-GB rate to viewers.

Words used above, in simple terms: egress = data leaving the cloud · ingress = data coming into the cloud · CDN = content delivery network: caches content near users · inter-AZ = between two data-centre groups in one Region · NAT gateway = a managed gateway that lets private servers reach the internet — charged per hour and per GB

Real scenario (TiffinGo)

TiffinGo serves 2 TB of tiffin photos a month straight from EC2 in Mumbai: (2,048 − 100) GB × $0.1093 = $212.92 (≈ ₹18,737) a month — more than three always-on m7i.large servers. Fix: compress images and serve them through CloudFront (origin-to-CloudFront transfer is free).

AWS Data Transfer (internet, inter-Region, inter-AZ), CloudFront; Azure bandwidth pricing and Azure Front Door; Google Cloud network egress (Premium vs Standard tier) and Cloud CDN.

For the experienced — did you know? Egress is why “multi-cloud” is expensive in practice: an app in one cloud reading a database in another pays internet egress on every query. And NAT gateways add a per-GB processing charge on top — routing S3 traffic through a free S3 gateway endpoint instead of NAT is a classic quick win.

Use it when: designing any architecture — estimate GB out per month, keep chatty services in the same AZ where safe, and put a CDN in front of public content.
Avoid / be careful when: you assume “storage is cheap so the app is cheap” — serving that data out can cost far more than storing it.

Why: Fault-tolerant, flexible batch → Spot.

B. A production database running 24×7 for the next 3 years.
  1. Spot
  2. On-Demand
  3. ✔ Reserved Instance / Savings Plan
  4. Free tier

Why: Steady, long-lived → commitment discount.

C. A one-day hackathon prototype.
  1. 3-year RI
  2. ✔ On-Demand (or free-tier credits)
  3. Dedicated Host
  4. BYOL

Why: Short and uncertain → no commitment.

D. The bill’s fastest-growing line is “DataTransfer-Out-Bytes”.
  1. Buy RIs
  2. ✔ Serve content via a CDN and compress it
  3. Switch to Spot
  4. Use bigger instances

Why: Egress problem → CDN, compression, caching.

6 Explained part by part

Six things in every cloud cost decision

Unit price

What it is: The rate per second, GB or request in one Region.

Why it exists: Every estimate starts here.

Where it is used: AWS Pricing Calculator, Azure Pricing Calculator, Google Cloud Pricing Calculator.

Region

What it is: The same service costs differently per Region.

Why it exists: Mumbai vs Hyderabad vs Virginia — compare before you choose (Lesson 5.1).

Where it is used: Price pages and the Price List API by Region.

Commitment

What it is: Term (1/3 yr) and payment option (all/partial/no upfront).

Why it exists: The biggest lever for steady workloads.

Where it is used: Savings Plans, Reservations, CUDs.

Tags

What it is: Labels like project=tiffingo, env=prod.

Why it exists: Without tags nobody knows which team spent what.

Where it is used: Cost allocation tags, Azure tags, GCP labels.

Budgets & alerts

What it is: Thresholds that notify or act.

Why it exists: Catch mistakes in days, not at month end.

Where it is used: AWS Budgets, Azure Cost Management budgets, GCP budget alerts.

TCO

What it is: Total cost of ownership, including people and facilities.

Why it exists: Compare cloud with on-premises fairly.

Where it is used: Migration Evaluator, Azure TCO calculator.

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: The cloud is always cheaper.
    Reality: Only if you use elasticity, commitments and the right models — lift-and-shift On-Demand can cost more.
    Proof: TCO deep dive.
  • Myth: Spot is just cheaper On-Demand.
    Reality: It can be taken back with two minutes’ warning.
    Proof: Type 3.
  • Myth: Storage is the big cost.
    Reality: Serving data out often costs more than storing it.
    Proof: Type 7.
7 Deep dive

TCO, CapEx vs OpEx, dedicated options and FinOps

TCO: comparing cloud with your own data centre honestly

Total cost of ownership (TCO) adds every cost of running a workload for its life — not only the server price. On-premises: hardware, power and cooling, space, network, licences, staff, maintenance and refresh every 4–5 years, plus the capacity you buy for the peak and leave idle. Cloud: usage, data transfer, support plan, tools and the (smaller) operations team.

Cost itemOn-premisesCloud
HardwareCapEx upfront, refreshed every 4–5 yrsnone — inside the hourly price
Capacity for peaksbought and idle most of the yearpay for peak hours only (5.3)
Power, cooling, spaceyou payincluded
Staffhardware + facilities + opsmostly ops/FinOps
Data transferISP bandwidthper GB out (type 7)
Exit costhardware resaleegress + re-platforming

Our Diwali example in numbers: 10 extra servers for 420 hours cost $44.54 On-Demand; owning them means paying for 87,600 server-hours of capacity a year. That idle capacity is where most cloud savings come from.

CapEx vs OpEx

  • CapEx (capital expenditure): buy assets upfront and depreciate them — classic data centres.
  • OpEx (operational expenditure): pay as you consume — cloud. A 3-year all-upfront Savings Plan moves cloud spending back towards CapEx-like behaviour.

Dedicated options and their price

Dedicated Instances and Dedicated Hosts (Lesson 5.2) cost more because the hardware is not shared; they are bought for compliance or per-core licences (type 6), not to save money. Capacity Reservations guarantee capacity at the On-Demand rate whether you use it or not.

FinOps in one paragraph

FinOps is the practice of making engineering, finance and business teams jointly accountable for cloud spend: inform (tags, dashboards), optimise (right-size, commitments, Spot, storage classes) and operate (budgets, reviews, unit cost such as “₹ per 100 orders”).

10 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

Every step has a UI way and a CLI way. Steps 1–2 cost nothing; step 3’s first two budgets are free on AWS; Cost Explorer API calls cost $0.01 each. Budget CLI output is from the moto emulator.

  1. 1 · Estimate a monthly bill

    UI way — AWS Pricing Calculator

    1. Open calculator.aws → Create estimate → search Amazon EC2 → Configure.
    2. Region Asia Pacific (Mumbai), OS Linux, instance m7i.large, quantity 2, usage 100% (24×7).
    3. Under pricing strategy compare On-Demand vs Compute Savings Plans 3 yr → Save and add service.
    4. Add Data Transfer: 2 TB/month out to the internet → Save and view summary → Share to get a link.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    # real account: the Price List API lives in us-east-1 (and a few other Regions)
aws pricing get-products --region us-east-1 --service-code AmazonEC2 \
  --filters Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=instanceType,Value=m7i.large \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=regionCode,Value=ap-south-1 \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=operatingSystem,Value=Linux \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=tenancy,Value=Shared \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=preInstalledSw,Value=NA \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=capacitystatus,Value=Used \
  --max-results 1 | head -c 600
    Expected: about $155/month On-Demand for the two servers (2 × $0.10605 × 730) plus about $213 for 2 TB out.

  2. 2 · Check the Spot price and interruption risk

    UI way — EC2 console

    1. EC2 console → Spot Requests → Pricing history → instance type m7i.large, platform Linux → see the price per AZ.
    2. Open the Spot Instance advisor page → Region Mumbai → note the “frequency of interruption” band for m7i.large.
    3. Compare with the On-Demand rate from step 1.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws ec2 describe-spot-price-history --region ap-south-1 --instance-types m7i.large \
  --product-descriptions "Linux/UNIX" --start-time $(date -u +%Y-%m-%dT%H:%M:%S) \
  --query "SpotPriceHistory[].[AvailabilityZone,SpotPrice]" --output table
    Our snapshot on 10 Nov 2026: $0.0442/h vs $0.10605 On-Demand (−58%). The emulator returns placeholder Spot prices, so this step is only meaningful on a real account.

  3. 3 · Create a budget alert — do this first on any new account

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Billing and Cost Management → Budgets → Create budget → Use a template → Monthly cost budget.
    2. Name tiffingo-monthly, amount 50 USD, e-mail owner@tiffingo.in → Create budget.
    3. Edit → Add an alert threshold: 80% of Actual and 100% of Forecasted.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    ACCT=$(aws sts get-caller-identity --query Account --output text)
aws budgets create-budget --account-id $ACCT \
  --budget '{"BudgetName":"tiffingo-monthly","BudgetLimit":{"Amount":"50","Unit":"USD"},"TimeUnit":"MONTHLY","BudgetType":"COST"}' \
  --notifications-with-subscribers '[{"Notification":{"NotificationType":"ACTUAL","ComparisonOperator":"GREATER_THAN","Threshold":80,"ThresholdType":"PERCENTAGE"},"Subscribers":[{"SubscriptionType":"EMAIL","Address":"owner@tiffingo.in"}]}]'
aws budgets describe-budgets --account-id $ACCT --output text
    == 1) monthly cost budget US$50 (≈ ₹4,400), e-mail at 80% of actual spend tiffingo-monthly 50 USD MONTHLY COST ACTUAL GREATER_THAN 80 PERCENTAGE
    You now get an e-mail before a forgotten server becomes a big bill.

  4. 4 · Read your costs and get a Savings Plan recommendation

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Billing and Cost Management → Cost Explorer → last 3 months, Group by Service, then Usage type — find the DataTransfer-Out line.
    2. Savings Plans → Recommendations → Compute, 1 year, No upfront, based on the last 30 days → read the estimated savings.
    3. Cost allocation tags → activate project so next month you can group by it.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=2026-10-01,End=2026-11-01 \
  --granularity MONTHLY --metrics UnblendedCost --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=SERVICE
aws ce get-savings-plans-purchase-recommendation --savings-plans-type COMPUTE_SP \
  --term-in-years ONE_YEAR --payment-option NO_UPFRONT --lookback-period-in-days THIRTY_DAYS
    Real account only (Cost Explorer data is not emulated). A brand-new account has little data — revisit after a month.

Clean up and cost check

Console: Budgets → select tiffingo-monthly → Actions → Delete (or keep it — the first two budgets are free). CLI: aws budgets delete-budget --account-id $ACCT --budget-name tiffingo-monthly. No other resources were created.

11 Mistakes to avoid

Common mistakes, why they happen, how to fix them

Mistake or symptomWhy it happensHow to fix or avoid
Running steady 24×7 servers On-Demand for years.Nobody owns the bill.Cover the always-on minimum with a Savings Plan; review monthly.
Buying a commitment for the peak.Sizing to the busiest hour.Commit only to the baseline; peaks stay On-Demand or Spot.
Putting a database or payment API on Spot.Seeing only the price.Spot only for interruptible, checkpointed work.
Ignoring data transfer in estimates.Storage looks cheap.Estimate GB out; use a CDN, compression and same-AZ design.
No budget on a new account.“It’s only practice.”Create a budget with alerts on day one.

15 Quiz and revision

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 5.5 in one screen

  • On-Demand: full rate, no commitment — peaks and experiments.
  • Savings Plans / RIs: 1–3 yr commitment — ~26–55% off in our Mumbai data (AWS: up to 72%).
  • Spot: spare capacity, up to ~90% off, 2-minute interruption notice — batch only.
  • Free tier: AWS up to $200 credits + 6-month free plan (since July 2025); Azure $200/30 days; GCP $300/90 days.
  • Tiers and GCP sustained use: automatic, small at small scale.
  • Licensing: included vs BYOL vs SaaS subscription.
  • Data in free; out $0.1093/GB from Mumbai after 100 GB free; CDN helps.
  • Budgets on day one; tags; Cost Explorer; TCO for cloud vs on-prem.

Interview and viva questions

Is Spot safe for production?

For stateless, fault-tolerant tiers mixed with On-Demand in an Auto Scaling group, yes; never for a single critical server or database.

Savings Plan or Reserved Instance?

Savings Plans for most compute (more flexible); RIs still matter for RDS, ElastiCache, OpenSearch and when you need zonal capacity reservation.

Why are prices in US$?

AWS prices most services in US$; Indian accounts are billed by AWS India in ₹ with GST, at the exchange rate on the invoice.
