History and evolution of cloud computing

The cloud’s ideas are sixty years old, but the business began in 2006. We walk through six eras as pictures, watch one bank’s IT change across fifty years, and see exactly how virtualization turned into the cloud.

1 The story

Is the cloud just mainframes again?

Before you start: 1.1 What is cloud computing?

🤔 Who invented the cloud? Was it Amazon in 2006, or is it much older?

The cloud is the answer to a 60-year-old problem: computers are expensive, and most of the time they sit idle. Every era — mainframes, PCs, server rooms, grids, virtualization — fixed one part of that problem and created a new one. In 2006 AWS put the last pieces together: rent a slice of a server, start it yourself, pay by the hour.

  • Six eras, each as a picture: what it solved and what it broke.
  • Virtualization: the one technology that made the cloud possible.
  • When AWS, Azure and Google Cloud started, and what came next.

Riya’s uncle ran a bank mainframe in the 1980s. He says the cloud is nothing new. Riya wants to know what really changed between his mainframe and today’s cloud.

Riya

Kabir

Arjun Sir

Yes. Let’s walk through the eras like a story, one picture at a time, and see what problem each one solved.

2 Definition

History and evolution of cloud computing

Formal definition (write this in exams)

The evolution of cloud computing runs from 1960s mainframe time-sharing and the idea of utility computing, through personal computers, client-server computing and the internet, grid computing and hardware virtualization, to on-demand public clouds (AWS 2006, Google App Engine 2008, Microsoft Azure 2010) and today’s serverless, container and AI services.

In simple words

First people shared one big computer, then everyone owned one, then companies filled rooms with half-idle servers, then split servers into VMs — and finally providers started renting those VMs to anyone, by the hour.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “Cloud computing evolved from 1960s mainframe time-sharing and the idea of utility computing, through client-server computing, the internet, grid computing and virtualization.”
  • “Virtualization was the key enabler: one physical server can run many isolated VMs. Providers added self-service, metering and internet access, and the public cloud began with AWS S3 and EC2 in 2006, followed by Google App Engine in 2008 and Azure in 2010.”
  • “Since then it has moved to containers, serverless with AWS Lambda in 2014, and today AI services.”

5 Explained part by part

The six eras, one by one

The picture above showed the eras; here is the detail of each, in one line of story and one idea the cloud kept.

1. Mainframe and time-sharing (1960s–70s)

Huge, costly computers shared by many users through “dumb” terminals; companies paid for computer time. In 1961 John McCarthy said computing could one day be sold like a public utility.

Idea kept: sharing + metering

2. Personal computers (1980s–90s)

Cheap PCs put computing on every desk, but data and software were scattered and hard to manage.

Idea kept: cheap standard hardware

3. Client-server and the web (1990s)

Shared applications moved to servers reached over networks and the internet. Usually one application per server, so most servers sat idle.

Idea kept: access over the network

4. Grid and utility computing (late 1990s–2000s)

Many computers pooled to solve big scientific jobs; companies tried “utility” pricing. Powerful but hard to use.

Idea kept: resource pooling

5. Virtualization (1999 →)

VMware (1999), Xen (2003) and KVM (2007) let one server run many isolated virtual machines, raising utilisation.

Idea kept: VMs, multitenancy

6. Cloud computing (2006 →)

AWS S3 (March 2006) and EC2 (August 2006) let anyone rent storage and VMs over the internet, self-service, by the hour. Salesforce (1999) had already sold software as a service.

Today: serverless, containers, AI

    Reality: The idea is from 1961; 2006 is when it became a business.
    Proof: Era cards 1 and 6.
  • Myth: Virtualization and cloud are the same.
    Reality: Cloud = virtualization + self-service + metering + internet.
    Proof: Animation 2.
  • Myth: The cloud stopped growing.

8 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

Prove virtualization with your own eyes: check whether the machine you are using is a virtual machine. Works on Linux, WSL on Windows, or any cloud VM.

  1. 1 · Ask the CPU

    lscpu | grep -E 'CPU\(s\)|Model name|Hypervisor vendor|Virtualization type'
    == 1) lscpu on our cloud lab machine CPU(s): 2 Model name: Intel(R) Xeon(R) Processor @ 2.10GHz Hypervisor vendor: KVM Virtualization type: full
    Our cloud lab machine shows a Hypervisor vendor (KVM): it is a VM, a 2-CPU slice of a big Xeon server.

  2. 2 · Look for the hypervisor flag

    grep -o -w hypervisor /proc/cpuinfo | head -1
    == 2) is the word 'hypervisor' in the CPU flags? hypervisor
    If this word appears, a hypervisor is under you. On a physical laptop it is usually absent.

  3. 3 · Ask systemd

    systemd-detect-virt
    == 3) systemd-detect-virt docker
    Our lab is a container inside a VM inside a physical server: three layers, each an era of this lesson. On a VM you may see kvm, microsoft or vmware; on bare metal, none.

  4. 4 · Compare with your laptop

    Run the same three commands on your own computer (WSL counts as a VM: you will see Microsoft / Hyper-V).

    Write down: physical or virtual?

Interview and viva questions

What is the difference between parallel and distributed computing?

Parallel computing splits one task across many processors that usually share memory in one machine; distributed computing uses many separate computers that talk over a network. Clusters, grids and clouds are all forms of distributed computing.

Is cloud the same as mainframe?

Same sharing idea, but self-service, internet access, elastic scale and millions of commodity servers.

Why did Amazon start a cloud?

It had learned to run huge infrastructure for its retail business and turned that into products (commonly told story).

Will the cloud keep growing?

Yes: AI, data and edge services keep adding services; the SDK still adds new ones every year.

Do exams ask dates?

Rarely exact dates; they ask benefits and evolution concepts.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

History interview questions.

Any companyFresher

“How did cloud computing evolve?”

A common warm-up question.

The interviewer is checking: Can you tell it as a cause-and-effect story?

Show a strong answer

“From mainframe time-sharing, to PCs, to client-server and the web, to grid and utility computing, to virtualization — and then providers added self-service, metering and internet access: AWS in 2006, Google App Engine in 2008, Azure in 2010. Today it continues with containers, serverless and AI services.”

Avoid: “Amazon invented the cloud in 2006.”

TrainingFresher

Cluster vs grid vs cloud

Asked in C-DAC and university vivas.

The interviewer is checking: Do you know the paradigms?

Show a strong answer
  • Cluster: tightly coupled machines in one place acting as one.
  • Grid: loosely coupled machines across organisations for big jobs.
  • Cloud: on-demand, self-service, metered resources for anyone.

Avoid: Saying they are all the same.

ConsultingMid-level

Is virtualization the same as cloud?

A client says “we already have VMware, so we have a cloud”.

The interviewer is checking: Do you know what makes a cloud?

Show a strong answer
  • Virtualization is the technology; a cloud also needs self-service, elasticity, metering and broad access (NIST).
  • A VMware farm becomes a private cloud only with a self-service portal and automation.

Avoid: Agreeing without checking.

ProductMid-level

Why is serverless “the next step”?

Team debates VMs vs functions.

The interviewer is checking: Can you link history to design choices?

Show a strong answer
  • Each era removed more ownership: hardware → OS → servers.
  • Serverless bills per call/ms and scales to zero, the utility idea taken furthest.

Avoid: “It is just a fashion.”
