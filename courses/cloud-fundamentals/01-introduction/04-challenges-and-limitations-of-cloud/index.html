2 Definition

Challenges and limitations of cloud computing

Formal definition (write this in exams)

The challenges of cloud computing are the risks and constraints of consuming shared, remote, provider-operated infrastructure: availability and network dependency, security and privacy, cost management, vendor lock-in, regulatory compliance and data residency, latency, reduced control, skills shortage and migration complexity.

In simple words

The cloud can still go down, can be set up wrongly, can cost more than expected, can be hard to leave and must follow data laws — so engineers plan for each.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “Cloud has trade-offs: downtime and internet dependency, security and privacy risks mostly from customer misconfiguration, cost overruns, vendor lock-in, compliance and data residency, latency, limited control and a skills gap.”
  • “We reduce them with multi-AZ or multi-region design, least-privilege IAM, MFA and encryption, budgets and tagging, open standards like containers and Terraform, and Indian regions for regulated data.”
  • “For example, a public storage bucket is the classic leak; Block Public Access and audit logs prevent and trace it.”

Typical questions: “Which is a disadvantage of cloud?” (internet dependency, lock-in, less control); “How to improve availability?” (multiple AZs/regions); “Who causes most breaches?” (customer misconfiguration). 3 The big picture

A map of where things go wrong

The cloud solves many problems (Lesson 1.3) but creates new ones. A good engineer knows where each problem can appear. Follow a request from the user to the provider: every box has its own risks.

Notice that most red boxes sit in your account, not at the provider. That is why “the cloud is insecure” is usually wrong: misconfiguration by customers causes most incidents.

5 Explained part by part

Nine challenges and their fixes

1. Downtime and internet dependency

Provider incidents and internet outages make apps unreachable.

fix: multi-AZ, multi-region, DR

2. Security and privacy

Mostly customer misconfiguration: public buckets, leaked keys, weak passwords.

fix: MFA, least privilege, encryption

3. Cost overrun

Forgotten VMs, NAT gateways, idle IPs and data transfer out.

fix: budgets, tags, cleanup

4. Vendor lock-in

Proprietary services and egress fees make moving hard.

fix: containers, Terraform, exit plan

5. Compliance and data residency

Laws like India’s DPDP Act and RBI rules decide where data may live.

fix: Indian regions, audits

6. Latency

Users far from the region feel delay.

fix: nearest region, CDN

7. Limited control

You cannot choose hardware or patch times of managed services.

fix: SLAs, support plans

8. Skills gap

Teams new to cloud make costly mistakes.

fix: training, certification, guardrails

9. Migration complexity

Old apps were not built for the cloud.

fix: 7 Rs strategy

10. Noisy neighbours

On shared hardware another customer’s heavy VM can slow yours a little.

fix: right instance type, dedicated options

11. Interoperability and portability

Data formats and APIs differ between clouds, so moving or mixing them is work.

fix: open formats (Parquet, SQL), containers

12. Multi-tenancy isolation

Many customers share hardware; a flaw in isolation would be serious, so providers harden hypervisors.

fix: provider certifications, encryption

13. Understanding SLAs

An SLA promises e.g. 99.99% and pays service credits, not your lost sales.

fix: design for failure anyway (Lesson 5.4)

    Counter(i["severity"] for i in last_12_months)

    end - begin in hours; median, 90th percentile, longest
    == 3) how long incidents lasted (hours) median 10.7 h · 90th percentile 516.0 h · longest 516.0 h

8 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

Check the health of three clouds and read incidents yourself. No account needed.

  1. 1 · Google incidents as JSON

    curl -s https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents.json | jq '.[] | {begin, severity, d: .external_desc[0:60]}' | head -40
    Each object is one incident.

  2. 2 · Filter India

    curl -s https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents.json | jq -r '.[] | select(tostring | test("asia-south")) | .begin + " " + .external_desc[0:70]'
    Matches Step 4.

  3. 3 · AWS and Azure status pages

    Open health.aws.amazon.com/health/status and azure.status.microsoft; filter Asia Pacific (Mumbai) / Central India.

    Note the format each uses.

  4. 4 · Run the lesson script

    python3 l14_challenges.py
    Same output style as Steps 1–5.

Interview and viva questions

Is the cloud less secure than my own server?

Usually more secure at the physical/infra level; risk moves to your configuration.

Can I avoid lock-in fully?

No, but containers, Terraform, open formats and an exit plan reduce it.

What if the internet is down?

Users cannot reach the cloud; use redundant ISPs for offices, and design apps to degrade gracefully.

Do providers pay for outages?

SLAs give service credits, not your lost business (Lesson 5.4).

Incident call.

The interviewer is checking: Can you respond step by step?

Show a strong answer
  • Block public access now; check access logs for downloads.
  • Rotate any exposed keys, inform security/legal (DPDP), add guardrails (account-level block, config rules).

Avoid: Deleting logs or hiding it.
