Join strategies

The same join can be 4x faster or slower depending on the physical strategy. This lesson forces broadcast hash, shuffled hash and sort-merge joins with hints, counts shuffles in the plan, tests a dangerous forced broadcast, and removes the shuffle with bucketing. All outputs are real (PySpark 4.3.0).

1 The story

“Same join, four times faster”

Before you start: join types in 6.2, plans in 7.3, AQE in 7.4.

TiffinGo’s loyalty job joins every order with the customer tier table. After someone set spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold=-1 to “fix” an out-of-memory error elsewhere, the job silently became 4x slower.

Kabir

Sir, a join is a join. How can the same join be 4x slower?

Arjun Sir

Spark has several physical join strategies. If one side is small, it copies it to every executor and never moves the big table. Otherwise it shuffles both sides by the key, and then either sorts them or builds hash tables.

Riya

And we can choose?

Arjun Sir

Spark chooses using size estimates and thresholds, and you can override it with hints. Today we force each strategy, time it, read the plans, and see how bucketing removes the shuffle for repeated joins. All outputs are real (PySpark 4.3.0).

2 Definition

Physical join strategies

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Spark SQL implements equi-joins with three main physical operators. A broadcast hash join (BHJ) sends the smaller relation to every executor and builds a hash table there, so the larger side is not shuffled; it is chosen when a side’s estimated size is below spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold (10 MB) or a broadcast hint is given. A sort-merge join (SMJ) shuffles both sides by the join key, sorts each partition and merges them; it is the default for large inputs. A shuffled hash join (SHJ) shuffles both sides and builds a hash table from the smaller side per partition, avoiding the sort. Non-equi joins use a broadcast nested loop join or a cartesian product. Hints (BROADCAST, MERGE, SHUFFLE_HASH, SHUFFLE_REPLICATE_NL) override the choice; bucketed tables can skip the shuffle.

In simple words

Spark can join by copying a small table to every machine (broadcast), or by moving both tables so matching keys meet and then sorting (sort-merge) or hashing (shuffled hash). Broadcast avoids moving the big table and is usually fastest; the others work for any size. Hints let you choose.

Join strategies at three levels

Pick your level. New to this? Start at Beginner and climb.

Beginner Matching guests to tables

  • Broadcast: give every waiter a copy of the short guest list. Nobody moves.
  • Sort-merge: both long lists are sorted by name and walked together.
  • Shuffle hash: split both lists by first letter, then use a lookup table per letter.

Intermediate The API

  • F.broadcast(df) or df.hint("broadcast"), "merge", "shuffle_hash", "shuffle_replicate_nl"; SQL /*+ BROADCAST(c) */.
  • spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold (10 MB), spark.sql.join.preferSortMergeJoin (true).
  • df.write.bucketBy(n, "key").sortBy("key").saveAsTable(...).

Advanced What the lab showed

  • Broadcast 1.55 s (8 tasks), shuffled hash 4.53 s, sort-merge 6.73 s (208 tasks each).
  • The broadcast plan had 0 hash Exchanges; sort-merge had 2.
  • Bucketed tables: sort-merge with 0 Exchanges, 1.68 s vs 5.31 s.

Exam tip: “Explain Spark join strategies.” Broadcast hash join copies the small side to all executors and avoids shuffling the big side; it is chosen below autoBroadcastJoinThreshold (10 MB) or with a hint. Sort-merge join shuffles and sorts both sides and is the default for large tables. Shuffled hash join shuffles both and hashes the smaller side per partition. Non-equi joins use broadcast nested loop or cartesian joins. Bucketing on the join key lets sort-merge skip the shuffle.

3 Types and ways to use it

Hints in DataFrame and SQL

HintDataFrameSQLPlan in our lab
BroadcastF.broadcast(c) / c.hint("broadcast")/*+ BROADCAST(c) */BroadcastHashJoin
Mergec.hint("merge")/*+ MERGE(c) */SortMergeJoin
Shuffle hashc.hint("shuffle_hash")/*+ SHUFFLE_HASH(c) */ShuffledHashJoin
Replicate NLc.hint("shuffle_replicate_nl")/*+ SHUFFLE_REPLICATE_NL(c) */CartesianProduct

A broadcast hint is an order, not a suggestion: Spark will try to collect the side on the driver and send it to every executor, even a huge one. Our forced broadcast of 5 million rows worked on a 3 GB driver (5.32 s), but on a cluster a mis-estimated side causes driver OOM or a broadcast timeout. Setting the threshold to -1 everywhere is the opposite mistake: every small dimension join becomes a shuffle.

4 Step by step

Forcing each strategy

All outputs are real: PySpark 4.3.0, local[4], AQE off (so the plan does not change at run time), 5,000,000 orders joined on customer_id to 4,000 customers. Strategies are read from the executed plan; timings are warm runs.

  1. Step 1 · Spark’s automatic choice

    cust = spark.range(1, 4001).select(F.col("id").cast("int").alias("customer_id"), (F.col("id") % 5).alias("tier"))
print(strat(o.join(cust, "customer_id")))
spark.conf.set("spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold", -1)
print(strat(o.join(cust, "customer_id")))
    default autoBroadcastJoinThreshold: 10485760b == 1) auto choice small dim (4000 rows) -> BroadcastHashJoin threshold -1 -> SortMergeJoin
    The customer side is tiny, far below 10 MB, so Spark picked a broadcast hash join by itself. Disabling the threshold with -1 forced it back to sort-merge, the default for “big” joins.

  2. Step 2 · Hints and timings

    for h in ("broadcast", "merge", "shuffle_hash", "shuffle_replicate_nl"):
    print(h, strat(o.join(cust.hint(h), "customer_id")))
for h in ("broadcast", "merge", "shuffle_hash"):
    run(f"join with {h}", sink(o.join(cust.hint(h), "customer_id")))
    == 2) hints (threshold -1, so only hint decides) hint broadcast -> BroadcastHashJoin hint merge -> SortMergeJoin hint shuffle_hash -> ShuffledHashJoin hint shuffle_replicate_nl -> CartesianProduct join with broadcast : 1.55 s, tasks 8, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB join with merge : 6.73 s, tasks 208, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB join with shuffle_hash : 4.53 s, tasks 208, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB
    Each hint produced exactly the requested operator. Broadcast: 1.55 s with 8 tasks (just scanning orders). Shuffled hash: 4.53 s. Sort-merge: 6.73 s, 4.3x slower, with 208 tasks because both sides were shuffled into 200 partitions and sorted. shuffle_replicate_nl on an equi-join gave a CartesianProduct: never use it for normal keys.

  3. Step 3 · Read the plan, count Exchanges

    for h in ("broadcast", "merge"):
    p = o.join(cust.hint(h), "customer_id")._jdf.queryExecution().executedPlan().toString()
    print(h, p.count("Exchange hashpartitioning"), p.count("BroadcastExchange"))
    == 3) exchanges in plan broadcast -> Exchange hashpartitioning: 0 | BroadcastExchange: 1 merge -> Exchange hashpartitioning: 2 | BroadcastExchange: 0
    The quickest way to understand a join’s cost: count Exchange hashpartitioning nodes. Each is a full shuffle of one side. Broadcast had 0 (plus one cheap BroadcastExchange of 4,000 rows); sort-merge had 2, moving all 5 million orders across the network.

  4. Step 4 · Forcing a broadcast of a big side

    o2 = o.select("order_id", "amount")          # 5,000,000 rows
run("broadcast 5M-row side", sink(o.join(F.broadcast(o2), "order_id")))
run("sort-merge same join",  sink(o.join(o2, "order_id")))
    == 4) forcing broadcast of a BIG side (5M rows) broadcast 5M-row side : 5.32 s, tasks 8, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB sort-merge same join : 7.62 s, tasks 208, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB
    It worked here and was even faster (5.32 s vs 7.62 s), because we run in one process with a 3 GB driver. On a cluster the driver collects the side, builds the hash table and ships it to every executor: a few hundred MB per executor, times every concurrent job. Spark refuses broadcasts above 8 GB and fails on driver memory or spark.sql.broadcastTimeout (300 s) long before that.

  5. Step 5 · Bucketing removes the shuffle

    o.write.bucketBy(16, "customer_id").sortBy("customer_id").saveAsTable("ob")
cust.write.bucketBy(16, "customer_id").sortBy("customer_id").saveAsTable("cb")
b = spark.table("ob").join(spark.table("cb"), "customer_id")   # threshold -1
    == 5) bucketed tables: join without shuffle bucketed join: SortMergeJoin | Exchange count: 0 plain join: SortMergeJoin | Exchange count: 2 bucketed SMJ : 1.68 s, tasks 16, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB plain SMJ : 5.31 s, tasks 208, spill mem 0.0 MiB disk 0.0 MiB
    Both tables were written in 16 buckets hashed by customer_id. The join is still a sort-merge join, but with 0 Exchanges: bucket 3 of orders only needs bucket 3 of customers. 1.68 s vs 5.31 s. The shuffle was paid once at write time, so bucketing pays off when the same big join runs many times. Both sides need the same bucket column and compatible bucket counts.

6 Real-world use

Join strategies at work

1. Fact-to-dimension joins

Orders joined to cities, restaurants and plans: every dimension under the threshold is broadcast.

Fact→broadcast dims→No shuffle

2. Large-to-large joins

Sessions joined to clickstream use sort-merge; tuning focuses on partitions and skew.

Big→sort-merge→Big

3. Repeated joins

Tables joined many times a day on the same key are bucketed or clustered once.

Write once→bucketBy→Join many

4. Range joins

Matching GPS pings to time windows uses non-equi conditions; keep one side small to get a broadcast nested loop join.

ts BETWEEN→BNLJ (small side)→Matches

5. Code reviews

Teams require a comment on every broadcast hint explaining why the side is small.

Hint→size proof→Safe merge

7 Run the code

Run the code: choosing a join

A simplified version of Spark’s join selection. Press Run, then try the challenges.

# How Spark picks a join strategy (simplified)
def choose(small_mb, threshold_mb=10, equi=True, hint=None):
    if hint:
        return hint
    if not equi:
        return "BroadcastNestedLoopJoin" if small_mb <= threshold_mb else "CartesianProduct"
    if threshold_mb >= 0 and small_mb <= threshold_mb:
        return "BroadcastHashJoin"
    return "SortMergeJoin"

print(choose(0.06))                    # 4,000 customers
print(choose(0.06, threshold_mb=-1))   # broadcast disabled
print(choose(800))                     # big dimension
print(choose(800, hint="ShuffledHashJoin"))

  • return "SortMergeJoin"spark.sql.join.preferSortMergeJoin=true makes this the default for big sides.

9 Quiz and revision

Check yourself

Quick revision

Lesson 8.2 in one screen

  • BHJ: copy small side, no shuffle of the big side (1.55 s).
  • SMJ: shuffle + sort both (6.73 s); SHJ: shuffle + hash (4.53 s).
  • Threshold 10 MB; -1 disables broadcast globally (dangerous).
  • Hints: broadcast, merge, shuffle_hash, shuffle_replicate_nl; SQL /*+ BROADCAST(t) */.
  • Count Exchange hashpartitioning nodes to see shuffles.
  • Forced broadcasts of big sides risk driver OOM and timeouts.
  • Bucketing on the join key: SMJ with 0 Exchanges (1.68 s vs 5.31 s).

Interview and viva questions

Which side should be broadcast?

The smaller side after filters, and for outer joins only the side that is allowed to be the build side (for a left join, the right side).

Why did Spark not broadcast a 6 MB Parquet table?

Size estimates use statistics; in memory the data may be much larger, or a filter or UDF may make the estimate unknown. Check the plan, use AQE or a hint.

Is shuffled hash join better than sort-merge?

Often faster because it skips the sort, but each partition of the build side must fit in memory. Spark 4 picks it more often when preferSortMergeJoin is false or a hint is given.

Does bucketing work with plain Parquet paths?

No, the bucket spec lives in the catalog; read it as a table with spark.table.

What is a storage-partitioned join?

A V2 data source feature (e.g. Iceberg) where tables partitioned the same way are joined without a shuffle, similar to bucketing.

Real-world scenario questions (asked in company interviews)

Join strategy questions are a favourite in Spark interviews.

Any company · SparkFresher

“What is a broadcast join?”

Explain broadcast joins.

The interviewer is checking: when used?

Show a strong answer

Spark sends the small table to every executor and builds a hash table, so the large table is joined where it sits without a shuffle. It is used automatically below autoBroadcastJoinThreshold (10 MB) or with F.broadcast. In my test it was 4.3x faster than sort-merge.

Avoid: Broadcasting large tables.

Data engineeringMid-level

Slow dimension join

Joining orders to a 5 MB city table takes 10 minutes.

The interviewer is checking: what do you check?

Show a strong answer
  • The plan: SortMergeJoin with two Exchanges?
  • The threshold setting (maybe -1) and size estimates.
  • Add a broadcast hint or rely on AQE; verify BroadcastHashJoin.

Avoid: Adding executors.

Data engineeringMid-level

Broadcast OOM

A job fails with driver OutOfMemory during a broadcast.

The interviewer is checking: fix?

Show a strong answer
  1. Find which side is broadcast; its real size grew.
  2. Remove the hint or lower the threshold for that query.
  3. Use shuffled hash or sort-merge for that join.

Avoid: Raising driver memory forever.

PlatformSenior

Repeated big joins

Two 1 TB tables are joined on the same key 20 times a day.

The interviewer is checking: design?

Show a strong answer
  • Bucket (or cluster/partition the same way in Iceberg/Delta) both on the key.
  • Verify zero Exchanges in the join plan.
  • Keep bucket counts aligned as data grows.

Avoid: Relying on more shuffle partitions only.
