1 The story

Eight systems, one consultant’s answer

Before you start: Lessons 2.1–2.4.

🤔 TiffinGo is moving 8 systems to the cloud: the order API, the website, accounting, the kitchen billing PC, email, a 10-year-old Java reporting tool, an Excel-macro inventory “system” and a legacy fax gateway. Should all of them go to EC2?

No — each system deserves its own answer, and the answer is a service model (IaaS, PaaS, SaaS, serverless) reached through a migration strategy. The industry uses the 7 Rs: retire, retain, rehost, relocate, replatform, repurchase, refactor — and each one lands on a different model. This lesson gives you a decision path, the trade-offs in one table, the classic “pizza as a service” picture, and the 7 Rs one by one — then on your own account you price the same app three ways and get right-sizing recommendations.

  • The 7 Rs of migration — and which service model each one produces.
  • Why the cheapest per-hour option is rarely the cheapest overall.

A consultant suggested moving all eight TiffinGo systems to virtual machines. The founder wanted to know whether one answer could really be right for email, a fax gateway and a brand-new order API.

2 Definition

IaaS vs PaaS vs SaaS: choosing a model

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Choosing a cloud service model means deciding, per workload, how much of the technology stack the organisation manages — IaaS (infrastructure; you manage OS and up), PaaS (platform; you manage application and data), SaaS (software; you manage users, settings and data) or serverless (functions and managed services) — based on control, speed, skills, total cost of ownership, compliance and portability, typically reached through migration strategies known as the 7 Rs.

In simple words

For each system, decide how much you want to run yourself: the computer (IaaS), just your code (PaaS/serverless), or nothing but the settings (SaaS).

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “I choose per workload: SaaS if a product fits, serverless for event-driven pieces, PaaS for standard apps and managed databases, and IaaS when I need OS control or for fast lift-and-shift.”
  • “Migration strategies are the 7 Rs — retire, retain, rehost, relocate, replatform, repurchase, refactor — each landing on a different model.”

Type 1 · Retire

Definition (say it in an interview): Retire means decommissioning applications that are no longer useful, after archiving any data that must be kept — removing them from the migration scope entirely.

In plain words (no jargon): Switching off old systems that nobody needs any more instead of moving them.

Analogy: Clearing the loft before moving house: why pay movers to carry things nobody has used in years?

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Discover. Inventory + usage metrics.
  2. Confirm. Business owner sign-off.
  3. Retain data. Export and archive per policy.
  4. Decommission. Shut down, remove access, cancel licences.
  5. Record. Update the asset register.

Words used above, in simple terms: decommission = permanently take out of service · archive = keep data cheaply for rare access · asset register = list of a company’s systems

Real-world example

AWS migration guidance commonly reports that a portfolio assessment finds a noticeable share of applications (often around 10%) that can simply be retired — savings before any migration starts.

Real scenario (TiffinGo)

TiffinGo’s fax gateway had zero traffic since 2022: its few old records went to S3 Glacier Deep Archive and the server was switched off — one less system to migrate, patch or pay for.

Products and cloud services

For the experienced — did you know? Retiring also shrinks the attack surface: unpatched forgotten servers are classic entry points for attackers.

Use it when: a system has little or no use, or its function exists elsewhere.
Avoid / be careful when: legal/regulatory retention requires access to its data — archive properly first.

Type 2 · Retain (revisit)

Definition (say it in an interview): Retain means keeping an application where it is — usually on-premises — for now, because of constraints such as latency to local equipment, unsupported hardware, regulation, licensing, recent investment or business priority, while connecting it to the cloud and revisiting later.

In plain words (no jargon): Keeping some systems where they are for now, because there is a good reason they cannot move yet.

Analogy: Keeping grandmother’s heavy wooden almirah in the old house until the new flat has a strong enough wall.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Record reason. Latency, hardware, regulation, licence, cost.
  2. Integrate. Hybrid networking and identity.
  3. Protect. Backups/DR possibly to the cloud.
  4. Set review date. Re-assess with new services.
  5. Plan exit. What must change to move it.

Words used above, in simple terms: hybrid = combining on-premises and cloud · latency = delay; local equipment may need very low delay · review date = when to reconsider the decision

Use it when: there is a real constraint or no business case yet.
Avoid / be careful when: “retain” becomes the default for everything difficult — it should come with a reason and a review date.

Type 3 · Rehost (lift and shift) → IaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Rehost (“lift and shift”) moves an application with its operating system to cloud virtual machines without changing its code or architecture, typically using block-level replication tools — the fastest migration path, landing on IaaS.

In plain words (no jargon): Copying a server exactly as it is into the cloud, without changing the software.

Analogy: Moving house with all your furniture exactly as it is — quick, but the old sofa still doesn’t fit the new room perfectly.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Discover. Inventory servers and dependencies.
  2. Replicate. Agent-based continuous disk replication.
  3. Test. Launch test instances in the cloud.
  4. Cut over. Final sync, switch DNS/users.
  5. Optimise later. Right-size, Savings Plans, replatform.

Words used above, in simple terms: lift and shift = move without changes · cutover = the moment users switch to the new system · replication agent = software that copies disk changes

Use it when: deadlines (data-centre exit), many servers, or as a first step before optimising.
Avoid / be careful when: you never come back to optimise — you keep paying for over-sized VMs and manual patching.

Type 4 · Relocate (hypervisor-level move)

Definition (say it in an interview): Relocate moves virtual machines at the hypervisor level — for example from on-premises VMware vSphere to a VMware environment running on cloud bare metal — without converting or changing the VMs, often with live migration; the workloads land on a cloud-hosted private-cloud style IaaS.

In plain words (no jargon): Moving whole groups of virtual machines to the same kind of virtualization software running in the cloud, without changing the machines.

Analogy: Moving an entire shop’s shelves on a truck as they are, then placing them in the new mall unit — products are not even taken off the shelves.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Provision. Managed VMware private cloud on bare metal.
  2. Connect. Network extension (HCX, ExpressRoute/VPN).
  3. Migrate. Bulk or live vMotion.
  4. Operate. Same vCenter tools.
  5. Modernise. Later move to native services.

Words used above, in simple terms: vMotion = VMware’s live VM migration · HCX = VMware tool for large-scale migration and network extension · private cloud (here) = a dedicated VMware environment in a public cloud

Type 5 · Replatform (lift and reshape) → PaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Replatform (“lift, tinker and shift”) moves an application to the cloud while making targeted optimisations — such as moving a self-managed database to a managed database, the app to an application platform or containers — without changing its core architecture, landing on PaaS services.

In plain words (no jargon): Moving an app to the cloud and, on the way, swapping some self-managed parts for ready-made cloud services.

Analogy: Moving house and replacing the old gas stove with the new building’s piped-gas stove: same kitchen routine, less maintenance.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Assess. Which components have managed equivalents?
  2. Adapt. Config changes, small code tweaks (storage, sessions).
  3. Migrate data. Database Migration Service / DMS.
  4. Deploy. App platform or containers.
  5. Operate. Provider handles patching/backups.

Words used above, in simple terms: replatform = move with small changes to use managed services · managed instance = a nearly fully compatible managed database (e.g. SQL Server) · DMS = database migration service

Use it when: clear operational wins (managed DB, managed runtime) for modest change.
Avoid / be careful when: hidden dependencies (local file writes, OS tools) break on the platform — test thoroughly.

Type 6 · Repurchase (drop and shop) → SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Repurchase (“drop and shop”) replaces an existing application with a different product — usually SaaS — moving data and users to it and retiring the old system.

In plain words (no jargon): Throwing away the old software and buying a ready-made online product that does the same job.

Analogy: Selling your old car and switching to a monthly cab subscription for office travel.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Requirements. What must the new product do?
  2. Select. Evaluate SaaS (Lesson 2.3 checklist).
  3. Migrate. Data import, integrations, SSO.
  4. Train. Users and admins.
  5. Retire. Switch off the old system.

Words used above, in simple terms: drop and shop = replace instead of moving · data migration = moving records to the new system · change management = helping people adopt the new way

Type 7 · Refactor / re-architect → cloud-native

Definition (say it in an interview): Refactor (re-architect) changes an application’s architecture and code to use cloud-native capabilities — microservices, serverless functions, managed databases, event-driven integration, containers — to gain scalability, agility and efficiency that the existing design cannot reach; it is the highest-effort, highest-benefit strategy.

In plain words (no jargon): Redesigning and rewriting an app so it uses the cloud’s modern services properly.

Analogy: Rebuilding an old house into a modern apartment with lifts, solar panels and piped gas — expensive, but life afterwards is much easier.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Business case. Which limits must be removed?
  2. Domain design. Boundaries, events, data ownership.
  3. Incremental. Strangler fig, feature by feature.
  4. Automate. IaC, CI/CD, observability.
  5. Optimise. Costs, performance, reliability.

Words used above, in simple terms: cloud-native = designed for the cloud: elastic, managed, automated · re-architect = change the structure of a system · strangler fig = replace a system piece by piece

6 Explained part by part

Six factors that decide the model

Control

What it is: How much of the stack you can change.

Why it exists: Special software or OS settings need IaaS.

Where it is used: Kernel modules, agents, licensed software.

Speed to market

What it is: How quickly you can deliver.

Why it exists: PaaS/SaaS/serverless start in minutes.

Where it is used: MVPs, campaigns, new products.

Skills

What it is: What your team can operate.

Why it exists: IaaS needs sysadmin skills; SaaS needs admin skills.

Where it is used: Small teams favour managed models.

Total cost of ownership

What it is: All costs over years, including people.

Why it exists: Per-hour prices mislead (Lesson 5.5).

Where it is used: Ops hours, licences, downtime.

Compliance and residency

What it is: Where data lives and who controls it.

Why it exists: May restrict SaaS choices or require dedicated options.

Where it is used: DPDP Act, RBI, sector rules.

Portability / lock-in

What it is: How hard it is to move later.

Why it exists: IaaS most portable; SaaS least.

Where it is used: Containers and open standards help.

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: IaaS is cheapest because VMs are cheap per hour.
    Reality: Staff time and idle capacity often make it the most expensive.
    Proof: Deep dive TCO.
  • Myth: Everything must be refactored to benefit from cloud.
    Reality: Rehost, replatform and repurchase all have their place.
    Proof: The 7 Rs.
  • Myth: One model per company.
    Reality: Models are mixed per component.
    Proof: Deep dive.
7 Deep dive

Mixing models, TCO example, migration frameworks

9 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

These labs run on your own cloud account — we cannot run them for you. Each step has three tabs (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud); pick the cloud you have and do both the UI way and the CLI way. Stay inside free tier / credit limits, use the India Region (AWS ap-south-1 Mumbai · Azure Central India · Google asia-south1 Mumbai), and always do the Clean up step at the end. Both steps only read prices and recommendations — nothing is created that costs money.

  1. 0 · Get ready: account, budget, CLI

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Create an AWS account (aws.amazon.com → Create an AWS Account); choose the Free plan while learning.
    2. Never work as root: IAM Identity Center (or IAM) → create an admin user with MFA and sign in as that user.
    3. Billing and Cost Management → Budgets → Create budget → monthly cost budget, e.g. $5, alert by e-mail (Lesson 5.5).
    4. Top-right Region menu → Asia Pacific (Mumbai). Open CloudShell (terminal icon) — the AWS CLI is already signed in there.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws sts get-caller-identity          # who am I? (must NOT be the root user)
aws configure set region ap-south-1
aws configure list

    You should see: your account ID and an IAM user/role ARN — not “root”.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Create a free account at azure.microsoft.com/free (credit for the first 30 days).
    2. Search Cost Management → Budgets → Add → monthly budget, e.g. ₹400, alert at 80%.
    3. Open Cloud Shell (the >_ icon in the top bar) → choose Bash. The Azure CLI is already signed in.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az account show --output table        # subscription in use
az account list-locations --query "[?name=='centralindia'].displayName" -o tsv

    You should see: your subscription name and state “Enabled”, then “Central India”.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

  2. 1 · Price the same web app three ways

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Open calculator.aws → Create estimate → Add group three times: IaaS, PaaS, Serverless.
    2. IaaS: EC2 t3.small, Linux, Mumbai, 730 h. PaaS: Elastic Beanstalk itself is free — price the EC2 (+ load balancer if multi-instance) it creates. Serverless: Lambda 1 million requests/month, 256 MB, 200 ms, plus API Gateway HTTP API 1 million requests.
    3. View summary → compare monthly cost — then add ~4 hours/month of patching time to the IaaS row yourself.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws pricing get-products --region us-east-1 --service-code AmazonEC2 \
  --filters Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=instanceType,Value=t3.small Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=regionCode,Value=ap-south-1 \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=operatingSystem,Value=Linux Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=tenancy,Value=Shared \
            Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=preInstalledSw,Value=NA Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=capacitystatus,Value=Used \
  --query 'PriceList[0]' --output text | python3 -c "import sys,json; d=json.loads(sys.stdin.read()); t=list(d['terms']['OnDemand'].values())[0]; print(list(t['priceDimensions'].values())[0]['pricePerUnit'])"

    You should see: the On-Demand hourly price in USD for t3.small in Mumbai; × 730 = the monthly IaaS compute cost. Lambda at this volume is mostly inside the free tier.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Open the Azure Pricing calculator → add Virtual Machines (B1s, Central India), App Service (Linux, Basic B1) and Azure Functions (Flex Consumption, 1M executions).
    2. Compare monthly totals; note what each includes (App Service includes the runtime, TLS, scaling settings).

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    curl -s "https://prices.azure.com/api/retail/prices?\$filter=serviceName%20eq%20'Azure%20App%20Service'%20and%20armRegionName%20eq%20'centralindia'%20and%20skuName%20eq%20'B1'%20and%20priceType%20eq%20'Consumption'" \
  | python3 -c "import sys,json; [print(i['productName'], i['meterName'], i['retailPrice'], i['unitOfMeasure']) for i in json.load(sys.stdin)['Items'][:5]]"

    You should see: retail prices for App Service B1 in Central India per hour from Azure’s public price API (no sign-in needed).

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Open the Google Cloud Pricing Calculator → add Compute Engine (e2-small, asia-south1, 730 h), App Engine standard (F1/F2), and Cloud Run (1M requests, 0.25 vCPU, 256 MiB).
    2. Compare; Cloud Run’s free tier covers small apps.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    gcloud compute machine-types describe e2-small --zone=asia-south1-a --format="value(guestCpus,memoryMb)"
# Prices come from the calculator or the Cloud Billing Catalog API (needs an API key); the CLI shows the shape you are pricing.

    You should see: e2-small = 2 shared vCPUs, 2,048 MB — the machine you priced.

    Per-hour prices are only half of TCO — add the people-hours each model needs.

  3. 2 · Right-size before (and after) you choose

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Compute Optimizer → Get started → Opt in (free).
    2. After about 30 hours of metrics, EC2 instances shows each instance as over-provisioned, under-provisioned or optimized, with cheaper suggestions.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws compute-optimizer update-enrollment-status --status Active
aws compute-optimizer get-enrollment-status
aws compute-optimizer get-ec2-instance-recommendations --query "instanceRecommendations[].[instanceArn,finding,recommendationOptions[0].instanceType]" --output table

    You should see: Active, then (once there is data) a table of findings; empty on an account without running instances.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Advisor → Cost → “Right-size or shut down underutilized virtual machines” and reservation suggestions.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az advisor recommendation list --category Cost --query "[].{problem:shortDescription.problem, impact:impact, resource:impactedValue}" --output table

    You should see: cost recommendations (empty if you have no long-running VMs).

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Recommendations → Cost → machine-type (“rightsizing”) and idle-VM recommendations per zone.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    P=$(gcloud config get-value project)
gcloud recommender recommendations list --project=$P --location=asia-south1-a \
  --recommender=google.compute.instance.MachineTypeRecommender --format="table(description,primaryImpact.costProjection.cost.units)"

    You should see: machine-type recommendations with projected savings (needs VMs with a few days of metrics).

    Right-sizing is part of choosing a model: a rehosted VM that is 4× too big may make IaaS look more expensive than it should — or hide that PaaS is cheaper.

Clean up and cost check

Nothing to delete. On AWS you can opt out of Compute Optimizer with aws compute-optimizer update-enrollment-status --status Inactive (it is free either way).

10 Mistakes to avoid

Common mistakes, why they happen, how to fix them

Mistake or symptomWhy it happensHow to fix or avoid
One model for the whole company.Standardisation instinct.Decide per workload with the decision path.
Lift and shift everything, never optimise.Deadline pressure.Plan waves: rehost → right-size → replatform.
Comparing only per-hour prices.Easy to compare.Compare 3-year TCO including staff time and downtime.
Refactoring without a business case.Technology enthusiasm.Refactor where scale/agility limits hurt; repurchase or rehost elsewhere.
Forgetting retire.Inventory not checked.Usage-based portfolio assessment first.
Ignoring data residency in SaaS choices.Feature focus.Check Region, certifications and export before buying.
