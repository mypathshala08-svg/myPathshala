1 The story

Six apps, zero servers

Before you start: 2.2 PaaS.

🤔 TiffinGo uses Gmail, Zoho Books, Freshdesk, Canva, Razorpay and Zoom — and owns zero servers for any of them. When the support tool had an outage, whose problem was it? And when an ex-employee still had access to the accounts software, whose problem was that?

The first was the vendor’s; the second was TiffinGo’s. In Software as a Service (SaaS) the provider runs the entire application — infrastructure, platform, code, updates — and you simply use it through a browser, app or API, usually per user per month. But identity, access, configuration and your data stay your responsibility. This lesson explains horizontal and vertical SaaS, multi-tenant and single-tenant delivery, API-first and low-code SaaS, buying SaaS through cloud marketplaces, how a SaaS request reaches your company’s data — and on your own account you set up single sign-on for a SaaS app and track SaaS spend.

  • Seven ways SaaS is built and sold — including the one India is famous for.
  • How one SaaS app serves thousands of companies without mixing their data.
  • Why “the vendor handles everything” is the most dangerous SaaS myth.

TiffinGo runs on SaaS — mail, accounts, support, design, payments, meetings. One day the support tool went down; another day an ex-employee still had access. Two incidents, two different owners.

R> Sir, Freshdesk was down for an hour today. Should we have hosted our own helpdesk? A> Then the outage — and the patching, backups and upgrades — would be ours. With SaaS the vendor runs all of it; we check their status page and SLA. K> But Manoj left two months ago and could still log into Zoho Books. A> That one is on us. In SaaS we still own who has access, how it is configured, and our data. Single sign-on fixes it: disable him once, and every app locks him out. R> So SaaS is not “no responsibility”. A> It is “less responsibility”. Let’s see the types and where the line is.
2 Definition

What is SaaS?

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Software as a Service (SaaS) is a cloud service model in which the provider delivers a complete application running on cloud infrastructure, accessed by users over the internet through a browser, app or API, typically on a subscription or usage basis; the consumer does not manage the infrastructure, platform or application, apart from limited user-specific configuration — but remains responsible for its users, access settings and data.

In simple words

Ready-made software you use over the internet and pay for monthly — the company that makes it runs everything; you just log in and use it.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “SaaS is complete software delivered over the internet — Microsoft 365, Salesforce, Zoho. The vendor manages infrastructure, platform, code and updates; the customer manages users, access, configuration and data.”
  • “Most SaaS is multi-tenant: one application serves many customers with logical data isolation; enterprises may buy single-tenant editions.”

Type 1 · Horizontal SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Horizontal SaaS delivers software for a business function that is common across industries — email and productivity, collaboration, CRM, HR, accounting, customer support, design — to a broad market of customers of every type.

In plain words (no jargon): Software that every kind of business needs — like email, documents or accounting — rented online per user.

Analogy: A general store: rice, soap and stationery that every household buys, whatever the family’s profession.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Sign up. Create an organisation (tenant).
  2. Add users. Per-seat licences, often via SSO.
  3. Configure. Policies, roles, integrations.
  4. Use. Browser and mobile apps.
  5. Scale. Add/remove seats monthly.

Words used above, in simple terms: seat = one paid user licence · tenant / organisation = your company’s own space in the SaaS app · subscription = regular payment for continued use

Type 2 · Vertical SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Vertical SaaS delivers software designed for the workflows, data and regulations of one specific industry — such as healthcare, education, restaurants, logistics or banking — often combining several functions (operations, billing, compliance, integrations) for that sector.

In plain words (no jargon): Software made for one kind of business — like a clinic, a school or a restaurant — with all its special needs built in.

Analogy: A specialist medical store: it stocks exactly what clinics need, and the staff understand prescriptions and regulations.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Industry model. Data and screens follow the sector’s process.
  2. Compliance. Built-in rules and reports.
  3. Integrations. Sector devices, partners, government portals.
  4. Payments. Often embedded billing/payments.
  5. Updates. Regulatory changes delivered centrally.

Words used above, in simple terms: vertical = one industry · compliance = following rules and regulations · integration = connecting with other systems

Type 3 · Multi-tenant SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Multi-tenant SaaS serves many customer organisations (tenants) from a single shared deployment of the application and infrastructure, keeping each tenant’s data and configuration logically isolated, so that the vendor runs one version, scales economically and delivers updates to all tenants at once.

In plain words (no jargon): One copy of the software serves many companies at the same time, while each company’s data is kept separate and private.

Analogy: An apartment building: many families, one building, one lift and one water tank — each flat locked and private.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Onboard tenant. Create tenant record, admin user, settings.
  2. Identify. Tenant from domain/token on every request.
  3. Isolate. Row-level security, per-tenant schema or DB, per-tenant keys.
  4. Meter. Usage per tenant for billing and limits.
  5. Release. Feature flags per tenant; one codebase.

Words used above, in simple terms: tenant = one customer organisation · row-level security = database rules that show each tenant only its rows · noisy neighbour = a tenant whose load slows others

Use it when: standard SaaS for many customers — this is the default model.
Avoid / be careful when: a customer needs physical isolation, custom versions or its own encryption keys and the vendor cannot offer them.

Type 4 · Single-tenant (dedicated) SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Single-tenant SaaS gives each customer a dedicated instance of the application and data store — sometimes in the customer’s chosen Region or even its own cloud account — while the vendor still operates, patches and supports it; it trades higher cost for stronger isolation, customisation and control.

In plain words (no jargon): The SaaS company runs a separate private copy of the software just for you — more privacy and control, at a higher price.

Analogy: An independent bungalow in a gated colony managed by one maintenance company: your own house, but someone else fixes the plumbing.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Provision. Dedicated environment per customer (often by IaC).
  2. Locate. Customer’s Region or account.
  3. Secure. Customer-managed keys, private connectivity.
  4. Operate. Vendor patches, backs up, monitors.
  5. Upgrade. On an agreed schedule.

Words used above, in simple terms: dedicated instance = a copy used by only one customer · BYOC = bring your own cloud — the SaaS runs in the customer’s account · customer-managed key = an encryption key the customer controls

Avoid / be careful when: you choose it by default — it costs more and you may lag behind on features.

Type 5 · API-first SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): API-first SaaS delivers a complete capability — payments, messaging, maps, identity, search, email — primarily through APIs and SDKs that developers embed in their own applications, typically with usage-based pricing, instead of offering an end-user interface.

In plain words (no jargon): Ready-made services that programmers plug into their own apps — like payments or SMS — paying for each use.

Analogy: Hiring a courier company for every delivery: you do not build vans; you call them with an address and pay per parcel.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Account. API keys, sandbox environment.
  2. Integrate. SDK or HTTPS calls; webhooks for results.
  3. Secure. Keys in a secret store; verify webhook signatures.
  4. Go live. KYC/compliance where required.
  5. Monitor. Usage, errors, costs.

Words used above, in simple terms: API key = a secret that identifies your app to the service · webhook = the service calling your URL when something happens · sandbox = a test mode with fake money/messages

Avoid / be careful when: costs per call grow faster than your revenue — negotiate volume pricing or reconsider at scale.

Type 6 · Low-code / no-code SaaS

Definition (say it in an interview): Low-code / no-code SaaS platforms let business users and developers build applications, forms, workflows and automations through visual designers on top of hosted data and connectors, with the vendor running the resulting apps as SaaS.

In plain words (no jargon): Websites where you can make your own small apps by dragging and dropping, without writing much code.

Analogy: Building with LEGO instead of carving wood: ready-made blocks snap together quickly — fine for many things, not for everything.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Connect data. Sheets, tables, SaaS connectors.
  2. Design UI. Forms, lists, dashboards.
  3. Automate. Triggers, approvals, notifications.
  4. Secure. Sharing and roles via the organisation’s identity.
  5. Govern. IT sets policies (data loss prevention, environments).

Words used above, in simple terms: low-code = little programming needed · citizen developer = a business user who builds apps · connector = a ready-made link to another app

Type 7 · SaaS bought through cloud marketplaces

Definition (say it in an interview): Marketplace SaaS is third-party SaaS listed and purchased through a cloud provider’s marketplace — AWS Marketplace, Microsoft Marketplace (Azure), Google Cloud Marketplace — using the customer’s cloud account for subscription and billing, often with private offers and eligibility to count towards cloud spending commitments.

In plain words (no jargon): Buying online software through your cloud provider’s shop, so it is paid on the same cloud bill.

Analogy: Buying from a mall instead of individual shops: one entry gate, one membership card, one bill — many brands inside.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Discover. Search the marketplace by category.
  2. Evaluate. Free trials, pricing plans, reviews.
  3. Subscribe. Accept terms with the cloud account; SSO link to the vendor.
  4. Bill. Usage/contract charges on the cloud invoice.
  5. Govern. Private marketplace / approved products list.

Words used above, in simple terms: marketplace = an online store of third-party software · private offer = a negotiated price for one customer · procurement = the company process of buying

6 Explained part by part

Six things every SaaS admin manages

Subscription / licence

What it is: What you pay for: seats, usage or tiers.

Why it exists: Drives cost and features.

Where it is used: Per user per month, per transaction, annual contracts.

Single sign-on (SSO)

What it is: Log in with the company identity provider.

Why it exists: One place to enable MFA and remove leavers.

Where it is used: SAML/OIDC with Entra ID, Okta, Google, IAM Identity Center.

SCIM provisioning

What it is: Automatic creation/removal of SaaS accounts from the IdP.

Why it exists: No forgotten ex-employee accounts.

Where it is used: Entra ID/Okta → SaaS user sync.

Admin console

What it is: Where you set policies, roles and sharing.

Why it exists: Most SaaS risk is misconfiguration.

Where it is used: Microsoft 365 admin, Google Admin, Zoho admin.

Data export / API

What it is: How to get your data out.

Why it exists: Your exit plan and backups.

Where it is used: CSV/JSON export, APIs, backup tools.

SLA and status page

What it is: The vendor’s uptime promise and incident page.

Why it exists: Know what is promised and where to check.

Where it is used: status.* pages, SLA documents (Lesson 5.4).

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: The vendor is responsible for everything.
    Reality: Identity, access and data stay yours.
    Proof: Responsibility figure.
  • Myth: SaaS data is automatically backed up for me.
    Reality: Service backups protect the vendor’s service; plan your own retention/backup.
    Proof: Mistakes.
  • Myth: SaaS cannot be customised.
    Reality: It is configured, extended via APIs and low-code — not changed at code level.
    Proof: Types 5–6.
7 Deep dive

NIST definition, SaaS characteristics, vendor evaluation checklist

NIST definition of SaaS

NIST SP 800-145: “The capability provided to the consumer is to use the provider’s applications running on a cloud infrastructure. The applications are accessible from various client devices through either a thin client interface, such as a web browser, or a program interface. The consumer does not manage or control the underlying cloud infrastructure … with the possible exception of limited user-specific application configuration settings.”

SaaS characteristics

  • Accessed over the internet from any device; nothing to install.
  • Subscription or usage pricing (OpEx).
  • Vendor-managed updates — everyone on the latest version.
  • Multi-tenant economics.
  • Configurable, not customisable at code level.

9 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

These labs run on your own cloud account — we cannot run them for you. Each step has three tabs (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud); pick the cloud you have and do both the UI way and the CLI way. Stay inside free tier / credit limits, use the India Region (AWS ap-south-1 Mumbai · Azure Central India · Google asia-south1 Mumbai), and always do the Clean up step at the end. Nothing here costs money unless you choose to subscribe to a paid SaaS product.

  1. 0 · Get ready: account, budget, CLI

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Create an AWS account (aws.amazon.com → Create an AWS Account); choose the Free plan while learning.
    2. Never work as root: IAM Identity Center (or IAM) → create an admin user with MFA and sign in as that user.
    3. Billing and Cost Management → Budgets → Create budget → monthly cost budget, e.g. $5, alert by e-mail (Lesson 5.5).
    4. Top-right Region menu → Asia Pacific (Mumbai). Open CloudShell (terminal icon) — the AWS CLI is already signed in there.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws sts get-caller-identity          # who am I? (must NOT be the root user)
aws configure set region ap-south-1
aws configure list

    You should see: your account ID and an IAM user/role ARN — not “root”.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Create a free account at azure.microsoft.com/free (credit for the first 30 days).
    2. Search Cost Management → Budgets → Add → monthly budget, e.g. ₹400, alert at 80%.
    3. Open Cloud Shell (the >_ icon in the top bar) → choose Bash. The Azure CLI is already signed in.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az account show --output table        # subscription in use
az account list-locations --query "[?name=='centralindia'].displayName" -o tsv

    You should see: your subscription name and state “Enabled”, then “Central India”.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Sign in at console.cloud.google.com and start the free trial (credit for 90 days).
    2. Create a project: project picker → New project → name tiffingo-lab.
    3. Billing → Budgets & alerts → Create budget → e.g. ₹400, alerts at 50/90/100%.
    4. Click Activate Cloud Shell (>_ icon). The gcloud CLI is already signed in.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    gcloud config set project tiffingo-lab      # use your real project ID
gcloud config set compute/region asia-south1
gcloud config list

    You should see: your account e-mail, the project and region asia-south1.

    Cloud Shell is the easiest CLI: free, in the browser, already logged in. On your laptop install the AWS CLI v2, Azure CLI or Google Cloud CLI and run aws configure / az login / gcloud init.

  2. 1 · Find a SaaS product in your cloud marketplace

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Console search → AWS Marketplace → Discover products → search helpdesk, filter delivery method SaaS and pricing Free trial.
    2. Open a listing: read Pricing, Usage information, Support and the vendor’s data-location statement.
    3. Do not subscribe unless you want to; Manage subscriptions shows what your account already uses.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    # The catalogue is browsed in the UI; the CLI shows what you already subscribed to (billing by entity):
aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=$(date -d '-30 days' +%F),End=$(date +%F) --granularity MONTHLY \
  --metrics UnblendedCost --filter '{"Dimensions":{"Key":"BILLING_ENTITY","Values":["AWS Marketplace"]}}'

    You should see: listings with SaaS delivery and trial options; the CLI shows $0 Marketplace spend on a new account.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Portal search → Marketplace → search helpdesk → product type SaaS.
    2. Open a listing: Plans + Pricing, Overview, publisher information.
    3. Cost Management later shows SaaS charges under publisher type Marketplace.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    # Browsing is UI-only; list Marketplace charges on your subscription:
az consumption marketplace list --top 10 --output table

    You should see: SaaS listings with plans; the CLI shows no Marketplace usage on a new subscription.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Marketplace → filter type SaaS → search helpdesk.
    2. Open a product: pricing, Overview, Support. Your orders shows subscriptions.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    # Google Cloud Marketplace browsing has no gcloud command; check which APIs/products are enabled in the project:
gcloud services list --enabled --format="value(config.title)" | head

    You should see: SaaS listings; your enabled Google services in the project.

    Compare a marketplace listing with buying directly — same product, different contract and billing path.

  3. 2 · Connect a SaaS app to single sign-on

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. IAM Identity Center → Enable (if not yet) → Applications → Add application → I want to select an application from the catalog.
    2. Search a SaaS app (e.g. Zoom, Slack, Salesforce) → note the SAML settings it needs → Submit → Assign users and groups.
    3. Users then see the app in the AWS access portal; disabling a user in Identity Center removes access to all assigned apps.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    INST=$(aws sso-admin list-instances --query 'Instances[0].InstanceArn' --output text)
aws sso-admin list-application-providers --query 'ApplicationProviders[].DisplayData.DisplayName' --output text | tr '\t' '\n' | head -20
aws sso-admin list-applications --instance-arn $INST --query 'Applications[].Name'

    You should see: catalog application names (e.g. Salesforce, Slack, Zoom) and the applications already configured in your instance.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Microsoft Entra ID → Enterprise applications → New application → search the gallery (e.g. Zoom) → Create.
    2. Open it → Single sign-on → SAML (shows the values to paste in the SaaS admin) → Users and groups → Add user/group.
    3. Provisioning (for apps that support SCIM) creates and removes SaaS accounts automatically.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az rest --method get --url "https://graph.microsoft.com/v1.0/applicationTemplates?\$filter=displayName eq 'Zoom'" --query "value[].{id:id, name:displayName}"
# create an instance of the gallery app (use the id from above):
az rest --method post --url "https://graph.microsoft.com/v1.0/applicationTemplates/TEMPLATE_ID/instantiate" --body '{"displayName":"Zoom (TiffinGo)"}'
az ad sp list --display-name "Zoom (TiffinGo)" --query "[].{name:displayName, appId:appId}" --output table

    You should see: the gallery template, then a new enterprise application and service principal “Zoom (TiffinGo)”. The free Entra ID tier supports SSO for gallery apps.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. SSO to SaaS is configured in the Google Admin console (Cloud Identity Free or Workspace): Apps → Web and mobile apps → Add app → Search for apps.
    2. Pick a SAML app (e.g. Zoom), copy the IdP details into the SaaS admin, then turn the service ON for a group.
    3. Disabling the user in Admin console blocks every SAML app at once.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    # SAML app setup has no gcloud command (it lives in the Admin console / Admin SDK).
# Check which Google identity you are using and its organisation:
gcloud config get-value account
gcloud organizations list

    You should see: your account and, if you use Cloud Identity/Workspace, your organisation ID.

    SSO is the single most important SaaS control: one place for MFA, one switch for leavers.

  4. 3 · See your SaaS spend in one place

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Billing and Cost Management → Cost Explorer → filter Billing entity = AWS Marketplace, group by service.
    2. Budgets → create a budget filtered to Marketplace spend.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws ce get-cost-and-usage --time-period Start=$(date -d '-90 days' +%F),End=$(date +%F) --granularity MONTHLY \
  --metrics UnblendedCost --group-by Type=DIMENSION,Key=BILLING_ENTITY --output table

    You should see: costs split into “AWS” and “AWS Marketplace” per month.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Cost Management → Cost analysis → add filter Publisher type = Marketplace → group by service name.
    2. Microsoft 365/Dynamics licences appear in the Microsoft 365 admin center billing instead.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az consumption marketplace list --top 20 --query "[].{product:offerName, cost:pretaxCost, date:usageStart}" --output table

    You should see: Marketplace line items (empty on a new subscription).

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Billing → Reports → filter services to Marketplace products, group by service.
    2. Workspace subscriptions are billed in the Google Admin console billing section.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    # Detailed SaaS cost queries use the billing export to BigQuery; list your billing accounts:
gcloud billing accounts list

    You should see: your billing account(s); Marketplace products appear as services in Reports.

    Finance’s question “what SaaS do we pay for?” is easier to answer when purchases go through one channel.

10 Mistakes to avoid

Common mistakes, why they happen, how to fix them

Mistake or symptomWhy it happensHow to fix or avoid
Believing the vendor handles all security.Misreading SaaS shared responsibility.Own identity, MFA, sharing settings and data.
Separate passwords for every SaaS app.Quick sign-ups.SSO + SCIM through one identity provider.
No exit plan.Easy to start, hard to leave.Check export formats and contract terms before buying.
Assuming the vendor backs up your data for you.Service backups ≠ your restore needs.Use retention features or third-party backup for critical SaaS data.
Shadow IT: teams buying SaaS on personal cards.Slow procurement.Approved catalogue, marketplace, SaaS management.
Leaked API keys for API-first SaaS.Keys in code.Secrets manager, restricted keys, rotation.

12 Mini project

Build something with what you learned

Mini project: TiffinGo’s SaaS inventory and access review

Goal: Take control of the SaaS your company already uses.

Combines: This lesson + 2.1–2.2 + 5.4 (SLAs).
