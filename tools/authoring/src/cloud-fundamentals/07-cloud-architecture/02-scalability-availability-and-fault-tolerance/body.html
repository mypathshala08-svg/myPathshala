1 The story

One data centre goes dark

Before you start: 5.3 Scalability and 5.4 SLAs.

🤔 At 9:12 p.m. one Mumbai data centre loses cooling and every server in it shuts down. TiffinGo’s customers keep ordering without noticing. A competitor is down for three hours. What did TiffinGo build that the competitor did not?

Three different things people mix up: high availability (stay up through a failure inside a Region, usually by running in several AZs), fault tolerance (no visible interruption at all), and disaster recovery (come back after a whole Region or the data is lost — with a chosen RTO and RPO). Scalability (Lesson 5.3) keeps you fast under load; these keep you alive under failure. This lesson explains redundancy, multi-AZ design, the four classic DR strategies and fault isolation — then on your own account you build a group of servers across zones, kill one and watch it heal, and copy a backup to another Region.

2 Definition

Scalability, availability and fault tolerance

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Scalability is a system’s ability to handle increased load by adding resources; availability is the proportion of time it is operational; high availability designs minimise downtime through redundancy and automatic failover across failure domains; fault tolerance is the ability to continue operating without interruption when components fail; disaster recovery restores service after a large-scale event within a defined recovery time objective (RTO) and recovery point objective (RPO).

In simple words

Scalability = handle more users. High availability = keep running when a part breaks. Fault tolerance = users notice nothing at all. Disaster recovery = come back after a big disaster.

Say it in an interview

30-second answer you can speak:

  • “HA keeps a service up through component or zone failures — typically multi-AZ with load balancing, auto scaling and Multi-AZ databases. Fault tolerance means zero interruption. DR is about recovering from a Region-level or data-loss event within an RTO and RPO.”
  • “DR strategies go from backup & restore (cheapest, hours) through pilot light and warm standby to multi-site active/active (near zero, most expensive).”

Type 1 · Redundancy (no single point of failure, N+1)

Definition (say it in an interview): Redundancy means duplicating critical components — servers, disks, network paths, power, whole zones — so that the failure of any one does not stop the service; N+1 (or N+2) sizing keeps at least one spare beyond the capacity the peak load needs.

In plain words (no jargon): Keeping a spare of everything important, so that when one part breaks, the spare quietly takes over.

Analogy: A car with a spare tyre — or better, a bus with six wheels at the back: one puncture and it still drives.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Find SPOFs. Walk the request path: DNS, LB, app, cache, DB, network links.
  2. Duplicate. Two or more of each, in different failure domains.
  3. Size N+1. Peak capacity plus spare (per AZ lost).
  4. Detect. Health checks per component.
  5. Fail over automatically. LB routing, DB promotion, DNS failover.

Words used above, in simple terms: SPOF = single point of failure · N+1 = needed amount plus one spare · failure domain = things that fail together, e.g. one AZ · failover = switching to the spare

Cloud data centres themselves are built N+1 or 2N for power and cooling — but a whole facility can still fail (fire, cooling, fibre cuts), which is why providers expose AZs and ask you to add your own redundancy across them.

Real scenario (TiffinGo)

TiffinGo needs 4 servers at Diwali peak; it runs 6 across three AZs (2 per AZ). Losing a whole AZ leaves 4 — exactly the peak need.

Products and cloud services

Load balancers with multiple targets, RAID/replicated storage (EBS, managed disks), Multi-AZ databases, redundant Direct Connect/ExpressRoute links, dual VPN tunnels.

For the experienced — did you know? Redundant components that share a hidden dependency are not redundant: two servers in different AZs that both read one config file from a single-AZ NFS server still have a SPOF. Draw dependencies, not just boxes.

Use it when: any component whose failure would stop customers — find SPOFs on your architecture diagram first.
Avoid / be careful when: you duplicate without health checks and automatic failover — a spare nobody switches to is useless.

Type 2 · Multi-AZ (active-active within a Region)

Definition (say it in an interview): A multi-AZ architecture runs a workload’s components in two or more Availability Zones of one Region at the same time — stateless tiers active in every zone behind a load balancer, data tiers with synchronous replication to a standby or zone-redundant service — so the loss of a zone causes at most a brief disruption.

In plain words (no jargon): Running copies of your app in two or three separate data-centre groups of the same city, so if one has a problem, the others keep serving.

Analogy: A restaurant chain with three kitchens in the same city: if one kitchen’s gas fails, orders are cooked in the other two, still hot and on time.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Subnets per AZ. Public and private subnets in each zone (Lesson 6.3).
  2. Stateless tier. Instances in every AZ; LB health checks; ASG keeps counts.
  3. Stateful tier. Synchronous replication or zone-redundant managed service.
  4. Static stability. Each AZ sized so the rest carry the load without new launches.
  5. Failover. Automatic: LB stops routing, DB promotes standby.

Words used above, in simple terms: synchronous replication = a write is confirmed only after both copies have it · stateless = keeps no user data locally, any copy can serve any request · zone-redundant = automatically spread across zones by the provider

Use it when: almost every production workload — it is the default high-availability design in the cloud and needed for the 99.99% EC2 SLA (Lesson 5.4).
Avoid / be careful when: you think multi-AZ protects against Region-wide problems, bad deployments or deleted data — it does not.

Type 3 · Backup and restore (DR)

Definition (say it in an interview): Backup and restore is the simplest disaster-recovery strategy: data is backed up regularly and copied to another Region (and ideally another account), infrastructure is defined as code, and after a disaster the environment is rebuilt and the data restored — the lowest cost, with the longest RTO (hours to days) and an RPO equal to the backup interval.

In plain words (no jargon): Keep copies of your data in another city, and if disaster strikes, rebuild everything there from the copies. Cheap but slow.

Analogy: Keeping photocopies of all your documents at your parents’ house in another city: cheap, but after a fire you still need days to rebuild your files.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Back up. Snapshots, DB backups and logs on a schedule.
  2. Copy away. To another Region and account; make them immutable.
  3. Codify. All infrastructure in templates (Lesson 6.4).
  4. Restore. Deploy templates in the DR Region, restore data, switch DNS.
  5. Test. Restore drills to measure the real RTO.

Words used above, in simple terms: RTO = recovery time objective — how long you may be down · RPO = recovery point objective — how much recent data you may lose · cross-Region copy = a backup duplicated to another Region · immutable = cannot be changed or deleted for a period

Use it when: RTO of hours and RPO of hours are acceptable, or as the baseline for every workload (every system needs backups anyway).
Avoid / be careful when: customers cannot wait hours — and never without regular restore tests.

Type 4 · Pilot light (DR)

Definition (say it in an interview): Pilot light keeps the core of a workload — usually the data tier — continuously replicated and running in the recovery Region, while application servers and other components exist only as images and templates (switched off or at zero); during a disaster they are started and scaled up, giving an RTO of tens of minutes and an RPO of minutes or less.

In plain words (no jargon): Keep only the most important part (the data) live in another city; everything else is ready to switch on when needed.

Analogy: The small flame in a gas geyser: almost nothing burns all day, but turning the knob brings full heat in seconds.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Replicate data. Asynchronous cross-Region replica of the database and objects.
  2. Prepare compute. Images, templates and scaling groups at size 0 in DR.
  3. Detect. Decide (often a human) to fail over.
  4. Ignite. Promote replica, scale up servers, update config.
  5. Switch traffic. DNS failover / global LB.

Words used above, in simple terms: read replica = a continuously updated read-only copy · promote = turn a replica into a writable primary · asynchronous = copies shortly after the write, not during it

Use it when: the data must be nearly current (low RPO) but tens of minutes of downtime are acceptable.
Avoid / be careful when: nobody has practised the switch-on — the first attempt during a real disaster usually fails.

Type 5 · Warm standby (DR)

Definition (say it in an interview): Warm standby runs a scaled-down but fully functional copy of the whole workload in the recovery Region at all times, with data continuously replicated; during a disaster the copy is promoted and scaled out to full production capacity, giving an RTO of minutes.

In plain words (no jargon): A small but complete working copy of your app runs in another city all the time; in a disaster it is quickly made bigger and takes over.

Analogy: A second cricket team that plays every practice match: smaller squad, but fully trained — on match day you just add players.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Run small. All tiers deployed in DR at reduced size.
  2. Replicate. Data asynchronously; config via the same IaC.
  3. Verify. Synthetic or internal traffic proves it works.
  4. Fail over. Promote data, scale out, switch DNS/LB.
  5. Fail back. Re-sync and return when the primary is healthy.

Words used above, in simple terms: standby = a copy waiting to take over · fail back = returning to the original site after recovery · synthetic traffic = test requests that imitate users

Use it when: customer-facing systems that need recovery in minutes but cannot justify a full second Region.
Avoid / be careful when: the standby is never tested with real traffic — configuration drift makes it fail when needed.

Type 6 · Multi-site active/active (DR)

Definition (say it in an interview): Multi-site active/active runs full production capacity in two or more Regions simultaneously, each serving live traffic, with data replicated between them; when one Region fails, traffic is routed to the others with near-zero downtime — the fastest and most expensive strategy, requiring the application to handle multi-Region data consistency.

In plain words (no jargon): Two complete copies of the app in two cities, both serving customers all the time. If one city has a problem, the other already handles everyone.

Analogy: Two branches of a bank in different cities, both open and serving customers; if one closes for a flood, customers simply go to the other.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Full deploy. Identical stacks in each Region from the same IaC.
  2. Route users. Latency-based DNS or global load balancer.
  3. Replicate data. Multi-Region databases; decide conflict resolution.
  4. Health-based routing. Unhealthy Region removed automatically.
  5. Capacity. Each Region can absorb the other’s load.

Words used above, in simple terms: global table = a database table replicated and writable in several Regions · conflict resolution = deciding which of two simultaneous updates wins · latency routing = sending users to the fastest Region

Use it when: downtime is extremely expensive or regulated, and the business can fund double capacity and engineering effort.
Avoid / be careful when: the data model cannot tolerate concurrent writes in two places — conflicting updates (two discounts applied to one order) need careful design.

Type 7 · Fault isolation and graceful degradation

Definition (say it in an interview): Fault isolation limits the blast radius of a failure by partitioning a system into independent units — bulkheads, cells, shards, separate accounts and Regions — so that a fault, bad deployment or overload in one unit does not spread; graceful degradation keeps core functions running by switching off or simplifying non-essential features when dependencies fail.

In plain words (no jargon): Splitting the system into separate compartments so a problem in one part does not sink the whole app — and turning off nice-to-have features to keep the important ones working.

Analogy: A ship divided into watertight compartments: a hole floods one compartment, not the whole ship — and the captain switches off the lights in the dining hall to keep the engines running.

How it works internally, step by step

  1. Partition. Cells/shards by customer, city or tenant; separate accounts.
  2. Independent stacks. Each cell has its own compute and data.
  3. Route. A thin routing layer maps users to cells.
  4. Stage changes. Deploy to one cell, watch, then continue.
  5. Degrade. Timeouts, fallbacks and feature flags for non-critical dependencies.

Words used above, in simple terms: blast radius = how many users a failure affects · bulkhead = a wall that stops a failure spreading · cell = a complete independent copy serving a subset of users · feature flag = a switch to turn a feature on or off without deploying

Use it when: one failure or one customer could otherwise affect everyone — multi-tenant SaaS, large platforms, risky deployments.
Avoid / be careful when: you add so many partitions that operating them becomes the main risk — start with accounts/Regions and staged deployments.

6 Explained part by part

Six words for every resilience discussion

RTO

What it is: Maximum acceptable downtime after a disaster.

Why it exists: Decides the DR strategy and its cost.

Where it is used: “Order API: 15 minutes.”

RPO

What it is: Maximum acceptable data loss measured in time.

Why it exists: Decides backup frequency or replication.

Where it is used: “Orders: 0; reports: 24 hours.”

Health check

What it is: Automated probe of a component.

Why it exists: Triggers failover and replacement.

Where it is used: LB target checks, Route 53 checks.

Failover

What it is: Switching to a standby.

Why it exists: Can be automatic (Multi-AZ DB) or manual (Region DR).

Where it is used: RDS Multi-AZ, Route 53 failover, Front Door.

Static stability

What it is: Works without changing anything during a failure.

Why it exists: Control planes may be impaired in big events.

Where it is used: Pre-provisioned N+1 capacity per AZ.

Game day / DR drill

What it is: Planned practice of failure.

Why it exists: Finds the gaps before real disasters.

Where it is used: Fault Injection Service, Chaos Studio, scripted drills.

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: Multi-AZ is disaster recovery.
    Reality: It is high availability inside one Region.
    Proof: Type 2.
  • Myth: More replicas protect against bad deployments.
    Reality: Replicas copy the bug; isolate and stage changes.
    Proof: Type 7.
  • Myth: The cloud provider handles DR for us.
    Reality: Resilience in the cloud is your responsibility.
    Proof: Deep dive.
7 Deep dive

Availability maths, choosing RTO/RPO, shared responsibility

Availability maths for redundancy

If one server is up 99% of the time and failures are independent, two in parallel give 1 − 0.01² = 99.99%; three give 99.9999%. Real failures are correlated (same AZ, same bug, same deployment), so spreading across failure domains matters more than the count (Lesson 5.4).

Shared responsibility for resilience

The provider is responsible for the resilience of the cloud (AZs, Regions, services meeting their SLAs); you are responsible for resilience in the cloud — using multiple AZs, backups, DR plans and testing. A single-AZ design is a customer choice, not a provider failure.

9 Hands-on

Do it yourself, step by step

These labs run on your own cloud account — we cannot run them for you. Each step has three tabs (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud); pick the cloud you have and do both the UI way and the CLI way. Stay inside free tier / credit limits, use the India Region (AWS ap-south-1 Mumbai · Azure Central India · Google asia-south1 Mumbai), and always do the Clean up step at the end. Two tiny VMs run for about 15 minutes; snapshots of 1–10 GB cost paise — delete everything at the end.

  1. 0 · Get ready: account, budget, CLI

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Create an AWS account (aws.amazon.com → Create an AWS Account); choose the Free plan while learning.
    2. Never work as root: IAM Identity Center (or IAM) → create an admin user with MFA and sign in as that user.
    3. Billing and Cost Management → Budgets → Create budget → monthly cost budget, e.g. $5, alert by e-mail (Lesson 5.5).
    4. Top-right Region menu → Asia Pacific (Mumbai). Open CloudShell (terminal icon) — the AWS CLI is already signed in there.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws sts get-caller-identity          # who am I? (must NOT be the root user)
aws configure set region ap-south-1
aws configure list

    You should see: your account ID and an IAM user/role ARN — not “root”.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Create a free account at azure.microsoft.com/free (credit for the first 30 days).
    2. Search Cost Management → Budgets → Add → monthly budget, e.g. ₹400, alert at 80%.
    3. Open Cloud Shell (the >_ icon in the top bar) → choose Bash. The Azure CLI is already signed in.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az account show --output table        # subscription in use
az account list-locations --query "[?name=='centralindia'].displayName" -o tsv

    You should see: your subscription name and state “Enabled”, then “Central India”.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Sign in at console.cloud.google.com and start the free trial (credit for 90 days).
    2. Create a project: project picker → New project → name tiffingo-lab.
    3. Billing → Budgets & alerts → Create budget → e.g. ₹400, alerts at 50/90/100%.
    4. Click Activate Cloud Shell (>_ icon). The gcloud CLI is already signed in.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    gcloud config set project tiffingo-lab      # use your real project ID
gcloud config set compute/region asia-south1
gcloud config list

    You should see: your account e-mail, the project and region asia-south1.

    Cloud Shell is the easiest CLI: free, in the browser, already logged in. On your laptop install the AWS CLI v2, Azure CLI or Google Cloud CLI and run aws configure / az login / gcloud init.

  2. 1 · Run two servers across two zones

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. EC2 → Launch templates → Create launch template → name tiffingo-lt, Amazon Linux 2023, t3.micro → Create.
    2. Auto Scaling groups → Create Auto Scaling group → template tiffingo-lt → default VPC, pick subnets in ap-south-1a and ap-south-1b.
    3. Desired 2, min 2, max 4 → Create Auto Scaling group → Instance management: one instance per AZ.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    aws ec2 create-launch-template --launch-template-name tiffingo-lt --launch-template-data \
  '{"ImageId":"resolve:ssm:/aws/service/ami-amazon-linux-latest/al2023-ami-kernel-default-x86_64","InstanceType":"t3.micro"}'
SUBNETS=$(aws ec2 describe-subnets --filters Name=default-for-az,Values=true --query 'Subnets[0:2].SubnetId' --output text | tr '\t' ',')
aws autoscaling create-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name tiffingo-asg \
  --launch-template LaunchTemplateName=tiffingo-lt --min-size 2 --max-size 4 --desired-capacity 2 \
  --vpc-zone-identifier "$SUBNETS"
sleep 60
aws autoscaling describe-auto-scaling-groups --auto-scaling-group-names tiffingo-asg \
  --query 'AutoScalingGroups[0].Instances[].[InstanceId,AvailabilityZone,HealthStatus]' --output table

    You should see: two instances in two different AZs, both Healthy.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Virtual machine scale sets → Create → rg-tiffingo, name tiffingo-vmss, Central India, availability zones Zones 1, 2, orchestration Flexible.
    2. Image Ubuntu Server 24.04 LTS, size Standard_B1s, SSH key → Scaling: initial count 2 → Review + create → Create.
    3. Open it → Instances: one VM in zone 1 and one in zone 2.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az group create --name rg-tiffingo --location centralindia
az vmss create --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name tiffingo-vmss --orchestration-mode Flexible \
  --image Ubuntu2404 --vm-sku Standard_B1s --instance-count 2 --zones 1 2 \
  --admin-username azureuser --generate-ssh-keys
az vm list --resource-group rg-tiffingo --query "[].{name:name, zone:zones[0]}" --output table

    You should see: two VMs, zone 1 and zone 2. If B1s is not available in a zone, use another small size such as Standard_B2ats_v2.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Instance templates → Create instance template → tiffingo-tpl, e2-micro, Debian 12 → Create.
    2. Instance groups → Create instance group → New managed instance group (stateless) → template tiffingo-tpl, location Multiple zones asia-south1 (a, b), autoscaling off, 2 instances → Create.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    gcloud compute instance-templates create tiffingo-tpl --machine-type=e2-micro \
  --image-family=debian-12 --image-project=debian-cloud
gcloud compute instance-groups managed create tiffingo-mig --region=asia-south1 \
  --zones=asia-south1-a,asia-south1-b --template=tiffingo-tpl --size=2
gcloud compute instance-groups managed list-instances tiffingo-mig --region=asia-south1

    You should see: two instances, one in asia-south1-a and one in asia-south1-b, STATUS RUNNING.

    One group, many zones: the scaling service keeps the count and spreads instances for you.

  3. 2 · Kill a server and watch it heal

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Instances → select one tiffingo-asg instance → Instance state → Terminate instance.
    2. Back in the group → Activity tab: “Terminating…” then “Launching a new EC2 instance…” within a minute or two.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    VICTIM=$(aws autoscaling describe-auto-scaling-groups --auto-scaling-group-names tiffingo-asg --query 'AutoScalingGroups[0].Instances[0].InstanceId' --output text)
aws ec2 terminate-instances --instance-ids $VICTIM
sleep 120
aws autoscaling describe-scaling-activities --auto-scaling-group-name tiffingo-asg --max-items 3 \
  --query 'Activities[].[StartTime,Description]' --output table

    You should see: an activity “Launching a new EC2 instance … due to an instance taken out of service in response to an EC2 health check”.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Open one VM of the scale set → Delete. The scale set now has 1 VM — Azure keeps your new count.
    2. Scale set → Scaling → set instance count back to 2 → Save (or enable Automatic repairs with a health probe — repairs start after a 30–90 minute grace period).

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    VM=$(az vm list --resource-group rg-tiffingo --query "[0].name" --output tsv)
az vm delete --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name $VM --yes
az vmss show --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name tiffingo-vmss --query sku.capacity
az vmss scale --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name tiffingo-vmss --new-capacity 2
az vm list --resource-group rg-tiffingo --query "[].{name:name, zone:zones[0]}" --output table

    You should see: capacity 1 after the delete, then 2 again after scaling. Lesson: on Azure, self-healing needs automatic repairs + health monitoring; deleting is treated as your intent.

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. VM instances → select one tiffingo-mig instance → Delete.
    2. Open the instance group → the instance shows “Recreating” and a new one starts in the same zone.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    V=$(gcloud compute instance-groups managed list-instances tiffingo-mig --region=asia-south1 --format="value(instance.basename())" --limit=1)
Z=$(gcloud compute instance-groups managed list-instances tiffingo-mig --region=asia-south1 --format="value(instance.segment(-3))" --limit=1)
gcloud compute instances delete $V --zone=$Z --quiet
sleep 60
gcloud compute instance-groups managed list-instances tiffingo-mig --region=asia-south1

    You should see: the MIG recreates the instance (CURRENT_ACTION RECREATING → NONE) to keep the target size of 2.

    This is self-healing: the group compares desired with actual and fixes the difference — the core of high availability.

  4. 3 · Put a backup in another Region (DR)

    Pick your cloud.

    UI way — AWS Management Console

    1. Volumes → Create volume gp3 1 GiB in ap-south-1a → select → Create snapshot.
    2. Snapshots → select it → Actions → Copy snapshot → destination Asia Pacific (Hyderabad) → Copy snapshot (enable the Hyderabad Region first if it is not enabled).
    3. Switch the console to Hyderabad → Snapshots: the copy appears.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    VOL=$(aws ec2 create-volume --availability-zone ap-south-1a --size 1 --volume-type gp3 --query VolumeId --output text)
aws ec2 wait volume-available --volume-ids $VOL
SNAP=$(aws ec2 create-snapshot --volume-id $VOL --query SnapshotId --output text)
aws ec2 wait snapshot-completed --snapshot-ids $SNAP
DR=$(aws ec2 copy-snapshot --source-region ap-south-1 --source-snapshot-id $SNAP --region ap-south-2 --query SnapshotId --output text)
aws ec2 describe-snapshots --snapshot-ids $DR --region ap-south-2 --query 'Snapshots[0].[State,Progress]' --output text

    You should see: a snapshot ID in ap-south-2 with state pending → completed. ap-south-2 is an opt-in Region; enable it under [[Account]] → [[AWS Regions]] if needed.

    UI way — Azure portal

    1. Disks → Create → dr-disk, 4 GiB, Central India → Create; open it → Create snapshot → incremental → dr-src-snap.
    2. Snapshots → Create → Region South India, source = snapshot dr-src-snap, incremental → Create (a cross-region copy).

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    az disk create --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name dr-disk --size-gb 4 --location centralindia
az snapshot create --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name dr-src-snap --source dr-disk --incremental true
SRC=$(az snapshot show --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name dr-src-snap --query id --output tsv)
az snapshot create --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name dr-copy-southindia --location southindia \
  --source $SRC --incremental true --copy-start true
az snapshot show --resource-group rg-tiffingo --name dr-copy-southindia --query "[location,provisioningState]" --output tsv

    You should see: southindia Succeeded (the copy may take a few minutes; completionPercent shows progress).

    UI way — Google Cloud console

    1. Disks → Create disk → dr-disk, asia-south1-a, 10 GB → Create.
    2. Snapshots → Create snapshot → source dr-disk, location Regional → asia-south2 (Delhi) → Create.

    CLI way — the same thing with commands

    gcloud compute disks create dr-disk --size=10GB --zone=asia-south1-a
gcloud compute snapshots create dr-snap --source-disk=dr-disk --source-disk-zone=asia-south1-a --storage-location=asia-south2
gcloud compute snapshots describe dr-snap --format="value(status,storageLocations)"

    You should see: READY ['asia-south2'] — the backup lives in Delhi, outside the Mumbai Region. You can restore it into any Region.

    This is the first rung of the DR ladder (backup and restore). Next would be replicating the database and copying images — pilot light.

Clean up and cost check

AWS: aws autoscaling delete-auto-scaling-group --auto-scaling-group-name tiffingo-asg --force-delete; aws ec2 delete-launch-template --launch-template-name tiffingo-lt; aws ec2 delete-snapshot --snapshot-id $DR --region ap-south-2; aws ec2 delete-snapshot --snapshot-id $SNAP; aws ec2 delete-volume --volume-id $VOL. Console: delete the group (instances terminate), template, volume and both snapshots (switch Region for the copy).
Azure: az group delete --name rg-tiffingo --yes --no-wait (the South India snapshot is in the same resource group).
Google Cloud: gcloud compute instance-groups managed delete tiffingo-mig --region=asia-south1 --quiet; gcloud compute instance-templates delete tiffingo-tpl --quiet; gcloud compute snapshots delete dr-snap --quiet; gcloud compute disks delete dr-disk --zone=asia-south1-a --quiet.

10 Mistakes to avoid

Common mistakes, why they happen, how to fix them

Mistake or symptomWhy it happensHow to fix or avoid
Everything in one AZ.Default subnet, quick start.Spread stateless tiers across ≥2 AZs; Multi-AZ databases.
Thinking multi-AZ = disaster recovery.Both sound like “backup”.Multi-AZ is HA in one Region; DR needs another Region/account and backups.
No RTO/RPO agreed with the business.Nobody asked.Write RTO/RPO per system; pick the cheapest strategy that meets them.
Backups never restored.Restore tests feel optional.Quarterly restore drills; measure real RTO.
Each AZ sized for a third of peak.Cost saving.N+1 per AZ (static stability) for critical tiers.
High DNS TTLs on failover records.Defaults.Low TTLs or anycast global load balancing.
DR quotas and KMS keys missing in the other Region.Only primary Region configured.Mirror quotas, keys, parameters and images in DR; test.
