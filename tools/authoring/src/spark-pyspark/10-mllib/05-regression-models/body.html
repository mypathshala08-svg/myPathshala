1 The story

From “30–40 minutes” to a real ETA

Before you start: 10.2 Features.

🤔 “Your tiffin will arrive in 38 minutes.” How does an app predict a number, not a class?

With regression. On 60,000 TiffinGo deliveries, LinearRegression reached R² 0.820 (RMSE 4.37 min), RandomForestRegressor 0.850, and GBTRegressor 0.912 (RMSE 3.06). Why? Linear models cannot see that 1 pm and 8 pm are rush hours: they under-predicted those hours by about 5 minutes. For a rainy 9 km dinner order in Mumbai, linear said 46.9 min, GBT 55.6.

  • Tree regressors: RandomForest and GBT, feature importances.

TiffinGo shows every customer the same delivery window. The product team wants a per-order prediction in minutes, trained on 60,000 past deliveries.

K> Sir, the app shows a fixed “30–40 min”. Customers complain. Can we predict real minutes? A> Yes: regression. Label = minutes, features = km, hour, rain, city. Start with LinearRegression. R> And if it is not linear? Rain plus long distance is worse than both together. A> Then trees: RandomForest and GBT. We will compare all three on the same test set.
2 Definition

Regression in MLlib

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Regression is supervised learning with a continuous label. LinearRegression fits weights minimising squared error (with optional L1/L2 regularisation); RandomForestRegressor averages bootstrap-trained decision trees; GBTRegressor builds trees sequentially on the residuals of the current ensemble (gradient boosting). Models are compared with RMSE, MAE and R² on held-out data.

In simple words

The model learns how much each input (distance, rain, time) adds to the answer, and then predicts a number for a new order.

Why, how, where

Why

Many business questions are numbers: delivery minutes, demand tomorrow, price, revenue. Classification (10.2) predicts categories; regression predicts amounts.

the problem it solves

How

LinearRegression fits weights by least squares (optionally regularised with regParam/elasticNetParam). RandomForest averages many deep trees; GBT adds small trees one after another, each fixing the previous errors. Evaluate with RegressionEvaluator (rmse, mae, r2).

the mechanism

Where

ETA prediction, demand forecasting features, pricing, credit limits, energy load, any “how much / how long”.

in real systems

When not

Linear needs hand-made features for curves and interactions; trees cannot extrapolate beyond training ranges; GBT is slower to train (27.6 s vs 3.7 s here).

Dissect the linear model

LEVELS> Regression at three levels L1> The tiffin uncle’s rule
  • “10 minutes to pack, 2.5 minutes per km, add more if it rains.” That rule is a linear model. A trained eye that also knows “8 pm is chaos” is a tree model.
L2> The API (pyspark.ml.regression)
  • LinearRegression(labelCol, regParam, elasticNetParam) → model.coefficients, intercept, summary.r2, summary.rootMeanSquaredError.
  • RandomForestRegressor(numTrees, maxDepth), GBTRegressor(maxIter, maxDepth, stepSize), also DecisionTreeRegressor, GeneralizedLinearRegression, IsotonicRegression, FMRegressor.
  • RegressionEvaluator(metricName="rmse" | "mae" | "r2"); featureImportances for tree models.
L3> Practical details
  • Encode categories with StringIndexer + OneHotEncoder; one category is dropped (baseline), so coefficients read “compared with Delhi”.
  • Group errors by segment (hour, city) to find what the model misses: our linear model was −5.5 min at 13:00.
  • For linear models, add features (hour buckets, km × rain) or switch to trees; tune with CrossValidator (10.3).
/LEVELS

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: High R² on train is enough.
    Reality (Spark 4.3): Compare on test.
    Proof: Step 2.
  • Myth: Linear handles any pattern.
    Reality (Spark 4.3): Rush hours missed by ~5 min.
    Proof: Step 3.
  • Myth: The most accurate model is always best.
    Reality (Spark 4.3): GBT took 7× longer; explainability matters.
    Proof: Step 2.
EXAM> “Regression algorithms in Spark ML?” LinearRegression (regParam, elasticNetParam), GeneralizedLinearRegression, DecisionTree/RandomForest/GBTRegressor, IsotonicRegression, FMRegressor; metrics rmse, mae, r2 via RegressionEvaluator; featureImportances for trees; one-hot baseline.

4 Step by step

Predicting delivery minutes

All outputs are real: PySpark 4.3.0 MLlib, local[2], 60,000 synthetic TiffinGo deliveries generated with a fixed seed.

  1. Step 1 · LinearRegression

    prep = [StringIndexer(inputCol="city", outputCol="ci"), OneHotEncoder(inputCols=["ci"], outputCols=["cv"]),
        VectorAssembler(inputCols=["km", "hour", "rain", "cv"], outputCol="features")]
lr = Pipeline(stages=prep + [LinearRegression(labelCol="mins")]).fit(train)
    == 1) LinearRegression: delivery minutes from km, hour, rain, city feature order: km, hour, rain, city=Pune, city=Mumbai coefficients: [2.62, 0.13, 7.7, -0.02, 4.05] intercept: 9.09 train R2: 0.825 train RMSE: 4.35
    Coefficients match the hidden rule closely: about 2.6 min per km, ~8 for rain, +4 in Mumbai.

  2. Step 2 · Three models, one test set

    for est in [LinearRegression(...), RandomForestRegressor(numTrees=40, maxDepth=8), GBTRegressor(maxIter=40, maxDepth=5)]:
    p = Pipeline(stages=prep + [est]).fit(train).transform(test)
    RegressionEvaluator(labelCol="mins", metricName="rmse").evaluate(p)
    == 2) three regressors on the same test set LinearRegression RMSE 4.37 MAE 3.50 R2 0.820 3.7 s RandomForestRegressor RMSE 3.98 MAE 3.19 R2 0.850 9.1 s GBTRegressor RMSE 3.06 MAE 2.44 R2 0.912 27.6 s
    GBT wins (RMSE 3.06, R² 0.912) but takes 7× longer to train than linear.

  3. Step 3 · Where linear fails

    lr.transform(test).withColumn("err", prediction - mins).groupBy("hour").agg(avg("err"))
    == 3) why trees win: hour is not linear (peak at lunch and dinner) +----+------------+ |hour|avg_error_lr| +----+------------+ | 12| 0.9| | 13| -5.5| | 17| 2.1| | 20| -4.5| +----+------------+
    At 13:00 and 20:00 linear under-predicts by ~5 min: one slope for “hour” cannot model two peaks.

  4. Step 4 · Feature importances

    gm.stages[-1].featureImportances
    == 4) feature importances (GBT) km 0.420 hour 0.507 rain 0.052 city=Pune 0.000 city=Mumbai 0.020
    Hour (0.507) and km (0.420) drive the trees; city adds little once rush hours are known.

  5. Step 5 · Predict one order

    new = spark.createDataFrame([(9.0, 20, 1, "Mumbai", 0.0)], df.schema)
lr.transform(new); gbt.transform(new)
    == 5) a new order: 9 km, 20:00, raining, Mumbai linear : 46.9 min GBT : 55.6 min
    True rule gives ≈ 54.5 min: GBT 55.6 is close; linear 46.9 would anger a hungry customer.
