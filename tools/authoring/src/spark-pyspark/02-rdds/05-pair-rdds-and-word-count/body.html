1 The story

“What are customers complaining about?”

Before you start: Lesson 2.4 (shuffles and reduceByKey vs groupByKey).

TiffinGo’s operations head has three months of customer reviews, 24,000 lines of free text, and one question: what do people complain about most? She also wants the average order value per city and the revenue per membership plan, for a meeting tomorrow.

R> Sir, reviews are just text. How do we count words on many machines? A> With the most famous program in big data: the word count. Split lines into words, turn each word into a pair (word, 1), and let reduceByKey add up the ones per word. Once data is in (key, value) form, a whole family of operations opens up: averages per key, joins, sorting by key. K> Is that not what DataFrames do with groupBy? A> Yes, and we will check that both give the same answer. Pair RDDs are how Spark did it first, how interviewers ask it, and still the simplest tool for free text. All outputs today are real (PySpark 4.3.0).
2 Definition

Pair RDDs and the word count

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A pair RDD is an RDD whose elements are two-element tuples (key, value). PySpark provides key-based operations on such RDDs: aggregations (reduceByKey, aggregateByKey, combineByKey, groupByKey, countByKey), value transformations that preserve partitioning (mapValues, flatMapValues), ordering (sortByKey) and relational operations (join, the outer joins, cogroup, subtractByKey). Most require a shuffle that brings equal keys to the same partition. The word count is the canonical example: flatMap lines into words, map each word to (word, 1), and reduceByKey(add) to sum the counts per word, with map-side combining before the shuffle.

In simple words

A pair RDD is an RDD of (key, value) pairs. Spark can then add up, average, sort or join by key. Word count: split text into words, make (word, 1) pairs, and add the 1s for each word.

LEVELS> Pair RDDs at three levels L1> Labelled boxes
  • A pair is a box with a label (the key) and something inside (the value): ("Mumbai", 530).
  • Spark can then bring together all boxes with the same label, on whichever machine they are.
  • Word count: label every word, put a 1 inside, add the 1s per label.
L2> The pair RDD toolkit
  • Make pairs with map(lambda x: (key, value)); inspect with keys(), values(), mapValues().
  • Aggregate per key: reduceByKey, aggregateByKey, combineByKey, countByKey (action), groupByKey (only when you need all values).
  • Order and combine: sortByKey, join, leftOuterJoin, rightOuterJoin, fullOuterJoin, cogroup.
L3> What the lab showed
  • The word count found 35 distinct words; “masala” led because it appears in two dishes (masala dosa, paneer butter masala); the complaint words were each about 2,000.
  • The DataFrame version (split + explode + groupBy) gave exactly the same top words.
  • aggregateByKey gave average order values per city in one pass; a pair-RDD join of 50,001 orders with 2,000 customers gave revenue per plan.
/LEVELS EXAM> “Write a word count in PySpark.” sc.textFile(path).flatMap(lambda l: l.split()).map(lambda w: (w, 1)).reduceByKey(lambda a, b: a + b), then takeOrdered or saveAsTextFile. Mention that reduceByKey combines counts per partition before the shuffle, and that the DataFrame equivalent is split + explode + groupBy().count().

4 Step by step

Word count, then by-key analytics on orders

All outputs are real: PySpark 4.3.0, local mode. Data: three months of TiffinGo reviews (24,000 lines such as thepla was too spicy and oily), orders.csv (50,002 rows) and customers.csv (2,000 customers with plans).

  1. Step 1 · The classic word count

    from operator import add
reviews = sc.textFile("reviews")
stop = {"was", "and", "for", "it", "too"}
counts = (reviews.flatMap(lambda line: line.lower().split())
                 .filter(lambda w: w not in stop)
                 .map(lambda w: (w, 1))
                 .reduceByKey(add))
print("distinct words:", counts.count())
print("top 8:", counts.takeOrdered(8, key=lambda kv: -kv[1]))
    distinct words: 35 top 8: [('masala', 4924), ('loved', 4905), ('tasty', 4839), ('quick', 4829), ('delivery', 4829), ('hot', 4811), ('great', 4786), ('packing', 4786)]
    Four transformations and an action: lines → words → (word, 1) → sums per word → top 8. Only 35 distinct words remain because the reviews use a small vocabulary. takeOrdered with a negative key returns the largest counts without sorting the whole RDD. Without the stop-word filter, “and” and “was” (24,000 each, Lesson 2.3) would lead.

  2. Step 2 · Find the complaints

    complaints = counts.filter(lambda kv: kv[0] in {"cold", "late", "oily", "spilled"}).sortByKey().collect()
    complaint words (sortByKey): [('cold', 2027), ('late', 1994), ('oily', 2071), ('spilled', 1996)]
    The answer for the operations head: about 2,000 mentions each of cold, late, oily, spilled; no single complaint dominates. sortByKey() returned them alphabetically. A real analysis would look at complaints per dish: make the key (dish, word) instead of word, a one-line change.

  3. Step 3 · The same with DataFrames

    from pyspark.sql import functions as F
df = spark.read.text("reviews")
top_df = (df.select(F.explode(F.split(F.lower("value"), " ")).alias("w"))
            .where(~F.col("w").isin(*stop)).groupBy("w").count()
            .orderBy(F.desc("count")).limit(3).collect())
    top 3: [('masala', 4924), ('loved', 4905), ('tasty', 4839)]
    Identical top words and counts. split makes an array per line, explode turns it into rows (the DataFrame flatMap), and groupBy().count() does what reduceByKey(add) did, with partial aggregation added by Catalyst. For structured output that feeds other tables, the DataFrame version is usually preferred (Module 4).

  4. Step 4 · Pairs from CSV lines, countByKey

    rows = (sc.textFile("orders.csv").filter(lambda l: not l.startswith("order_id"))
          .map(lambda l: l.split(",")).filter(lambda f: f[5].isdigit()))
city_amt = rows.map(lambda f: (f[3], int(f[5])))          # (city, amount)
print(city_amt.take(2))
print(sorted(city_amt.countByKey().items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])[:3])
    first pairs : [('Mumbai', 530), ('Mumbai', 500)] countByKey : [('Mumbai', 10015), ('Bengaluru', 9298), ('Pune', 7930)]
    We parsed the CSV by hand, skipped the header and dropped the row whose amount is abc (isdigit()), leaving 50,001 orders. countByKey is an action: it returns a Python dict to the driver, fine for 8 cities, dangerous for millions of keys (use reduceByKey instead).

  5. Step 5 · Average per key with aggregateByKey

    sum_cnt = city_amt.aggregateByKey((0, 0),
            lambda a, v: (a[0] + v, a[1] + 1),             # within a partition
            lambda a, b: (a[0] + b[0], a[1] + b[1]))       # across partitions
avg = sum_cnt.mapValues(lambda s: round(s[0] / s[1], 2)).sortByKey()
print(avg.take(3))
    aggregateByKey avg: [('Bengaluru', 292.97), ('Chennai', 298.16), ('Delhi', 295.0)]
    An average cannot be computed by reducing averages, so we carry (sum, count) per city: the zero value (0, 0), a function to add one amount within a partition, and a function to merge two partial pairs. Then mapValues divides, keeping the partitioner. Average order values are close across cities (about ₹293–298) in this generated data.

  6. Step 6 · Several statistics with combineByKey

    comb = city_amt.combineByKey(
    lambda v: (v, v),                                         # first value of a key in a partition
    lambda c, v: (min(c[0], v), max(c[1], v)),                # add a value
    lambda a, b: (min(a[0], b[0]), max(a[1], b[1])))          # merge partitions
print(sorted(comb.collect())[:3])
    combineByKey (min,max): [('Bengaluru', (40, 840)), ('Chennai', (40, 840)), ('Delhi', (40, 840))]
    combineByKey is the general form behind reduceByKey and aggregateByKey: you say how to create a combiner from the first value, how to merge a value into it, and how to merge two combiners. Here (min, max) per city: ₹40 to ₹840 everywhere, the generator’s range.

  7. Step 7 · Join two pair RDDs

    cust = (sc.textFile("customers.csv").filter(lambda l: not l.startswith("customer_id"))
          .map(lambda l: l.split(",")).map(lambda f: (f[0], f[5])))        # (customer_id, plan)
orders_by_cust = rows.map(lambda f: (f[1], int(f[5])))                   # (customer_id, amount)
joined = orders_by_cust.join(cust)                                       # (cid, (amount, plan))
plan_rev = joined.map(lambda kv: (kv[1][1], kv[1][0])).reduceByKey(add).sortBy(lambda kv: -kv[1])
print(plan_rev.collect())
print(orders_by_cust.count(), joined.count(), orders_by_cust.leftOuterJoin(cust).count())
    join -> revenue by plan: [('Basic', 7376620), ('Gold', 3827990), ('Silver', 3516780)] rows: orders 50001 | joined 50001 | leftOuterJoin 50001
    Both RDDs were keyed by customer_id; join returned (cid, (amount, plan)). Re-keying by plan and reducing gave revenue per plan: Basic ₹73,76,620, Gold ₹38,27,990, Silver ₹35,16,780 (Basic customers are half of all customers). The inner and left outer joins both kept all 50,001 orders, so every order had a known customer; a smaller left-join count than orders would mean a bug, a larger one duplicate keys.

6 Real-world use

Pair RDD patterns at work

1. Text and log mining

Counting terms, error codes or URLs in logs and reviews is the word count with a different key.

Lines→(term, 1)→Counts

2. KPIs per entity

Average basket per city, orders per restaurant, revenue per plan: aggregateByKey and reduceByKey.

Orders→by key→KPI table

3. Enriching events

Joining events with customer or product attributes by id before reporting.

Events→join→Enriched

4. Inverted indexes

Search systems build word → documents maps with flatMap and groupByKey or reduceByKey.

Docs→(word, doc)→Index
