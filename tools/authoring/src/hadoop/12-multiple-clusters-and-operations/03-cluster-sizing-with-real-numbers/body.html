1 The story

“How many servers should we buy?”

Kabir's internship has moved to a payments startup. Its UPI switch writes about 2 TB of transaction logs a day, the regulator wants three years kept, and the fraud reports must be ready by 6 AM. The CFO wants one number by Friday: how many servers. Kabir's first guess comes from the lab: “Our three containers wrote 210 MB/s, so…” Riya stops him.

R> Kabir, those 210 MB/s came from three containers sharing one laptop disk. A real server has twelve disks and its own network card. You can't multiply a laptop. K> Then where do I even start? A> From the data, not from the hardware. How much arrives each day, how small it gets on disk, how long you keep it, how many copies. That gives the storage, and storage usually decides the server count. Then you check three things against it: can the nightly work finish in its window, can the NameNode hold all the file and block objects, and does the network keep up. Today we do the whole calculation and also benchmark our own lab properly, so you know what a benchmark number really means.
2 Definition

Cluster sizing

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Cluster sizing (capacity planning) is the process of estimating the hardware a cluster needs to meet its storage, processing, availability and growth requirements. For Hadoop it combines: storage = data ingested per period × compression ratio × retention × replication factor (or erasure-coding overhead), plus space for temporary and intermediate data and headroom to keep disks below a target fill level; compute = the number of concurrent containers needed so that the workload, expressed in container-seconds, completes within its time window; memory and CPU per node derived from container sizes after reserving resources for the operating system and daemons; NameNode heap estimated from the expected number of files, directories and blocks; network bandwidth for ingest, replication and shuffle; and the master and edge nodes required for high availability and access. Estimates rest on stated assumptions and on benchmarks (such as TestDFSIO) of representative hardware.

In simple words

Work out how much data you will keep and how much work you must finish in how much time, add safety margins, and turn that into servers. Write down every assumption, and measure real hardware before trusting a number.

The UPI example, sized. Typical hardware values (disks, RAM, NICs) are assumptions for illustration.

LEVELS> Sizing at three levels L1> Building a godown
  • A grain trader asks: how big a godown? You ask how many sacks arrive a day, how long they stay, and whether every sack must be stored three times (copies) in different corners.
  • You never fill a godown to the roof: you need aisles and space for a sudden delivery (headroom and temp space).
  • Then you check the loading crew: can they move tonight's sacks before morning? That is compute against the batch window.
L2> The calculation, in order
  • Storage: 2 TB × 0.25 = 0.5 TB/day → × 1,095 = 547.5 TB → × 3 = 1,642.5 TB → × 1.25 ÷ 0.70 × 1.3 = 3,812.9 TB → ÷ 96 = 40 workers.
  • NameNode: 2,048 files of 256 MB a day → 2,242,560 files and 4,485,120 blocks in 3 years → about 0.9 GB of objects at 150 B each. Plan a heap several times larger.
  • Compute: 60 TB scanned nightly at 20 MB/s per container = 3,145,728 container-seconds ÷ 10,800 s = 291 containers, against 40 × 30 = 1,200.
  • Per node: 256 GB − 32 GB reserved = 224 GB for YARN; 30 vcores → about 7.5 GB per vcore (Lesson 11.4).
L3> What experienced planners add
  • Tiering: keep 90 hot days with 3 copies and older data in erasure coding: 888.8 TB of data and 22 workers instead of 40 (Lesson 7.3).
  • Network: 20 workers × 10 Gb = 200 Gb against 80 Gb of uplink is 2.5:1 oversubscription; replication and shuffle cross racks, so keep it modest.
  • Failure budget: losing one 96 TB node means re-replicating up to 96 TB; headroom and network decide how long that takes.
  • Benchmark, then re-plan: run TestDFSIO and a real job on one candidate server, replace the assumed throughput, and review against actual growth every quarter.
/LEVELS EXAM> For “How will you size a Hadoop cluster for X TB per day?”, write the storage formula (daily × ratio × retention × replication × temp ÷ fill × growth ÷ disk per node), then one line each for compute (container-seconds ÷ window), NameNode heap (objects × ~150 B) and masters (2 NN, 3 JN, 3 ZK, 2 RM).

Animated example: sizing the payments cluster

From 2 TB a day to 40 servers 6 steps
Animated example
  1. Compress: 2 TB of raw logs a day becomes about 0.5 TB as Parquet with zstd (an assumption based on Lesson 10.2).
  2. Keep and copy: 1,095 days × 0.5 TB = 547.5 TB, three copies = 1,642.5 TB.
  3. Margins: +25% temp, keep disks 70% full, +30% growth = 3,812.9 TB; with 96 TB per server that is 40 workers.
  4. NameNode: 256 MB files give 2.2 million files and 4.5 million blocks: under 1 GB of objects, so the heap is comfortable.
  5. Compute: the nightly 60 TB scan needs 291 containers at once; 40 workers offer 1,200. Storage decides, compute fits.
  6. Tiering: keep 90 hot days with three copies and the rest in erasure coding: 22 workers.
TIP> Size from the data, check with benchmarks. A sizing that starts from “we have budget for 20 servers” and works backwards is a budget, not a plan.
4 Step by step

Benchmark first, then size

Run on the Module 8 lab with dfsio_lab.sh (in “Run it on your PC”), then do the arithmetic for the payments example.

  1. Benchmark writes with one task

    yarn org.apache.hadoop.fs.TestDFSIO -libjars $T -write -nrFiles 1 -fileSize 64MB
    Total MBytes processed: 64 Throughput mb/sec: 113.48 Test exec time sec: 17.6
    One map task writes one 64 MB file with three replicas. 113 MB/s is what one writer gets from our laptop's disk, through the DataNode pipeline.

  2. Add writers

    write, 3 files: Throughput mb/sec: 61.82
write, 6 files: Throughput mb/sec: 34.99
    Per task the number drops; the total grows from about 113 to 185 to 210 MB/s and then flattens. Three “workers” sharing one disk can't add disk speed. On real servers, each node adds its own disks and the total keeps growing.

  3. Benchmark reads

    read, 1 file:  Throughput mb/sec: 320
read, 3 files: Throughput mb/sec: 175.34
read, 6 files: Throughput mb/sec: 130.3
    Reads look faster because the files were written seconds earlier and are still in memory (page cache). For an honest read benchmark, use files much larger than RAM or drop the caches first.

  4. Storage for the payments company

    2 TB × 0.25 = 0.5 TB/day × 1,095 days = 547.5 TB
× 3 copies = 1,642.5 TB × 1.25 temp ÷ 0.70 fill × 1.3 growth = 3,812.9 TB
÷ 96 TB per worker (12 × 8 TB) = 39.7 → 40 workers
    Storage decides the cluster size. The compression ratio and disk per node are assumptions; measure the ratio on a sample of your real data (Lesson 10.2 measured about 0.14 for Parquet + zstd on our CSV, so 0.25 is a cautious choice).

  5. Check NameNode heap and compute

    files: 2,048 a day × 1,095 = 2,242,560; blocks: 4,485,120
objects × 150 B ≈ 0.9 GB  (rule of thumb; plan a much larger heap)
nightly work: 60 TB ÷ 20 MB/s = 3,145,728 container-seconds
÷ 10,800 s (3 h) = 291 containers at once; available 40 × 30 = 1,200
    Both fit with room to spare. If either did not, it would add nodes (compute), larger files (heap) or a second namespace (Lesson 7.2).

  6. Masters, edge and network

    masters: 3 servers (2 NN, 2 RM, 3 JN, 3 ZK, JobHistory), one per rack
edge: 2 servers
racks: 45 servers ÷ 20 per rack = 3 racks
oversubscription: 20 × 10 Gb ÷ 80 Gb uplink = 2.5 : 1
    Spreading masters across racks means a single rack failure can't take the NameNode pair or the JournalNode quorum down (Lesson 7.1).
WARN> Never copy a benchmark from a laptop, a VM or another company's blog into a purchase order. Run TestDFSIO and one real job on a candidate server first, and treat every typical value in this lesson as a placeholder for your own measurement.
5 See it happen

Why more writers didn't mean more speed

Six steps of our TestDFSIO runs: what the tool printed and what was happening on the one shared disk.

Per task vs in total 6 steps
What you see
  1. One writer: 113.48 MB/s for one 64 MB file with three replicas.
  2. Three writers: each gets 61.82 MB/s.
  3. Six writers: each gets only 34.99 MB/s.
  4. In total the cluster wrote about 113, 185 and 210 MB/s: it stopped scaling.
  5. Reads: 320 MB/s for one task, 130.3 each for six, helped by data still in memory.
  6. A real worker has many disks and its own network card, so totals keep growing with nodes.
Behind the screen
  1. The three “workers” are containers on one laptop, all writing to the same physical disk.
  2. With three writers the disk's bandwidth is shared three ways.
  3. With six, each gets a smaller slice.
  4. The total grows only from 185 to 210 MB/s: the disk is saturated.
  5. Freshly written files are still in memory, so reads beat the disk's real speed.
  6. On real hardware every worker adds its own disks, which is why benchmarks must be run on the real thing.
6 Real-world use

Sizing at work

1. Payments: regulation drives retention

Payment companies keep transaction records for years by regulation. Retention × copies decides most of the cluster, so tiering old data to erasure coding or archive storage is the biggest cost lever.

Retention rule→Storage formula→Workers + tiering plan

2. E-commerce: the sale day sets the compute

Clickstream volume can be many times normal during a festival sale. Teams size compute for the peak night's window, and often burst extra capacity in the cloud for those days.

Peak volume→Container-seconds ÷ window→Containers needed

3. Telecom: format is the cheapest saving

Call records compress very well in columnar formats. Converting raw CDR text to ORC or Parquet often reduces the hardware more than any server discount.

Raw CDR text→ORC conversion→Fewer disks

4. Healthcare: file counts, not bytes

Hospitals store millions of small reports and image metadata files. Their sizing worry is NameNode heap, solved by packing files into containers or object storage, not by more disks.

Millions of files→Objects × 150 B→Pack or federate

5. Government: phased buying and growth

Public schemes grow as enrolment grows and data is rarely deleted. Planners model growth year by year and buy hardware in phases, reviewing against real usage before each purchase.

Growth model→Yearly sizing→Phased purchase

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies. Hardware figures in this lesson are assumptions for illustration.
