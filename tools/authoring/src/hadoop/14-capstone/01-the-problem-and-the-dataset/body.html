1 The story

Four canteens, 130 files and no answer

Kadak Canteens runs four college canteens in Kharghar, Vashi, Panvel and Nerul. Every evening each till (POS machine) saves a CSV file of the day's orders, and every night the UPI gateway sends a file of payments. Mrs. Shinde, the owner, hands Kabir a pen drive with all of September and a question her accountant could not answer in four days of Excel: "How much did each canteen really earn, what sells, when are we busiest, and did all the UPI money reach my account?"

K> Fifty megabytes? My laptop can open that in Excel. Why would I need Hadoop? A> Fair question, and you should ask it in every project. Today it is one month and four canteens. Mrs. Shinde wants forty canteens and three years of history next year: that is thousands of files a month and gigabytes a day. We build the pipeline now, on a size we can check by hand, and it scales by adding nodes. R> Then the first job is to write code? A> No. The first job is to understand the problem and the data: what exactly is asked, what is wrong with the files, and how we will prove our answer is right. That is today's lesson. Tomorrow we build. K> How can we prove it? Nobody knows the right answer. A> For this capstone we do. Our generator writes truth.json, the correct totals, alongside the messy files. In a company the same role is played by a trusted source: a bank statement, the finance ledger, the source database's row counts.
2 Definition

A capstone: one real problem, end to end

Formal definition (write this in exams)

A capstone project applies the whole course to one realistic problem. In data engineering it starts with requirements (the business questions and the deliverables), a source inventory (each dataset's format, frequency, volume, keys and owner), data profiling (measuring the actual content: row counts, distinct keys, nulls, invalid values, duplicates and late records) and a set of data quality rules that say how each problem is handled. It ends with acceptance criteria: measurable checks, ideally reconciled against an independent source of truth, that the delivered tables must pass.

In simple words

Before building anything, write down what the business wants, look hard at the files you were given, list everything that is wrong with them and decide what to do about each problem, and agree on the numbers that will prove you got it right.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

RequirementA question the business needs answered, e.g. revenue per canteen per day.
Source inventoryA list of every input: format, size, how often it arrives, key, owner.
ProfilingMeasuring the data as it really is before trusting it.
GrainWhat one row means: here, one order (not one item, not one day).
Business keyThe column that identifies a row: order_id.
Late recordA row for 12 September that arrives in the file of 14 September.
QuarantineA place for rows that cannot be parsed, kept for inspection, never silently dropped.
Acceptance testA check with an expected number; the pipeline is done when all pass.

LEVELS> The capstone at three levels L1> What we are building
  • A small business has messy daily files from four shops and a payments company.
  • We load them into Hadoop, clean them, and produce four tables that answer the owner's four questions.
  • We check our answers against known correct totals, so we are not guessing.
L2> Profiling and quality rules
  • Grain and key: one row = one order, key order_id (e.g. KHR-0914-00610 = canteen, month-day, counter). Duplicates are rows with the same key.
  • Rules: unparsable line → quarantine; student_id = TEST → drop; same order_id twice → keep one; item not in menu → keep, category unknown; amount ≠ qty × menu price → keep, flag and measure the gap.
  • Business date comes from the order's timestamp, not from the file name: 2,100 orders arrive in a later file and must still count on their own day.
L3> Design decisions you must defend
  • Deduplication needs all copies in one place: a copy can sit in the next day's file, so the dedupe key must be order_id across all files. That is a shuffle: MapReduce keyed by order_id (Module 8) does exactly this.
  • Keep the earliest copy: it tells us when the order first arrived, which decides "late".
  • Never fix money silently: Panvel's till charged old prices. Revenue stays what was actually charged; the ₹ gap is reported as a finding, not "corrected".
  • Partition silver by business date so a re-run of one day overwrites only that day (idempotency, Lesson 13.4).
/LEVELS EXAM> For "Describe a big data project you did", start with the problem and the data, not the tools: "4 canteens, 130 daily files, 3.5 lakh orders with duplicates, late rows and price errors; the goal was daily revenue, item mix, peak hours and UPI reconciliation, verified against bank totals." Then the architecture, then results.

Animated example: how one till creates dirty data

A Saturday at the Kharghar till 6 steps
Animated example
  1. On a normal day the till writes one line per order into that day's file.
  2. The canteen's wifi drops; the till keeps billing and stores orders locally.
  3. When the network returns it re-sends recent orders, so order 00377 appears twice.
  4. Orders still unsent at closing go out with the next file. The canteen is shut on Sunday, so they land in Monday's file.
  5. If the till crashes while writing, a line is cut off half-way.
  6. None of this is rare. The rules handle it: one row per order_id, business date from the timestamp, broken lines to quarantine. (Illustrative; our generator plants the same problems at known rates.)
TIP> Write the rules down before you write code, and give each rule a counter. "We dropped some bad rows" is not acceptable in a review; "296 lines quarantined, here they are" is.
4 Step by step

From a pen drive to profiled data in HDFS

All on our Module 8 lab cluster. The full scripts are in "Run on your PC" below.

  1. Generate the dataset

    python3 make_capstone.py
    132 files, 52,085,748 bytes in capstone_raw/ clean orders 354,135, revenue Rs 11,524,009 duplicates 2,784 malformed 296 late 2,100 unknown_item 1,619 price_mismatch 11,711 undercharged 43,710 upi_unconfirmed 6,854
    random.seed(14) makes the files identical on every computer, so your counts match ours. The script also writes truth.json with these numbers.

  2. Look at the raw data

    hdfs dfs -cat /capstone/bronze/orders/college=KHR/orders_KHR_2026-09-14.csv | head -4
    order_id,ts,college,student_id,item,qty,amount,pay_mode KHR-0914-00449,2026-09-14 10:42:17,KHR,S07344,samosa,1,18,UPI KHR-0914-01282,2026-09-14 12:30:28,KHR,S07543,idli,3,90,UPI KHR-0914-01489,2026-09-14 10:01:47,KHR,S06912,idli,1,30,UPI
    Rows are not in time order and every file repeats the header. Note the grain (one order) and the key (order_id) now; both drive the design.

  3. Land it in bronze, exactly as received

    bash land.sh
    5 104 20.9 M /capstone/bronze/orders 1 26 28.8 M /capstone/bronze/payments 1 1 389 /capstone/ref
    Bronze keeps the original bytes, one folder per canteen (college=KHR). If a rule is wrong next month, we can rebuild everything from here.

  4. Profile storage

    hdfs fsck /capstone/bronze | grep -E "Total (files|blocks)|Average block"
    Total files: 130 Total blocks (validated): 130 (avg. block size 400652 B) Default replication factor: 3
    130 files, 130 blocks, 390 replicas, each block 0.4 MB. Every file costs a NameNode entry and, later, one map task. We will pay for this in Lesson 14.3.

  5. Test the cleaning rules on your PC first

    bash local_test.sh      # mapper | sort | reducer, no cluster
    CLEAN 354135 DUPLICATE 2784 LATE 2100 MALFORMED 296 PRICE_MISMATCH 11711 TEST_ORDER 13 UNDERCHARGED_RS 43710 UNKNOWN_ITEM 1619
    The same trick as Lesson 9.8: a Streaming job is just a mapper, a sort and a reducer, so a Linux pipe tests it in 25 seconds. Every counter already equals truth.json.

  6. Write the acceptance tests

    ("orders = truth", s[0], t["clean_orders"]),
("order_id is unique", s[2], s[0]),
("quarantined lines", quarantine_count, t["malformed"]),
("UPI gap = unconfirmed", recon_gap, t["upi_unconfirmed"]),
    Ten checks, written before the pipeline exists. In 14.2 the build is finished only when all ten print PASS.
5 See it happen

Landing 130 files: the terminal and the NameNode

On the left what you type and see; on the right what HDFS stores and what it costs.

From pen drive to bronze 6 steps
What you see
  1. The generator writes 132 files (130 data files plus menu.csv and truth.json).
  2. land.sh creates one folder per canteen and copies its 26 daily files.
  3. hdfs dfs -count: 104 order files and 26 payment files in bronze.
  4. du shows logical size and size with 3 replicas: 7.0 MB of Kharghar data uses 20.9 MB of disk.
  5. fsck: 130 files in 130 blocks, each only about 0.4 MB.
  6. The cleaning rules, run locally, reproduce every number in truth.json.
Behind the screen
  1. Until we put them, the files exist only on the local disk; HDFS knows nothing.
  2. Each -put asks the NameNode for a block and streams it through a pipeline of DataNodes (Lesson 6.1).
  3. After landing, the NameNode holds 130 file and 130 block entries in memory.
  4. With replication 3 on 3 workers, every worker stores a copy of every block: 52 MB of data uses about 156 MB of disk.
  5. Each block is about 0.4 MB. Small files waste NameNode memory and create one map task each.
  6. The local pipe test runs the same Python on one machine: it checks the rules, not the cluster. (Block squares are symbolic.)
6 Real-world use

The same first week, in other companies

1. Retail chain: store sales

A supermarket chain receives nightly POS files from hundreds of stores. Before building dashboards, the team profiles them and finds re-sent batches, test transactions and stores with outdated price lists, exactly our dirt at a larger scale.

Store tills→inventory + profiling + rules→Trusted sales tables

2. Payments: settlement reconciliation

A payment aggregator matches merchant orders with bank and UPI switch files every day. The acceptance test is the bank statement: every rupee must be matched, refunded or explained.

Orders + bank files→match on transaction key→Exceptions report

3. Highways: toll transactions

Toll lanes upload FASTag reads in batches; lanes that lose connectivity upload hours later. Reports must use the time of passage, not upload time, the same "business date from the timestamp" rule.

Lane batches→event time, dedupe on read ID→Daily revenue per plaza

4. Hospitals: pharmacy sales

A hospital group finds medicines sold that are missing from the central drug master. Like our maggi, they are kept and flagged, and the master-data team is told, instead of silently losing revenue from reports.

Branch sales→join with master, flag unknown→Fix the master

5. Any migration

When a company moves pipelines to a new platform, the old system's output is the truth file. The new pipeline is accepted only when counts and totals match, day by day (Lesson 13.4).

Old reports→acceptance tests→Cut-over

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies. Kadak Canteens and its data are fictional.
