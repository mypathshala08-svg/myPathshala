1 The story

The day the NameNode went down

Exam-result week at the university. The results portal reads marks files from HDFS. At 10:40 the NameNode server's motherboard dies. Every DataNode is healthy, every block is safe on disk, and yet nobody can open a single file: the only machine that knows which blocks make which file is gone. Restarting on new hardware from the last checkpoint takes the admin team almost an hour. Riya, watching the angry messages, asks the obvious question: why was there only one?

A> In Hadoop 1 the NameNode was a single point of failure. Since Hadoop 2 you can run two (or more) NameNodes: one active, the others standby. The standby replays every change from a shared edit log kept on JournalNodes, and receives block reports from all DataNodes, so it is ready to take over in seconds. R> Who decides when to switch? A> ZooKeeper and a small process next to each NameNode, the ZKFailoverController. And there's a harder question: what if the old active isn't dead, just frozen, and wakes up later? Two active NameNodes writing different histories would corrupt the file system. Today we build a real HA cluster with 2 NameNodes, 3 JournalNodes and ZooKeeper, crash it, freeze it, and starve it, and watch exactly what Hadoop does.
2 Definition

NameNode high availability

Formal definition (write this in exams)

HDFS high availability (HA) runs two or more NameNodes for one namespace (a nameservice): exactly one is active and serves clients; the others are standby. With the Quorum Journal Manager (QJM), the active NameNode writes every namespace edit to a group of JournalNodes (typically 3 or 5) and an edit succeeds only when a majority acknowledges it; standbys tail these edits to keep their namespace current, while DataNodes send heartbeats and block reports to all NameNodes. Automatic failover uses a ZooKeeper ensemble and a ZKFailoverController (ZKFC) beside each NameNode: the ZKFC monitors its NameNode's health and holds an ephemeral lock znode while it is active; when the active's ZooKeeper session expires, another ZKFC takes the lock, fences the old active, and transitions its NameNode to active. JournalNodes additionally enforce a monotonically increasing epoch, rejecting writes from any NameNode with an older epoch, which prevents split-brain.

In simple words

Two NameNodes, one in charge. The one in charge writes every change to three JournalNodes; the spare reads those changes and stays up to date. ZooKeeper notices when the one in charge disappears and promotes the spare in about ten seconds. A NameNode that was replaced and comes back is refused by the JournalNodes, so there is never more than one boss.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

NameserviceA logical name for the HA pair, e.g. hdfs://canteen; clients never name a single NameNode.
Active / standbyThe NameNode serving clients / the warm spare replaying edits.
JournalNode (JN)A small daemon storing the shared edit log; run 3 or 5.
QJMQuorum Journal Manager: writes succeed when a majority of JNs accept.
ZKFCZKFailoverController: health-checks its NameNode and runs the election in ZooKeeper.
FencingMaking sure the old active can no longer act (e.g. ssh and kill it).
EpochA number that grows with each new active writer; JNs reject older epochs.
Split-brainTwo nodes both believing they are active: the disaster HA must prevent.

LEVELS> HA at three levels L1> A cricket team captain
  • The captain (active NameNode) makes every decision; the vice-captain (standby) watches every ball and knows the match situation.
  • The scorebook is kept by three scorers (JournalNodes). A decision counts only if at least two of them write it down.
  • If the captain is injured, the umpire (ZooKeeper) hands the armband to the vice-captain. If the old captain walks back on, the scorers refuse to record his decisions: the new captain has a newer armband number.
L2> How a failover happens
  • The active's ZKFC holds an ephemeral znode (ActiveStandbyElectorLock) in ZooKeeper. It disappears when its session expires (10 s without contact).
  • The standby's ZKFC grabs the lock, fences the old active, and tells its NameNode to become active. Our log: “Checking for any old active” → “Fencing successful” → “Successfully transitioned … to active state” within one second.
  • Clients use a failover proxy provider: they know both addresses and retry the other on errors such as StandbyException.
  • Manual, graceful switch: hdfs haadmin -failover nn2 nn1 (2.2 s in our lab).
L3> Why split-brain can't happen with QJM
  • Each new active asks the JournalNodes for a new epoch greater than any they have seen; a majority must promise it. Our epochs went 2 → 3 → 4 → … with every switch.
  • A frozen old active wakes up with epoch 7 and tries to write: every JN answers “IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8”, and the NameNode exits. Even with a fake fencer (/bin/true), the shared log cannot be corrupted.
  • Fencing is still needed in production (e.g. sshfence): an old active could otherwise answer stale reads to clients for a while.
  • With fewer than a majority of JNs, no NameNode can write, so both shut down: HDFS chooses consistency over availability (the CP side of the CAP theorem).
  • Since Hadoop 3, more than two NameNodes are supported (one active, several standbys), and Observer NameNodes can serve reads.
/LEVELS EXAM> For “Explain NameNode HA with QJM”: draw active + standby NameNodes, 3 JournalNodes, ZooKeeper with two ZKFCs, and DataNodes reporting to both. Then list: shared edits via quorum (majority), standby tails edits, automatic failover via ZooKeeper session + ZKFC, fencing and epochs to prevent split-brain. Mention the Secondary NameNode is not used in HA; the standby does checkpointing.

4 Step by step

Building and breaking a real HA cluster

Our lab: ZooKeeper, 3 JournalNodes, NameNodes nn1 and nn2 (each with a ZKFC) and 3 DataNodes, all apache/hadoop:3.5.0 containers. The full files are in “Run it on your PC”.

  1. The HA configuration

    CORE-SITE.XML_fs.defaultFS=hdfs://canteen
CORE-SITE.XML_ha.zookeeper.quorum=zk:2181
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.nameservices=canteen
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.ha.namenodes.canteen=nn1,nn2
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.namenode.rpc-address.canteen.nn1=nn1:8020
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.namenode.shared.edits.dir=qjournal://jn1:8485;jn2:8485;jn3:8485/canteen
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.client.failover.proxy.provider.canteen=org.apache.hadoop.hdfs.server.namenode.ha.ConfiguredFailoverProxyProvider
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.ha.automatic-failover.enabled=true
HDFS-SITE.XML_dfs.ha.fencing.methods=shell(/bin/true)
    The client sees one name, canteen, with two possible addresses. shell(/bin/true) is a “fencer that always succeeds”, acceptable only in a lab; we'll see why QJM keeps us safe anyway.

  2. First start: format, bootstrap, elect

    # on nn1, once:
hdfs namenode -format -nonInteractive -clusterId canteen-cluster
hdfs zkfc -formatZK -nonInteractive
# on nn2, once:
hdfs namenode -bootstrapStandby -nonInteractive
# on both:
hdfs --daemon start zkfc; hdfs namenode

hdfs haadmin -getAllServiceState
    nn1:8020 active nn2:8020 standby
    nn2 copies nn1's fsimage; both ZKFCs race for the lock znode in ZooKeeper, and nn1 won. ZooKeeper now holds /hadoop-ha/canteen/ActiveStandbyElectorLock and ActiveBreadCrumb (we listed them with the ZooKeeper client).

  3. Crash the active while a client writes

    docker kill lab7-nn1-1
    15:44:15 before: nn1:8020=active nn2:8020=standby 15:44:25 >>> docker kill lab7-nn1-1 (the active NameNode crashes) 15:44:27 nn2 is standby ... 15:44:34 nn2 is standby 15:44:36 nn2 is active 15:44:24 write 3 ok (took 3 s) 15:44:46 write 4 ok (took 22 s) 15:44:48 write 5 ok (took 2 s) ... 15:45:03 write 12 ok (took 2 s) 15:45:04 files written: 12
    About 10 s to fail over: the ZooKeeper session timeout. The client's write 4 was in progress: it tried nn1 (20 s connect timeout to the dead address), then nn2, and succeeded. 12 of 12 writes succeeded; the application only saw one slow write.

  4. What the standby's ZKFC did

    grep … /var/log/hadoop/hadoop-hadoop-zkfc-nn2.out
    15:44:34 ActiveStandbyElector - Checking for any old active which needs to be fenced... 15:44:35 NodeFencer - ====== Beginning Service Fencing Process... ====== 15:44:35 NodeFencer - ====== Fencing successful by method ShellCommandFencer(/bin/true) ====== 15:44:35 ZKFailoverController - Trying to make NameNode at nn2/172.20.0.7:8020 active... 15:44:35 ZKFailoverController - Successfully transitioned NameNode at nn2/172.20.0.7:8020 to active state
    Lock taken → old active fenced → standby promoted, all within about a second. Before becoming active, nn2 reads any remaining edits from the JournalNodes, so no change is lost.

  5. Freeze the active (the zombie test)

    docker pause lab7-nn1-1 ; sleep 25 ; docker unpause lab7-nn1-1
    15:40:49 before : nn1:8020=active nn2:8020=standby | epochs: jn1=7 jn2=7 jn3=7 15:41:16 during : nn2=active | epochs: jn1=8 jn2=8 jn3=8 15:41:19 client write to the new active: ok 15:41:19 >>> docker unpause lab7-nn1-1 (the old active wakes up, still thinking it is active) 172.20.0.5:8485: IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8 ; journal id: canteen 172.20.0.4:8485: IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8 ; journal id: canteen 172.20.0.2:8485: IPC's epoch 7 is less than the last promised epoch 8 ; journal id: canteen Exiting with status 1: Error: flush failed for required journal ... SHUTDOWN_MSG: Shutting down NameNode at nn1/172.20.0.6 15:41:39 after : nn1:8020=Failed nn2:8020=active
    A frozen process (like a long garbage-collection pause) is the dangerous case: it doesn't know it was replaced. When nn1 woke and tried to write with epoch 7, all three JournalNodes refused, and nn1 shut itself down. Split-brain prevented, even with a fencer that does nothing.

  6. Lose the JournalNode majority

    docker stop lab7-jn3-1 ; … ; docker stop lab7-jn2-1
    15:45:56 nn1:8020=standby nn2:8020=active 15:46:00 write 1: ok 15:46:00 >>> stop jn3 (2 of 3 JournalNodes left) 15:46:02 write 2: ok 15:46:02 >>> stop jn2 (1 of 3 left: no majority) 15:47:32 write 3: FAILED (no answer) 15:47:42 nn1:8020=Failed nn2:8020=Failed nn1: java.io.IOException: Timed out waiting 20000ms for a quorum of nodes to respond. nn2: java.io.IOException: Timed out waiting 20000ms for a quorum of nodes to respond.
    With 2 of 3 JournalNodes, everything works. With 1 of 3, the active can't get a majority for 20 s and stops; the standby can't become active either. HDFS stops rather than risk losing edits. Starting the JournalNodes and NameNodes again (restore.sh) brought the cluster back with all files.
WARN> Put the three JournalNodes on three different machines (ideally different racks), never all on the NameNode hosts. Losing two JournalNodes stops the whole file system, as step 6 showed.
5 See it happen

Crash, takeover, and the zombie that wakes up

Six steps from our lab. Left: what admins and clients see. Right: ZooKeeper, the ZKFCs and the JournalNodes.

Automatic failover with QJM 6 steps
What you see
  1. nn1 is active, nn2 standby. A client writes a file every 2 seconds.
  2. nn1 crashes. For a few seconds nn2 is still standby; one client write is waiting.
  3. About 10 s later nn2 is active. The waiting write succeeds (22 s), and all 12 writes complete.
  4. A different test: nn1 is frozen, not dead. nn2 takes over and the epoch rises from 7 to 8.
  5. nn1 wakes up and tries to write edits with epoch 7. All three JournalNodes refuse.
  6. nn1 shuts itself down. There is only ever one active writer.
Behind the screen
  1. nn1's ZKFC holds the lock znode; nn1 writes each edit to the JournalNodes; nn2 tails them.
  2. nn1 crashes. Its ZooKeeper session stops being renewed.
  3. After the 10 s session timeout the lock disappears; nn2's ZKFC takes it, fences nn1 and makes nn2 active with a higher epoch.
  4. In the zombie test, nn1 is only frozen. nn2 becomes active with epoch 8, and the JNs promise to ignore anything older.
  5. nn1 wakes up and sends edits with epoch 7. The JNs reject them.
  6. nn1 exits. The epoch rule guarantees a single writer even if fencing fails.

6 Real-world use

HA at work

1. Every production cluster

Enterprise distributions and admin guides treat NameNode HA with QJM as the standard for production clusters; a single NameNode is accepted only for dev and test.

2 NameNodes→3 or 5 JournalNodes→ZooKeeper

2. Rolling upgrades and patching

Admins patch the standby, fail over to it with haadmin -failover, then patch the old active. The file system stays online during OS and Hadoop upgrades.

Patch standby→Graceful failover→Patch the other

3. Long garbage-collection pauses

A NameNode with a very large heap can pause for many seconds. If that exceeds the ZooKeeper timeout, a failover happens and the paused NameNode exits on waking, exactly our zombie test. Admins tune heap and timeouts to avoid needless failovers.

Pause > 10 s→Failover→Old NN exits

4. Monitoring failovers

Monitoring reads tag.HAState from each NameNode's JMX and alerts on changes. A failover is not an outage, but it means something failed and should be investigated.

/jmx→HAState watch→On-call alert

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.
