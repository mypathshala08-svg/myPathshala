1 The story

A ten-second report that waited two minutes

Monday morning. Kabir starts a big experiment on the college cluster: 30 map tasks, 40 seconds each. Fifteen seconds later Riya submits the placement-cell report the principal needs at 9:30. Her job has two tiny tasks, 5 seconds of work each. It sits in ACCEPTED, then waits. And waits. The report that needs ten seconds of work finishes after 2 minutes 40 seconds, because the cluster gave every free container to whoever asked first.

R> Sir, my job was tiny. Why did YARN make it wait behind Kabir's? A> Because the ResourceManager was running the FIFO scheduler: first in, first out. Kabir asked first, so every container that freed up went to him until his last task had a home. YARN doesn't decide who is important. The scheduler does, and you choose the scheduler and its queues. K> And when we gave our team a separate queue, my experiment became slower: 430 seconds instead of 199. A> That is the whole lesson in two numbers. Every scheduler trades something. Today we run the same pair of jobs under FIFO, the Capacity Scheduler (three different settings) and the Fair Scheduler, and read exactly what each one gave and took away.
2 Definition

The YARN scheduler

Formal definition (write this in exams)

The scheduler is the pluggable component of the YARN ResourceManager that decides which application receives each free container, based on configured queues and policies. It is a pure scheduler: it doesn't monitor or restart applications. Hadoop ships three: the FIFO Scheduler, which serves applications in submission order; the Capacity Scheduler (the default in Apache Hadoop 3), which divides the cluster into a hierarchy of queues, each guaranteed a percentage of resources, able to grow elastically up to a maximum when others are idle, with per-user limits and optional preemption to reclaim guaranteed capacity; and the Fair Scheduler, which divides resources so that all running applications (or queues, by weight) receive an equal fair share over time, with minimum and maximum shares and optional preemption. The scheduler is selected with yarn.resourcemanager.scheduler.class; applications choose a queue with mapreduce.job.queuename or placement rules.

In simple words

The ResourceManager hands out containers; the scheduler decides who gets the next one. FIFO: whoever came first. Capacity: every team owns a slice and can borrow idle slices. Fair: everybody running gets an equal share.

Words you'll meet in this lesson

QueueA named share of the cluster that applications are submitted to, e.g. root.prod.
CapacityThe guaranteed share of a queue, in percent of its parent: prod 70.
Maximum capacityHow far a queue may grow when others are idle (elasticity).
User limit factorHow many times its capacity one user may use. Default 1.
PreemptionKilling containers of an over-using queue to give an under-served one its guarantee.
Fair shareWhat a queue or app would get if resources were split equally (by weight).
DRFDominant Resource Fairness: fairness counted on memory AND CPU.
Placement ruleA rule that picks the queue for an app (by user, group, or what was asked).

LEVELS> Schedulers at three levels L1> A canteen with one counter
  • FIFO is one counter and one line. If the person in front orders 30 plates, you wait for all 30, even for one chai.
  • Capacity is separate counters for staff and students. Each counter has its own cooks; if the staff counter is empty, its cooks may help the students, and come back when staff arrive.
  • Fair is one cook who serves everyone in the line in turns, a plate each, so a short order is never stuck behind a long one for long.
L2> What really decides who gets a container
  • Every NodeManager heartbeat (about once a second) tells the RM how much is free. The scheduler then picks a queue, then an app inside it, then a request, and hands out a container.
  • Capacity picks the queue that is furthest below its guaranteed capacity first. A queue may grow up to maximum-capacity, but one user may hold only user-limit-factor × capacity. With the default factor 1, our single-user big job could never pass 30%, which is why it took 430 s.
  • Without preemption, the scheduler only reassigns containers when they finish. Our small job in prod waited 51 s for a 40-second dev task to end.
  • Fair computes each queue's fair share from weights and gives the next container to whoever is furthest below theirs.
L3> Production design
  • Typical layout: root.prod.etl, root.prod.reports, root.adhoc, root.ds, with guarantees that add up to 100% at each level and maximums that allow borrowing.
  • Preemption makes guarantees real. The cost is wasted work: killed containers rerun. Tune max_wait_before_kill, total preemption per round and natural termination factor, and exempt queues with disable_preemption.
  • maximum-am-resource-percent (we set 0.5) limits how much of a queue AMs may take, so 50 waiting jobs can't fill a queue with ApplicationMasters and no tasks.
  • Change queues without restarting: edit capacity-scheduler.xml, then yarn rmadmin -refreshQueues. Node labels let a queue own specific machines (GPU nodes, for example).
  • Cloudera's platform standardised on the Capacity Scheduler; the Fair Scheduler is still in Apache Hadoop, but new clusters usually start with Capacity.
/LEVELS EXAM> For “Compare FIFO, Capacity and Fair schedulers”, give one line each (order of arrival / guaranteed queue shares with elasticity / equal share over time), name the default (Capacity in Hadoop 3), and the two settings that make Capacity behave well: maximum-capacity for elasticity and preemption to win back guarantees.

Animated example: the 9 AM regulatory report at a bank

A bank must send a regulatory report by 9:00 every morning, from the same cluster where analysts run ad-hoc queries. Watch the same morning under three set-ups. (A story with round numbers, built on what our lab showed.)

Report vs ad-hoc queries 6 steps
Animated example
  1. FIFO, 8:30: an analyst's huge query arrives first and takes every free container.
  2. FIFO, 8:40: the regulatory report waits behind it and misses 9:00.
  3. Capacity: the report queue owns 70%, so the report starts at once.
  4. Capacity, at night: with no elasticity the query can't use the idle 70%, so hardware sits idle.
  5. Elastic capacity: with maximum-capacity 100% the query borrows everything at night, but in the morning the report must wait for borrowed containers to finish.
  6. Preemption: the RM takes back 2 containers from the query; the report is on time and the query reruns a little work.

4 Step by step

Six runs, one pair of jobs

Each run recreates only the ResourceManager with another scheduler (rm.sh); HDFS and the NodeManagers keep running and re-register. Then sched_lab.sh starts the big job, waits 15 s and starts the small one. Both are Hadoop's built-in SleepJob, so the work is identical every time.

  1. FIFO: the head of the line takes everything

    bash rm.sh fifo
docker exec lab8-namenode-1 bash /tmp/mr/sched_lab.sh default default
    scheduler: scheduler.fifo.FifoScheduler big queue=default AM waited 0.6 s, job took 198.8 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0 small queue=default AM waited 120.4 s, job took 159.6 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0
    The small job couldn't even start its ApplicationMaster for two minutes: FIFO gives every free container to the oldest app that still wants one.

  2. Capacity with queues: the small job flies, the big one crawls

    bash rm.sh capacity
docker exec lab8-namenode-1 bash /tmp/mr/sched_lab.sh dev prod
    Queue Name : prod Capacity: 70.0, MaximumCapacity: 100.0, CurrentCapacity: 0.0 Queue Name : dev Capacity: 30.000002, MaximumCapacity: 100.0, CurrentCapacity: 0.0 big queue=root.dev AM waited 0.6 s, job took 430.0 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0 small queue=root.prod AM waited 0.1 s, job took 24.2 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0
    Prod had room the whole time. But the big job took more than twice as long as under FIFO, even though dev's maximum is 100%.

  3. Why so slow? Measure the big job alone

    curl -s http://resourcemanager:8088/ws/v1/cluster/metrics   # allocatedMB, containersAllocated
    capacity allocated 2560 of 6144 MB, containers 5 capacity allocated 2560 of 6144 MB, containers 5 fair allocated 6144 of 6144 MB, containers 6
    Under Capacity the job never grew past about 30% (5 containers of 512 MB) with the rest of the cluster idle. The cause is user-limit-factor, default 1: a single user may use at most 1 × the queue's capacity. Under Fair it took all 6,144 MB, but in only 6 containers: Fair rounds requests up to its 1,024 MB increment, so each 384 MB map got a 1 GB container.

  4. Capacity with user-limit-factor 4: elastic again

    bash rm.sh capacity-ulf      # adds root.dev.user-limit-factor=4
docker exec lab8-namenode-1 bash /tmp/mr/sched_lab.sh dev prod
    big queue=root.dev AM waited 0.7 s, job took 207.6 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0 small queue=root.prod AM waited 51.0 s, job took 121.5 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0
    Dev borrowed the whole idle cluster, so the big job is back to FIFO speed. The price: when prod's job arrived, every container was busy, and the scheduler could only wait for dev's 40-second tasks to finish.

  5. Add preemption: borrowed containers are taken back

    bash rm.sh capacity-ulf-preempt   # + yarn.resourcemanager.scheduler.monitor.enable=true
docker exec lab8-namenode-1 bash /tmp/mr/sched_lab.sh dev prod
    big queue=root.dev AM waited 0.7 s, job took 215.4 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 2 small queue=root.prod AM waited 20.7 s, job took 77.9 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0
    The preemption monitor (ProportionalCapacityPreemptionPolicy) saw prod below its 70% guarantee, waited, then killed 2 of dev's containers. Prod's wait dropped from 51 s to 21 s; the big job paid 8 seconds for the redone work.

  6. Fair Scheduler with two equal queues

    bash rm.sh fair
docker exec lab8-namenode-1 bash /tmp/mr/sched_lab.sh dev prod
    scheduler: scheduler.fair.FairScheduler big queue=root.dev AM waited 1.0 s, job took 390.6 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0 small queue=root.prod AM waited 37.5 s, job took 152.8 s, SUCCEEDED, preempted containers: 0
    Without preemption, Fair also waits for containers to finish, and with 1 GB containers the cluster ran only half as many tasks at once. Our lab's small maps simply don't suit Fair's default 1 GB increment (yarn.resource-types.memory-mb.increment-allocation).
WARN> A real trap we hit: the Docker image writes capacity-scheduler.xml from the environment, and when we set only one capacity key, the file contained only that key. The ResourceManager refused to start: Queue configuration missing child queue names for root. On any cluster, a partial capacity-scheduler.xml breaks the RM the same way; always keep root.queues and the capacities in the file.
5 See it happen

The same 12 containers, three schedulers

Six steps through the real runs: what the terminal reported, and how the scheduler filled the cluster's 12 container slots.

Who gets the next container? 6 steps
What you see
  1. FIFO run: the big job starts, the small one follows 15 s later.
  2. The small job waits 120 s just to get its ApplicationMaster.
  3. Capacity with two queues: the small job is done in 24 s, but the big job takes 430 s.
  4. Measured alone, the big job never holds more than 2,560 MB: the user limit.
  5. With user-limit-factor 4 the big job is fast again, and the small one waits 51 s.
  6. With preemption, 2 containers are taken back and the small job's wait drops to 21 s.
Behind the screen
  1. FIFO hands every free container to the oldest application that still has tasks waiting.
  2. The small job sits in the queue until the big job's last tasks have been placed.
  3. Capacity keeps 70% for prod, so the small job starts immediately while dev stays near its 30%.
  4. The cap comes from user-limit-factor 1: one user may hold only 1 × the queue's capacity, so slots stay idle.
  5. With user-limit-factor 4, dev borrows the idle cluster; prod must wait for a container to free up.
  6. Preemption returns 2 containers to prod quickly; dev loses and reruns the work they held.
6 Real-world use

Schedulers at work

1. Banking: regulatory reports with a deadline

A bank gives its overnight regulatory and risk jobs a queue with a large guarantee and preemption enabled, so analysts' ad-hoc work can borrow the cluster during the day but never delays the morning filing.

reports queue 40%→preemption on→Filed by 9 AM

2. E-commerce: a sale day

During a big festive sale, an online retailer raises the live-dashboard and fraud queues' capacity for the week (a config change plus yarn rmadmin -refreshQueues) and caps the data-science queue's maximum, then reverts afterwards.

Sale week→refreshQueues→Dashboards first

3. Telecom: billing windows

Call-detail-record billing runs in a fixed night window. Its queue has a high guarantee; the day-time analytics queues use a large maximum capacity so the hardware isn't idle while billing sleeps.

Night: billing→elastic queues→Day: analytics

4. University research cluster

Departments that paid for the hardware get queues in proportion to their share, with user-limit-factor and per-user limits so one PhD student's thousand-task job can't take a whole department's capacity.

Funding share→queue per dept→Per-user limits

5. Mixed hardware with node labels

A healthcare-imaging team's queue is allowed onto nodes labelled gpu; other queues can't use them. Node labels plus queues let one YARN cluster serve very different workloads.

Label nodes→queue → label→GPU jobs isolated

Simplified; organisations differ in tools and policies.
