1 The story

Why waiting made us faster

Before you start: 2.1 How a producer sends a message.

🤔 Waiting 5 milliseconds made our producer 40× faster. Why would waiting help?

With linger.ms=0 our 20,000 orders went out in 1,250 small batches and took 2.4 s. With linger.ms=5 they went in 20 batches and took 0.06 s. Then compression shrank the stored data 10×. This lesson shows the batch on disk and every trade-off.

2 Definition

Batching and compression

Formal definition (write this in exams)

The producer groups records destined for the same partition into a record batch. A batch is sent when it reaches batch.size bytes or when linger.ms has elapsed since its first record, whichever is first; multiple batches for the same broker travel in one Produce request. If compression.type is set, the producer compresses each batch as a unit (gzip, snappy, lz4 or zstd); brokers store batches in compressed form (recompressing only if the topic’s compression.type differs from the producer’s), and consumers decompress on read.

In simple words

The producer waits a tiny moment to collect records into a group, squeezes the group with compression, and sends it in one go. Kafka stores the group squeezed, and readers unsqueeze it.

Why, how, where

Why

Every request has a fixed cost (network round trip, disk write, replication). Many tiny requests waste it; compression also saves disk and bandwidth.

the problem it solves

How

The producer collects records per partition until batch.size is reached or linger.ms passes, compresses the whole batch, and sends it. Brokers store the batch compressed; consumers decompress.

the mechanism

Where

High-volume topics: clicks, logs, orders at peak, CDC streams. JSON compresses especially well.

in real systems

When not

Very latency-sensitive single events (keep linger small) and already-compressed payloads such as images (compression adds CPU, saves little).

Dissect a compressed batch

LEVELS> Batching at three levels L1> Shared auto-rickshaw
  • An auto that leaves with one passenger (linger 0) makes many trips. Waiting 5 seconds for 3 passengers saves fuel and the road. Packing luggage tightly (compression) fits more in each trip.
L2> The settings
  • linger.ms: maximum time to wait for more records (Java default 5 ms since Kafka 4.0, librdkafka 5 ms).
  • batch.size: maximum bytes per partition batch (Java 16 KB; librdkafka batch.size 1 MB and batch.num.messages 10,000). A batch is sent when full or when linger expires.
  • compression.type: none, gzip, snappy, lz4, zstd. The whole batch is compressed as one unit.
L3> Where compression happens
  • The topic config compression.type defaults to producer: the broker stores the producer’s compressed batch as is (it still validates it). If the topic sets a different codec, the broker recompresses, which costs broker CPU.
  • Consumers decompress. Since Kafka 3.8 you can also set compression.gzip.level, compression.lz4.level, compression.zstd.level (KIP-390).
  • Bigger batches also raise per-record latency slightly and use more producer memory; max.request.size (1 MB) and the broker’s message.max.bytes cap one batch.
/LEVELS

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: Kafka compresses each message separately.
    Reality (Kafka 4.4): The whole batch is compressed as one block.
    Proof: Step 3: 10× smaller because repeated JSON fields compress across records.
  • Myth: linger.ms always adds noticeable delay.
    Reality (Kafka 4.4): A few ms often makes total throughput far higher.
    Proof: Step 2: 2.4 s → 0.06 s with linger 5 ms.
  • Myth: Brokers decompress and store raw data.
    Reality (Kafka 4.4): With compression.type=producer they store the compressed batch.
    Proof: dump-log shows compresscodec per batch.
EXAM> “How do you increase producer throughput?” Increase linger.ms and batch.size so batches fill, enable compression (zstd/lz4), keep acks/idempotence for safety, use enough partitions, and keep topic compression.type=producer to avoid broker recompression.

4 Step by step

Batches and bytes, measured

All outputs are real: Kafka 4.4.0 (3 brokers), Python confluent-kafka 2.16.1, topics with 3 partitions and RF 3. Batches counted with kafka-dump-log.sh, bytes with du on the broker. Script: jobs/m2/l23_batch.py.

  1. Step 1 · The payload

    events = [json.dumps({"order_id": f"o{i:06d}", "city": random.choice(cities),
          "items": [...], "amount": ..., "status": "PLACED"}) for i in range(20000)]
    == payload: 20,000 JSON orders, 1967 KiB of JSON values (plus keys and record headers on disk)
    20,000 realistic JSON orders. On disk each record also carries its key, offset delta, timestamp delta and headers.

  2. Step 2 · linger.ms: 0 vs 5 vs 50

    for lg in (0, 5, 50):
    run(f"linger-{lg}", {"linger.ms": lg, "compression.type": "none"})
# batches counted with kafka-dump-log on every partition
    == 1) linger.ms: wait a little, send bigger batches linger.ms=0 batches on disk 1250 avg 16.0 records/batch time 2.40 s linger.ms=5 batches on disk 20 avg 1000.0 records/batch time 0.06 s linger.ms=50 batches on disk 3 avg 6666.7 records/batch time 0.04 s
    With no waiting the producer sent 1,250 batches of about 16 records and needed 2.4 s. Waiting just 5 ms gave 20 batches of 1,000 records and 0.06 s. 50 ms changed little more: the gain comes from the first few milliseconds.

  3. Step 3 · Five codecs

    for c in ("none", "gzip", "snappy", "lz4", "zstd"):
    run(f"comp-{c}", {"linger.ms": 50, "compression.type": c})
# size = du -cb of the partitions' .log files on the broker
    == 2) compression.type (linger.ms=50) none log size (3 partitions) 2268 KiB 1.0x smaller than none time 0.05 s gzip log size (3 partitions) 221 KiB 10.3x smaller than none time 0.11 s snappy log size (3 partitions) 413 KiB 5.5x smaller than none time 0.04 s lz4 log size (3 partitions) 458 KiB 4.9x smaller than none time 0.07 s zstd log size (3 partitions) 231 KiB 9.8x smaller than none time 0.04 s
    gzip and zstd shrank the log about 10×, snappy and lz4 about 5×. Times are all tiny here; at large scale gzip costs the most producer CPU, lz4 the least, zstd sits between with a ratio close to gzip.

6 Real-world use

Batching at work

1. Log pipelines

Big batches + lz4/zstd: terabytes per day with modest hardware.

Logs→batch + zstd→Cheap

2. Clickstreams

Millions of small JSON events compress ~10×.

Clicks→compress→Less disk

3. Database change streams

Large bursts during bulk updates are smoothed by batching.

DB→batch→Kafka

4. Payments

Small linger keeps latency low; lz4 adds little CPU.

Payment→linger 1–5 ms→Fast

5. Edge devices

Compression saves expensive mobile bandwidth.

Device→gzip→Less data

Ratios and timings depend on data and hardware; ours are from the lab.
