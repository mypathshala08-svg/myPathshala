1 The story

Queue or log?

Before you start: 1.2 Messaging models.

🤔 Five orders go into RabbitMQ and five into Kafka. A billing service joins five minutes later. Who still has the orders for it?

Only Kafka. In our lab RabbitMQ 4.4 handed the five orders to two competing consumers and, once acknowledged, they were gone: the late billing consumer got nothing. Kafka kept all five; two groups each read all five, and a new group replayed them. That one difference, a queue that deletes versus a log that keeps, explains most of the “Kafka vs X” question.

2 Definition

Kafka vs RabbitMQ, Pulsar and others

Formal definition (write this in exams)

Message brokers such as RabbitMQ route messages to queues and remove them once consumers acknowledge them, optimising per-message delivery and routing. Distributed logs such as Kafka append records to partitioned, replicated logs retained independently of consumption, letting many consumer groups read and replay at their own pace. Pulsar combines a log with queue-style subscriptions on a layered architecture (stateless brokers, BookKeeper storage).

In simple words

RabbitMQ is like a post office: deliver and forget. Kafka is like an archive: keep everything, let anyone read from any point. Pulsar tries to do both with a different design. Pick by what you need to do with the data afterwards.

Why, how, where

Why

Interviewers and architects ask it constantly, and picking the wrong tool is expensive: a task queue on Kafka can be awkward; an event history on RabbitMQ is impossible once messages are acked.

the problem it solves

How

RabbitMQ routes messages through exchanges into queues and deletes them after acknowledgement (streams are the exception). Kafka appends to partitioned logs and keeps data by retention; consumers track their own offsets. Pulsar stores segments in BookKeeper with stateless brokers and supports both streaming and queue subscriptions.

the mechanism

Where

RabbitMQ: task queues, RPC, complex routing, per-message TTL/priority. Kafka: event streaming, many independent consumers, replay, CDC, analytics. Pulsar: multi-tenant, geo-replicated messaging with tiered storage. Cloud services when you want zero operations on one cloud.

in real systems

When not

Kafka has no per-message routing or priorities and (before share groups) no per-message acks; RabbitMQ has limited replay and lower throughput for huge fan-out; Pulsar has more moving parts (BookKeeper, ZooKeeper/metadata store).

A quick decision guide

LEVELS> Comparing at three levels L1> Post office vs newspaper archive
  • A post office delivers each letter once and keeps nothing (queue). A newspaper archive keeps every edition, and any reader can start from any date (log).
L2> The main differences
  • Storage: RabbitMQ deletes acked messages (except RabbitMQ Streams); Kafka retains by time/size or compaction.
  • Consumers: RabbitMQ competing consumers on a queue, broker tracks delivery; Kafka groups track offsets, any number of groups read independently; Kafka share groups (4.x) add queue-style per-record acks.
  • Routing: RabbitMQ exchanges (direct, topic, fanout, headers), priorities, per-message TTL; Kafka routes by topic and key only.
  • Scale: Kafka partitions scale to very high throughput and long retention; RabbitMQ quorum queues favour moderate rates and low latency per message.
L3> Others
  • Apache Pulsar: brokers are stateless, storage in Apache BookKeeper; topics with segments, tiered storage, multi-tenancy (tenants/namespaces), exclusive/shared/failover/key_shared subscriptions, built-in geo-replication.
  • Cloud: AWS SQS (queue) and Kinesis (stream), Google Pub/Sub, Azure Service Bus / Event Hubs; NATS JetStream, Redis Streams for lighter needs; Redpanda as a Kafka-API engine.
  • Many systems use two: RabbitMQ (or share groups) for task dispatch, Kafka as the event backbone. With Kafka 4.x share groups, some teams no longer need a separate queue.
/LEVELS

Myth vs reality: things many websites still say

  • Myth: Kafka is a faster RabbitMQ.
    Reality (Kafka 4.4): Different models: log vs queue.
    Proof: Lab.
  • Myth: RabbitMQ cannot scale.
    Reality (Kafka 4.4): It scales well for its use cases; replay is the gap.
    Proof: Table.
  • Myth: Kafka cannot do queues.
    Reality (Kafka 4.4): Share groups (4.x) add queue semantics.
    Proof: Lesson 6.1.
EXAM> “Kafka vs RabbitMQ?” Log (retained, replay, many groups, partitions, high throughput) vs broker (routing, ack-delete, priorities, RPC); Pulsar: stateless brokers + BookKeeper, multi-tenant; choose by replay/fan-out vs routing/tasks; often combined.

4 Step by step

Same orders, two systems

All outputs are real: RabbitMQ 4.4.0 (quorum queue, pika) and Kafka 4.4.0 (confluent-kafka), five orders each.

  1. Step 1 · RabbitMQ

    ch.queue_declare(queue='orders-112', durable=True, arguments={'x-queue-type': 'quorum'})
ch.confirm_delivery()
for o in orders: ch.basic_publish(exchange='', routing_key='orders-112', body=o)
m, props, body = ch.basic_get('orders-112'); ch.basic_ack(m.delivery_tag)
    == 1) RabbitMQ 4: a quorum queue, two consumers on it, then a late consumer published 5; queue depth: 5 consumer A got ['order-1', 'order-3', 'order-5'] consumer B got ['order-2', 'order-4'] (competing consumers) queue depth after acks: 0 late consumer C (billing) gets: nothing, messages are gone after ack RabbitMQ version: 4.4.0
    Two consumers shared the five orders (3 + 2) and acked them; the queue emptied. The late billing consumer found nothing. (RabbitMQ 4 also refused a classic transient queue: deprecated, so we used a quorum queue.)

  2. Step 2 · Kafka

    for o in orders: p.produce('orders-112', o.encode())
read('kitchen-112'); read('billing-112'); read('audit-112')   # three groups
    == 2) Kafka 4.4: same 5 orders in a topic, two groups, then replay group kitchen got ['order-1', 'order-2', 'order-3', 'order-4', 'order-5'] group billing got ['order-1', 'order-2', 'order-3', 'order-4', 'order-5'] (each group gets everything) records still in the topic after both read: 5 replay: a new group (or an offset reset) reads them again: ['order-1', 'order-2', 'order-3'] ...
    Each group got all five; the topic still holds them; a new group replayed from the start.
